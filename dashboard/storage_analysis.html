Battery and long-duration storage dispatch patterns across all 7 ISOs

Storage in the Optimal Mix: All 7 ISOs

Across 1,102,248 scenario evaluations spanning 7 ISOs, 21 thresholds, and up to 17,496 cost sensitivity combinations per region, the optimizer reveals a clear hierarchy: 4-hour Li-ion batteries are the default storage workhorse, appearing in cost-optimal mixes across nearly every region from moderate thresholds onward. Long-duration storage (100hr iron-air) enters only at the last mile (≥99%) in wind-heavy ISOs. 8-hour batteries play a marginal niche role. Green hydrogen is priced out everywhere. The storage story is threshold-driven and deeply regional — shaped by VRE resource quality, existing baseload, and what alternatives each grid has available.

Modeling note: This analysis models 4hr and 8hr Li-ion, 100hr iron-air LDES, and 1000hr green H2. Intermediate durations (12hr, 24hr Li-ion) are not explicitly modeled; results should be read as directionally capturing the role of short-duration (≤8hr) vs. long-duration (≥100hr) storage rather than as precise duration optimization.

The Storage Hierarchy: Bat4 First, Then LDES, Then Nothing Else

The optimizer reveals a clear pecking order. 4-hour Li-ion batteries handle daily cycling at low cost. 100-hour iron-air LDES bridges multi-day wind lulls at the last mile. 8-hour batteries appear at the margin. Hydrogen never competes.

Battery 4hr: the default storage choice

4-hour Li-ion is the most ubiquitous storage technology across all ISOs. In solar-heavy regions (CAISO, PJM, NYISO), it appears from the lowest thresholds — handling the evening ramp as solar output drops. At ≥99% CFE, Bat4 appears in 53–100% of cost scenarios across all ISOs. PJM hits the ceiling at 10% dispatch (≥99.9%), CAISO runs 2–7% through the mid-range, and even late adopters like ERCOT and MISO pick up 2–3% at the last mile. The daily charge/discharge cycle is cost-effective because 4hr Li-ion has the highest revenue stacking potential (capacity markets + arbitrage + regulation).

LDES (100hr iron-air): last-mile insurance in wind-heavy grids

LDES never appears in cost-optimal mixes below 99% in any ISO. It enters at 99.9% in MISO, SPP, and ERCOT (≥99.99%), always paired with wind-heavy mixes that need multi-day bridging. At the last mile, LDES is present in 20–36% of scenarios in wind-heavy ISOs (MISO, SPP, NEISO, ERCOT) with dispatch ranging from 2–50% of demand. It never appears in PJM's optimal mixes and is negligible in NYISO. The 50% round-trip efficiency is a handicap, but for multi-day gaps that batteries physically can't cover, it's the cheapest option.

Battery 8hr: marginal niche player

8-hour Li-ion makes the cost-optimal cut in only 3 scenarios across all ISOs: CAISO at 99.5% (0.5% dispatch), MISO at ≥99.99% (0.5%), and SPP at ≥99.99% (2%). The jump from 4hr to 100hr largely skips the 8hr duration — the optimizer prefers more 4hr batteries or goes straight to LDES for longer gaps. In the broader scenario space, Bat8 appears in 7–40% of last-mile scenarios in 5 ISOs (never in PJM or NYISO).

Green H2: priced out everywhere

Green hydrogen (1000hr, 35% round-trip efficiency) is zero across all 7 ISOs, all 21 thresholds, all cost scenarios. The 35% RTE means ~3× the input energy of LDES for the same output, and the $/MWh-equivalent cost is 4–5× higher. Even at ≥99.99% where the optimizer is desperate for gap-filling capacity, it prefers LDES + overbuilt renewables over hydrogen every time.

Three Archetypes: How Regional Resources Shape Storage Need

ISOs cluster into distinct patterns based on VRE resource quality, existing baseload, and what alternatives each region has. The dividing line isn't storage cost — it's whether daily cycling (Bat4) or multi-day bridging (LDES) is the binding constraint.

Battery-First (Solar-Heavy)

CAISO · PJM · NYISO

Bat4 enters early (from the lowest thresholds) to handle daily solar cycling. CAISO runs 2–7% Bat4 through the mid-range; PJM hits 10% Bat4 at ≥99.9%. NYISO maintains 5% Bat4 across all thresholds. LDES is negligible or absent — these ISOs solve multi-day gaps with clean firm buildout and offshore wind rather than long-duration storage. PJM at ≥99% is 100% Bat4 with zero LDES.

Wind-Heavy, Last-Mile LDES

ERCOT · MISO · SPP · NEISO

Massive wind overbuild covers most hours without storage. Bat4 enters late (97.5–99%) as a minor complement. LDES appears at 99.9% in MISO, SPP, and ERCOT (≥99.99%) to bridge multi-day wind lulls. NEISO's offshore wind pushes LDES even further into the margin. These ISOs run 20–36% of last-mile scenarios with LDES at 2–50% dispatch — it's last-mile insurance, not a core resource.

Minimal Storage / Baseload-Rich

NYISO · MISO (overlap)

Both ISOs achieve near-100% CFE with minimal storage. NYISO uses 5% Bat4 throughout but never deploys LDES — nuclear + hydro + offshore wind covers all gaps. MISO reaches 99.5% with zero storage; only at 99.9%+ does LDES (5%) and Bat4 (3%) enter. Strong baseload + quality VRE can substitute for storage almost entirely.

Battery 4hr vs. LDES: Different Problems, Different Thresholds

Bat4 solves daily cycling (solar evening ramp, overnight gaps). LDES solves multi-day weather events. The optimizer deploys them at fundamentally different points in the cost curve.

Bat4 is everywhere, LDES is last-mile only

Bat4 appears in cost-optimal mixes from the earliest thresholds in solar-heavy ISOs (CAISO from 10%, PJM from 10%, NYISO from 10%). It handles the predictable daily solar cycle efficiently with 85% round-trip efficiency and high revenue stacking (capacity + arbitrage + regulation). LDES, by contrast, only appears at 99.9%+ in ERCOT, MISO, and SPP where multi-day wind lulls create gaps that no amount of Bat4 can fill. The optimizer reaches for the cheap daily-cycle solution first and only deploys expensive multi-day storage when it's physically necessary.

PJM: 100% Bat4, zero LDES through the entire threshold range

PJM is the clearest battery-first story: Bat4 appears in 100% of last-mile scenarios, reaching 10% dispatch at ≥99.9%. LDES never appears in any PJM optimal mix — the combination of diversified VRE (solar + onshore + offshore wind) plus clean firm buildout eliminates multi-day gaps before LDES becomes necessary. NYISO tells a similar story with 5% Bat4 at all thresholds and no LDES.

Key Takeaways

Bat4 is the workhorse, not LDES

4-hour Li-ion appears in 53–100% of last-mile scenarios across all ISOs. It handles daily solar cycling efficiently with 85% RTE and strong revenue stacking. PJM runs 100% Bat4 penetration at ≥99% with zero LDES. The optimizer reaches for cheap daily-cycle storage first and only deploys LDES when physically necessary.

LDES is last-mile insurance, not a core resource

100-hour iron-air LDES never appears below 99% in cost-optimal mixes. It enters at 99.9%+ in wind-heavy ISOs (MISO, SPP, ERCOT) to bridge multi-day wind lulls. Present in 20–36% of last-mile scenarios in these regions, but negligible or absent in PJM and NYISO. LDES is a necessary complement, not a substitute for generation.

The 8hr–100hr gap may matter

This model evaluates 4hr, 8hr, 100hr, and 1000hr durations — but not 12hr or 24hr Li-ion. The sharp jump from 8hr to 100hr means intermediate-duration storage could potentially fill gaps that the optimizer currently solves with LDES or generation overbuild. Results should be read directionally: short-duration storage is the primary need, with long-duration filling a smaller last-mile niche.

Threshold drives storage, not geography alone

Below 85–90%, most ISOs achieve targets with generation alone. Storage is fundamentally a high-threshold phenomenon. The regional story is which storage enters: solar-heavy grids use Bat4 for evening ramps; wind-heavy grids need LDES for multi-day lulls. But both types are irrelevant below ~85% CFE in most regions.
