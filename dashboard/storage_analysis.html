Storage in the Optimal Mix: All 7 ISOs

A common assumption in clean energy planning is that massive battery and long-duration storage deployment is essential for decarbonization. Across 1,102,248 scenario evaluations spanning 7 ISOs, 21 thresholds, and up to 17,496 cost sensitivity combinations per region, our optimization reveals a nuanced picture: 100-hour iron-air LDES is the dominant storage technology, appearing as early as 50% CFE in PJM and 65% in CAISO. Short-duration batteries appear marginally (0.1% dispatch) in some regions, while 8-hour batteries and hydrogen are never cost-optimal. The storage story is fundamentally regional — driven by offshore wind availability, nuclear baseload, and VRE resource quality.

Why Storage Plays a Supporting Role, Not a Leading One

The binding constraint for hourly CFE matching isn't daily solar cycling — it's multi-day weather events. This makes LDES (100hr iron-air) the only storage technology that consistently enters optimal mixes. Short-duration batteries appear marginally in some regions.

Batteries: viable but rarely cost-optimal

4-hour Li-ion batteries appear at just 0.1% dispatch in CAISO (from 70% CFE) and PJM (from 95% CFE) under medium cost assumptions. In the broader efficient frontier, batteries appear in 30–55% of feasible mixes at high thresholds — they're physically viable but the optimizer almost always finds wind overbuild + LDES or clean firm buildout to be cheaper. Under low-VRE / high-firm cost scenarios, batteries become more relevant: appearing in 20–28% of NEISO and CAISO scenario evaluations. The daily charge/discharge cycle helps but doesn't solve the multi-day gap problem.

Hydrogen: too expensive, too lossy

Green hydrogen (1000-hour duration, 35% round-trip efficiency) never appears in any medium-cost optimal mix. It enters the efficient frontier at t≥95% thresholds (18–50% of EF mixes in PJM) but loses on cost to clean firm + LDES combinations. The triple conversion loss (electrolysis × storage × turbine = 35% round-trip) means nearly two-thirds of input energy is lost, making it uncompetitive even at the lowest projected costs.

Three Archetypes: How Regional Resources Shape Storage Need

ISOs cluster into three distinct patterns based on their VRE resource quality, offshore wind potential, and nuclear/hydro baseload. The dominant factor isn't storage technology cost — it's what alternatives each region has available.

Early LDES Adopters

PJM · CAISO

LDES enters early: 50% CFE in PJM (5% dispatch via wind overbuild), 65% in CAISO (solar-to-wind transition). PJM's cheapest path at low thresholds is wind-heavy (50 GW) + LDES (5%) rather than clean firm. CAISO uses LDES to bridge evening ramps as solar share declines. Both ISOs also see marginal battery entry (0.1% dispatch) at higher thresholds.

Late LDES / VRE-Heavy

ERCOT · NEISO · SPP

LDES enters at 90–95% CFE with moderate penetration. ERCOT and SPP rely on massive wind overbuild (80–105 GW) with LDES as a small multi-day buffer (1%). NEISO's offshore wind resource (up to 33% of demand) reduces storage need significantly — offshore wind's higher capacity factor and nighttime generation profile fills gaps that would otherwise require storage.

No Storage Needed

NYISO · MISO

LDES never appears in medium-cost optimal mixes. NYISO's combination of nuclear baseload (79 GW clean firm) + hydro (16 GW) + offshore wind (up to 20 GW) eliminates the need for storage entirely. MISO's abundant wind (80 GW) + aggressive clean firm ramp (14→120 GW) achieves 99.99% without any storage. These ISOs prove that strong baseload + quality VRE can substitute for storage completely.

The Storage–Firm Tradeoff: Wind + LDES vs. Clean Firm Buildout

When LDES appears, it's always in wind-heavy mixes with low clean firm. The optimizer is revealing a fundamental substitution: LDES is an alternative to clean firm, not a complement.

PJM: wind + LDES is cheaper than clean firm at low thresholds

PJM's cheapest path to 50–90% CFE is a wind-heavy mix (50 GW onshore) with 5% LDES at just $36.61/MWh. The optimizer prefers this over building additional clean firm because PJM's wind resource, combined with multi-day LDES bridging, covers enough hours cheaply. Above 92.5%, the mix pivots to clean firm + solar + small offshore wind (4 GW) — but LDES remains at 0.2% dispatch for gap-filling even at 99.99%.

Offshore wind displaces storage in NEISO and NYISO

NEISO's offshore wind scales from 0% to 25–30% of demand above 90% CFE, providing nighttime and winter generation that would otherwise require storage. NYISO goes further: above 92.5% CFE, 100% of wind capacity is offshore, and the ISO achieves 99.99% matching with zero storage of any kind. Offshore wind's higher capacity factor (~45% vs ~30% onshore) and complementary diurnal profile effectively substitute for batteries.

Key Takeaways

LDES enters earlier than expected

PJM's cheapest path to 50% CFE already includes 5% LDES dispatch paired with 50 GW wind. CAISO follows at 65%. The optimizer reveals LDES isn't just a last-mile technology — it's a cost-effective complement to wind overbuild even at moderate CFE targets in some regions.

Batteries: marginal, not transformative

4-hour batteries appear at 0.1% dispatch in CAISO (from 70% CFE) and PJM (from 95% CFE) — but 8-hour batteries and hydrogen are never cost-optimal. Batteries exist in 30–55% of feasible mixes but can't compete with wind + LDES or clean firm buildout on total system cost.

Offshore wind substitutes for storage

NEISO and NYISO demonstrate that offshore wind's higher capacity factor and nighttime generation can replace the need for storage entirely. NYISO achieves 99.99% CFE with zero storage — offshore wind (up to 20 GW) fills gaps that would otherwise require batteries or LDES.

Regional resources drive the storage decision

PJM uses LDES from 50% CFE, CAISO from 65%, NEISO from 90%. NYISO and MISO never need it. The divergence isn't about storage economics — it's about what alternatives each region has: nuclear baseload, offshore wind, or cheap onshore wind.
