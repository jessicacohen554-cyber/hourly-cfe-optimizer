The Replacement Cost Paradox

Wholesale price degradation threatens existing nuclear viability across all 7 ISOs — but replacing retired nuclear with new-build clean firm costs 3–5× the wholesale price that caused the retirement ($105–163/MWh new nuclear vs. $20–34/MWh wholesale at 90% clean). Nuclear doesn't just need enough revenue to survive — it needs profit to reinvest in uprates, relicensing, and capacity expansion at already-permitted sites.

PJM Wholesale Market Analysis

Clean energy halves wholesale prices in PJM — but the path is narrower than you think

Across 151,944 simulated PJM scenarios, wholesale prices decline from $35/MWh at 50% clean (SBTi 2030) to $17/MWh at 95% (SBTi ~2045). But the spread between best- and worst-case outcomes peaks in the middle of the transition — precisely where most utilities are planning today.

Fuel prices explain nearly everything until ≥99.99% clean, where the rules change entirely and renewable costs, transmission, and policy levers take over.

Price Drivers

Fuel prices explain 88–100% of wholesale cost variation — until they don't

From 50% to 95% clean energy, the single biggest determinant of wholesale prices is the fossil fuel price assumption. At 50% clean, fuel level explains 100% of the variance in average LMP. At 80%, it still accounts for 88%.

The gap between Low and High fuel scenarios in PJM is massive: at 80% clean, Low fuel yields $17/MWh while High fuel yields $37/MWh — a 2.2× difference driven entirely by what marginal gas plants cost to run.

Low fuel: $17/MWh at 80% · High fuel: $37/MWh at 80%

At ≥99.99% clean, the game changes completely

When clean energy reaches ≥99.99%, fuel prices become irrelevant — there are no fossil plants left to set the price. Variance fragments across multiple levers: renewable costs (14%), fuel level (14%), transmission (11%), 45Q policy (7%), and CO₂ price (5%).

This is the structural break in the wholesale market. Everything that mattered before ≥99.99% stops mattering. Everything that didn't — transmission buildout, tax policy, clean generation costs — becomes decisive.

At ≥99.99%: No single factor explains >15% of price variance

Market Structure

PJM zero-price hours explode from 265 to 7,980 — and the market flips inside out

At 50% clean, PJM sees about 265 zero-or-negative-price hours per year — roughly 3% of the time, typically overnight with must-run nuclear surplus. By 95%, that figure hits 4,268 hours — nearly half the year. At ≥99.99%, it's 7,980 hours: 91% of all hours produce free or negatively-priced electricity.

This isn't a gradual shift. The curve accelerates sharply past 75%, as each incremental percentage of clean energy adds more zero-marginal-cost generation to hours where fossil was already barely dispatching.

265 → 7,980 zero-price hours: a 30× increase

Scarcity events decline but never disappear

Hours above $200/MWh drop from a median of 68 at 50% clean to 10 at ≥99.99% — but they persist even in a fully clean grid. Clean firm generation and storage smooth the worst supply crunches, but extreme weather and demand spikes still produce scarcity pricing in the tail.

The P90 of scarcity hours (worst 10% of scenarios) remains above 40 even at ≥99.99% clean — a reminder that resource adequacy risk doesn't vanish, it transforms.

Price Structure

The peak premium persists — and that's the arbitrage signal

Off-peak prices crash faster than peak: at 95% clean, off-peak hits $14/MWh while peak holds at $22/MWh. The median spread between peak and off-peak actually widens from 80% to 97.5% before collapsing only at ≥99.99%.

For storage operators and demand-response providers, this is the signal: the economic case for time-shifting energy strengthens through most of the clean energy transition, even as absolute prices fall.

Peak–offpeak spread: $13–$16/MWh from 80% to 97.5%

Off-peak prices go negative first — creating the duck curve in price space

At ≥99.99% clean, off-peak median is −$12/MWh while peak is still −$2/MWh. Even in a fully decarbonized grid, demand-supply timing mismatch creates a $10 spread. This is the price manifestation of the duck curve — too much clean energy when demand is low, and storage arbitrage is the only remedy.

At ≥99.99%: peak −$2, off-peak −$12 — $10 spread remains

Total Wholesale Market Revenue: Energy + Capacity

Capacity markets add $5–12/MWh in PJM, NYISO, CAISO — $0 in energy-only markets (ERCOT, SPP)

Revenue Streams

Capacity payments cushion the price decline — but only where markets exist

ISOs with capacity markets (PJM, NYISO, CAISO, NEISO, MISO) provide generators with additional revenue beyond the energy spot market. For nuclear plants, capacity revenue adds $12/MWh in PJM at 50% clean — enough to delay the retirement threshold by ~10 percentage points.

In energy-only markets (ERCOT, SPP), generators must survive on energy revenue alone. This amplifies retirement risk as clean energy depresses wholesale prices.

Nuclear Viability

Nuclear retirement risk shifts with total market revenue

When capacity payments are included, nuclear viability extends further into the clean energy transition. In PJM, the total market revenue (energy + capacity) crosses below nuclear's all-in cost of $38–44/MWh at approximately 78% clean — compared to ~68% under energy-only pricing.

The 45U PTC ($15/MWh) combined with capacity payments creates a stronger floor, potentially extending nuclear viability to ~85% clean.

Convergence Zone

Models converge below ~65% clean

At lower clean energy penetrations (50–65%), both models produce similar wholesale prices — the grid is still fossil-dominated and the merit order functions the same way regardless of whether clean energy was mandated or market-driven.

Above 75% clean, the models diverge: Step 5B's policy pathway forces deployment past the market equilibrium, pushing wholesale prices lower than what Step 10's market-only pathway would produce at the same clean%. The capacity market premium (purple dashed line) partially offsets this decline.

The uncertainty paradox: widest where it matters most

The P10–P90 spread in wholesale prices peaks at 75–80% clean energy — exactly the threshold range where most corporate buyers and utilities are currently planning. At 80%, the gap between the best- and worst-case outcome is $31/MWh. That's the difference between wholesale power cheaper than natural gas alone and wholesale power more expensive than today's average.

The implication: forecasting wholesale prices at 75–85% clean requires getting fuel cost assumptions right. Every other input is noise until you approach ≥99.99%.

$31/MWh spread at 80% · $0.4/MWh spread at ≥99.99%

Three eras of the wholesale market

Era 1: 50–75% (2030–~2034) — Fossil sets the price. Fuel costs dominate. Wholesale markets function as they do today, just at modestly lower levels. Zero-price hours are a nuisance, not a structural feature.

Era 2: 75–95% (~2034–2045) — The transition zone. Price uncertainty is at its peak. Zero-price hours become a defining feature (half the year by 95%). Storage and flexibility gain premium value.

Era 3: 95–≥99.99% (2045–2050) — The structural break. Fossil no longer sets marginal price. All hours converge toward zero or negative. Revenue for all generators collapses. The market requires fundamental redesign.

2030–2034: business as usual · 2034–2045: transition · 2045+: new paradigm
