Corporate Procurement Strategies

How different approaches to clean energy procurement drive real-world grid decarbonization — and where each strategy actually deploys capital.

The Framework: Six Strategies, Three Philosophies

Not all clean energy procurement is created equal. The same corporate dollar can displace dramatically different amounts of carbon depending on how and where it’s deployed. We model six procurement strategies across three families, each with distinct rules about what counts as “clean” and where investment goes.

All simulations target a 95% clean grid in each ISO’s SBTi milestone year, using 2045 demand projections with medium growth rates. Corporate & Industrial (C&I) participation ranges from today’s ~13% to a theoretical 80% of load.

Group 1 — Consequential

Cross-Regional Carbon Displacement

Deploy capital wherever it displaces the most carbon per dollar — across all ISOs, not just the buyer’s own grid. A merit-order queue ranks every possible clean energy investment by marginal abatement cost.

1A — Grid-Average Baseline: Credits emission reductions against the full grid emission rate (includes clean energy in the denominator). Lower bar for claiming displacement.

1B — Fossil-Average Baseline: Credits reductions against only the fossil fleet’s emission rate. Uses marginal displacement rates where available; falls back to fossil-average as proxy. Higher bar, more accurate accounting of actual fossil displacement.
Group 2 — Hourly Matching

Same-ISO, Hour-by-Hour

Match clean generation to consumption every hour within the buyer’s own ISO. The most rigorous temporal standard — no annual averaging, no cross-regional credit.

2A: 100% new-build required
2B: Grid baseline credit for existing clean
2C: Pro-rata allocation + premium for existing fleet
Group 3 — Annual Matching

Volume-Based, Annual Accounting

Match total annual clean generation to annual consumption. Simpler accounting, but allows temporal mismatches (e.g., solar surplus in summer offsets winter gas).

3A — Cross-Regional New Build: Annual matching with additionality requirement, deployed across ISOs.

3B — Cross-Regional, No Additionality: Unbundled RECs — cheapest option, but no guarantee of new generation. Existing clean counts.

Following the Money: How Consequential Procurement Deploys Capital

Consequential procurement is the most economically efficient strategy for displacing carbon: it builds clean energy wherever the marginal abatement cost is lowest, regardless of where the buyer sits. This creates a cross-regional deployment queue — a merit order of decarbonization opportunities ranked by $/tCO₂ displaced.

1A vs 1B: Same Resources, Different Accounting

Strategies 1A and 1B deploy identical resources in the same sequence — the merit-order queue is the same. The only difference is how each strategy accounts for the carbon displaced.

1A (Grid-Average) divides total grid emissions by total grid generation, including existing clean energy. This dilutes the emission rate — a grid that’s already 40% clean has a lower “average” rate, making each new clean MWh appear to displace less carbon than it actually does.

1B (Fossil-Average) calculates the emission rate using only the fossil fleet. Where marginal displacement data is available (from our 8,760-hour dispatch model), it uses the actual marginal rate. Where it isn’t, it uses the fossil-fleet average as a proxy. This more accurately reflects the emissions that new clean energy actually displaces.

Why 1B is the superior method

At 80% C&I participation, Strategy 1B attributes ~40% more CO₂ displacement than 1A — for the exact same investment. This isn’t a rounding error; it’s the difference between measuring what you actually displace (fossil generation) versus what you statistically offset (grid average including existing clean). 1B gives a more honest — and more motivating — picture of impact.

The Fuel Displacement Sequence: When Does Coal Run Out?

Consequential deployment follows the fossil fuel merit order: coal is displaced first (highest emissions per MWh), then oil, then gas. This matters because coal displacement yields ~2.5× the carbon reduction per MWh compared to gas.

The “coal wall” is the threshold where all coal generation has been retired from the dispatch stack. In most ISOs, this happens at roughly 70% CFE. Beyond the coal wall, every additional clean MWh only displaces gas — cutting the marginal carbon benefit nearly in half and sharply increasing the marginal abatement cost.

CAISO: No coal wall at all

CAISO is the only ISO with zero coal in its dispatch stack — its fossil fleet is entirely gas (plus a small amount of oil). This means every clean MWh in CAISO displaces gas at ~0.39 tCO₂/MWh, regardless of CFE target. It also means CAISO has the least efficient carbon displacement per dollar, which is why it ranks last in the consequential merit order.

Where Capital Flows: Resource Deployment by Participation Level

The consequential queue reveals a clear deployment hierarchy. At low participation, all investment goes to nuclear uprate — the cheapest form of clean firm generation, unlocking existing capacity that’s already licensed and connected. Wind enters next, concentrated in ERCOT and SPP where capacity factors are highest. Solar follows at higher participation levels. Clean firm new-build (beyond uprate) doesn’t appear until ~80% participation.

First clean firm new-build: not until ~80% participation

In the consequential merit order, CCS-equipped gas (the first new firm resource beyond uprate) doesn’t appear until 80% C&I participation — and even then only 6 TWh. The economics strongly favor variable renewables (wind, then solar) for the first ~60% of deployment. This means early corporate procurement through consequential methods is overwhelmingly a VRE story, concentrated in wind-rich ERCOT and SPP.

Investment deserts: NEISO, NYISO, and CAISO lag behind

The three most expensive ISOs — NEISO, NYISO, and CAISO — receive almost no consequential investment at participation levels below 30%. Their higher electricity costs and lower renewable capacity factors make them poor targets for cost-optimized carbon displacement. CAISO’s lack of coal makes gas-only displacement especially expensive per ton. In a consequential framework, these regions are last in line — the final dollars chasing diminishing returns.
