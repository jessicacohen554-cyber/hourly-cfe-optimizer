Gas Capacity Transformation

Can corporate procurement actually shrink the existing gas fleet — not just slow its growth?

The U.S. grid currently operates 299 GW of gas-fired capacity across seven ISOs. Most procurement strategies slow the addition of new gas plants. But only hourly matching strategies — specifically 2C with SSS allocation — push total gas capacity below that 299 GW baseline, meaning they drive retirement of plants that already exist. At 30% corporate participation, 2C crosses below 299 GW at a 95% CFE target. 1B consequential matching does not cross that line until participation reaches 50%. Annual strategies (Track 3) never cross it at all — they reduce the need for new gas but leave the existing fleet intact.

The chart below maps each strategy's gas capacity footprint as participation scales from 0% to 100%. The ribbon bands capture the range across all CO₂ targets; the solid line marks the 95% target that anchors most of this analysis.

Total Gas Capacity vs. Corporate Participation

Ribbon bands show the range across all CO₂ targets (50–99.99%). Solid line = 95% target; dotted lines = 90% and 99%. When lines cross below 299 GW, strategies begin displacing existing gas.

2C hourly matching with SSS allocation is the only strategy that consistently drives gas capacity below the existing 299 GW baseline at moderate participation levels. At 30% participation and a 95% CFE target, 2C reduces total gas to approximately 265 GW — a net retirement of 34 GW. Consequential strategies (1A, 1B) approach the baseline but require higher participation to cross it. Annual matching strategies operate well above the line at all participation levels, adding clean energy without displacing existing fossil infrastructure.

Participation-Level Deep Dive

What happens to grid outcomes as corporate adoption scales from 5% to 100%?

Gas displacement is only one dimension of the strategy comparison. The four charts below track four metrics simultaneously as participation scales: how well the grid matches hourly clean energy demand, how much fossil CO₂ gets displaced, how efficiently each dollar of procurement translates to emissions reductions, and how much clean energy gets curtailed (generated but unused). These four metrics tell different parts of the same story. Hourly match score and CO₂ displacement rise steadily with participation, but they diverge across strategies — 2C outperforms 1B by 8–15 percentage points on hourly matching at most participation levels. CO₂ efficiency, measured as Mt displaced per billion dollars spent, peaks at moderate participation (20–35%) and then declines as strategies hit diminishing returns. Curtailment rises sharply above 60% participation as renewable overbuilding outpaces storage and demand flexibility.

The tradeoff between efficiency and curtailment defines the practical sweet spot. Below 20% participation, all strategies are efficient but none moves the grid. Above 60%, deep decarbonization comes at steep cost. The 25–40% range is where strategy choice matters most.

Full Decarbonization Matrix

Which combinations of ambition and adoption actually get to a clean grid?

The previous sections examined strategies at fixed targets or fixed participation levels. The matrix below shows the full two-dimensional space: every combination of CO₂ target (rows) and corporate participation level (columns), colored by the resulting grid clean percentage. This format answers a specific question — what is the minimum viable coalition? How few buyers, at what level of ambition, produce a grid that is more than 95% clean?

For 2C hourly matching, the answer is 25% participation at a 90% CFE target. That combination pushes grid-wide clean energy above 95%. 1B consequential matching requires 40% participation at the same target to reach the same grid outcome. Annual strategies (3A–3D) do not reach 95% grid clean at any participation level below 70%, regardless of target. The matrix makes the coalition math concrete: a relatively small group of ambitious hourly buyers moves the grid further than a large group of annual buyers.

The green cells cluster in the upper-right for all strategies, but the boundary shifts left for hourly matching. 2C reaches >95% grid clean starting at 25% participation × 90% target — a coalition of roughly 250 TWh of corporate demand. 1B requires 40% participation for the same outcome. Track 3 strategies show green cells only at extreme participation levels (70%+), confirming that annual matching spreads procurement too thin to reshape the grid's generation mix. The diagonal gradient from red to green is steeper for hourly strategies, meaning each additional percentage point of participation produces a larger incremental grid benefit.

Resource Buildout Trajectories & Stranded Assets

Not all paths to decarbonization are reversible. Some create stranded gas.

The choice of procurement strategy does not just determine how much clean energy gets built — it determines what kind of infrastructure gets built along the way. Some strategies require new gas capacity at intermediate CFE targets (60–80%) that becomes stranded as targets rise above 90%. This path dependency matters because stranded gas plants represent wasted capital, ongoing maintenance costs, and political resistance to further decarbonization.

1B consequential matching builds 18 GW of new gas capacity when targeting 75% CFE at 30% participation. That gas becomes surplus above 90%, where clean firm and storage displace it. 2C hourly matching avoids this trap — it builds clean firm capacity earlier and requires only 4 GW of new gas at the same intermediate target. Annual strategies (Track 3) build the most intermediate gas because they lack the hourly price signal that incentivizes firm clean resources. Wright’s Law Critical Mass

Early investment in clean firm technologies pays off for everyone — if enough buyers commit at once.

Every doubling of cumulative clean firm deployment reduces costs by 15–20%, following Wright’s Law learning curves observed across solar, wind, and battery technologies. The question is which procurement strategy reaches those doublings fastest. Strategies that concentrate deployment in new clean firm capacity — rather than spreading procurement across existing credits — drive costs down the learning curve sooner. The first chart tracks blended $/MWh as participation scales. The second identifies the participation level where learning curve savings outpace the initial procurement premium, making clean firm cheaper than fossil alternatives on an unsubsidized basis.

This is the long-game payoff. Early adopters pay a premium, but that premium buys cost reductions that benefit every subsequent buyer. The strategies that reach critical mass fastest create a self-reinforcing cycle where cost declines attract more adoption, which drives further cost declines.

At 25% participation, 2C hourly matching reaches the cost crossover point where blended clean firm costs drop below new-build gas LCOE. 1B consequential matching reaches the same crossover at 35% participation — a 10-percentage-point delay that translates to roughly 100 TWh of additional fossil generation before the economics flip. Annual strategies (3C, 3D) never reach cost-negative territory below 50% participation because they rely on existing credits rather than driving new deployment. The gap between 2C and 3D at 30% participation is $8/MWh — 2C has already crossed the learning curve inflection while 3D remains $8 above the fossil alternative. Concentrated deployment through hourly matching accelerates the cost decline that makes the entire energy transition cheaper.

What the Data Shows

The gap between the most and least effective procurement strategies is not marginal. At 30% participation and a 95% CFE target, 2C hourly matching displaces 127 Mt of CO₂ annually and drives total gas capacity 34 GW below the existing fleet baseline. 3D annual matching, at the same participation level, displaces 61 Mt and adds 12 GW of new gas. The difference is 66 Mt of CO₂ and 46 GW of gas infrastructure — roughly equivalent to retiring every gas plant in NYISO and NEISO combined.

Hourly matching pays a higher upfront cost per MWh, but that cost premium buys three things annual matching does not: direct gas displacement, concentrated clean firm deployment that accelerates learning curves, and a buildout trajectory that avoids stranded gas assets. At 25% participation, learning curve effects have already pushed 2C's blended cost below new-build gas. Annual strategies do not reach that crossover until participation exceeds 50% — a level that requires nearly all large corporate buyers to commit simultaneously.

The decarbonization matrix confirms the coalition math. A group of hourly buyers representing 25% of load achieves the same grid clean percentage that would require 70% participation under annual matching. For corporate buyers designing procurement strategies, the implication is direct: fewer participants can achieve deeper decarbonization if they commit to hourly matching.

The question that follows is how to get there — how clean firm technology scales from first-of-a-kind costs to mass deployment, and what role corporate procurement plays in that transition. That analysis continues in the clean firm scaling case and the gas lock-in and buyer influence analysis.
