1. Two Procurement Paths

Every corporate buyer pursuing hourly clean energy faces a fundamental choice: procure from the clean generation that already exists on the grid, or finance entirely new-build projects. The optimizer evaluates both.

Track 1: With Existing Clean

Leverages existing nuclear, hydro, and merchant renewables first. New-build fills only the gap. Requires clean energy premiums (EAC) to keep existing plants economically viable.

2. What You Need Depends on the Path You Take

The two procurement strategies produce very different resource portfolios. Track 1 builds on top of existing clean assets — existing nuclear, hydro, and renewables form the base (solid bars), with new build filling the gap (lighter bars). Track 2 starts from scratch — everything is new build.

Reading This Chart

For each hourly matching target, two bars sit side by side. The left bar (Track 1) shows existing resources in solid color at the base, with lighter new-build additions stacked on top. The right bar (Track 2) is entirely new build — no existing assets. The dashed line marks the region's current clean energy share. Where Track 1's solid base is large, that region has a cost advantage from leveraging what already exists.

3. What Gets Built at Scale?

As aggregate corporate demand scales from 10% to 100% of C&I load, the optimal resource mix shifts. At low participation, existing nuclear dominates. As merchant supply exhausts (~90 TWh in PJM), new-build takes over — and the resource mix converges toward Track 2.

Reading This Chart

Each bar shows the cheapest resource mix to meet the selected CFE target. At low participation (left), buyers access cheap existing nuclear. As participation rises (right), existing supply runs out and new-build resources — primarily new nuclear — dominate the mix. The transition accelerates around the supply ceiling (~23% of C&I load).

4. The Cost Convergence

Track 1's cost advantage narrows as more buyers compete for the same pool of existing clean energy. Beyond the supply ceiling, every new MWh of corporate demand requires new-build — and the cost curves converge.

The First-Mover Advantage is Real

Buyers who secure existing clean generation early — through long-term EAC contracts or PPAs — lock in $20+/MWh savings. Late movers face new-build economics regardless of strategy. The window narrows as corporate hourly matching adoption accelerates.

6. When Does Stranding Begin?

The IRS Section 45U technology-neutral tax credit sets a strike price of ~$44/MWh for existing nuclear. As hourly clean targets rise, zero-marginal-cost renewables push wholesale LMP down through the merit order — the growing gap between LMP and $44 is the bare minimum clean premium the market must provide per 45U.

The Procurement Paradox

A strategy that relies exclusively on new-build clean energy accelerates the very feedback loop that undermines existing nuclear viability:

  1. New build depresses LMP — zero-marginal-cost renewables push fossil (and nuclear) out of the merit order
  2. Existing nuclear becomes uneconomic — operating costs exceed wholesale revenue
  3. Nuclear retires — without state subsidies or clean premiums, plants shut down
  4. Reliability gap emerges — PJM loses 32 GW of baseload; must be replaced with new firm generation
  5. Replacement cost is higher — new nuclear costs $105/MWh vs $33/MWh to keep existing plants running

45U Production Tax Credit: A Bridge, Not a Solution

The IRA's Section 45U provides existing nuclear up to $15/MWh in production tax credits (2024–2032). This helps close the gap today — but it phases down as carbon-free generation increases, and expires before most SBTi targets are reached.

Procuring from existing clean generation saves ~$20/MWh compared to an all new-build portfolio — but only while supply lasts. PJM has roughly 90 TWh of uncontracted merchant clean energy. As aggregate corporate demand approaches that ceiling, the cost advantage narrows and eventually disappears.

The economically rational strategy: secure existing clean generation through EAC premiums now while those premiums are low ($3–15/MWh), and complement with new-build where existing supply is insufficient. Attempting to procure only new-build accelerates wholesale price decline, threatens existing nuclear viability, and ultimately increases total system cost.

Clean premiums are not a subsidy — they're a market signal that existing clean generation has value beyond the wholesale energy market. Without that signal, the cheapest clean electrons in the system retire, and everyone pays more.
