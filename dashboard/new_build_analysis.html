2. The Scaling Curve

What happens when more buyers enter the market? As aggregate corporate demand scales, existing merchant supply is consumed. Beyond a critical threshold, every buyer faces new-build economics regardless of their stated procurement strategy.

PJM Available Merchant Supply: ~90 TWh

Of PJM's 280 TWh total clean generation, 95 TWh is locked in state subsidy programs (SSS-fixed), 70 TWh fulfills RPS mandates, and 25 TWh is already under corporate PPAs. That leaves roughly 90 TWh of uncontracted merchant supply available for voluntary hourly matching procurement.

3. The Economic Feedback Loop

Clean energy procurement doesn't happen in a vacuum. Large-scale hourly matching reshapes wholesale electricity markets — which feeds back to undermine the very assets that make "with existing" procurement cheap.

Wholesale prices fall as clean energy displaces fossil generation

PJM's wholesale electricity price (LMP) declines from $37/MWh at 50% clean to under $7/MWh at 99%. The merit-order effect is relentless: every MWh of zero-marginal-cost clean energy pushes higher-cost fossil plants out of the dispatch stack.

The widening gap: when wholesale prices fall below operating costs

Nuclear plants cost roughly $33/MWh to operate (fuel + O&M). When the wholesale price drops below that floor, existing nuclear can only survive with out-of-market revenue — clean energy premiums, state subsidies, or federal tax credits.

Illinois ZEC/CMC Expiry (2027): 94 TWh at Risk

Illinois' Zero Emission Credit (ZEC) and Clean Energy Jobs Act (CMC) programs — which subsidize six nuclear stations (Dresden, Braidwood, Byron, LaSalle, Clinton, Quad Cities) — expire in 2027. Without renewal or replacement revenue, these 94 TWh of SSS-fixed nuclear generation could shift to merchant status or face retirement pressure.

This is not hypothetical. Exelon already announced potential early retirement of Quad Cities and Byron before the original ZEC program was enacted in 2016.

45U Production Tax Credit: A Bridge, Not a Solution

The Inflation Reduction Act's Section 45U provides existing nuclear plants up to $15/MWh in production tax credits (2024–2032). This helps close the gap between operating costs and declining wholesale prices — but it phases down as carbon-free generation increases, and expires entirely by 2032.

At 90% clean energy penetration (LMP ~$24/MWh), nuclear needs ~$9/MWh beyond what markets provide. The 45U PTC covers this today. But as thresholds rise further and LMP drops below $12/MWh, even the full $15 PTC becomes insufficient — and it sunsets before most SBTi targets are reached.

The Procurement Paradox

A strategy that relies exclusively on new-build clean energy accelerates the very feedback loop that undermines existing nuclear viability:

  1. New build depresses LMP — zero-marginal-cost renewables push fossil (and nuclear) out of the merit order
  2. Existing nuclear becomes uneconomic — operating costs exceed wholesale revenue
  3. Nuclear retires — without state subsidies or clean premiums, plants shut down
  4. Reliability gap emerges — PJM loses 32 GW of baseload; must be replaced with new firm generation
  5. Replacement cost is higher — new nuclear costs $105/MWh vs $33/MWh to keep existing plants running

The cheapest path includes both: clean premiums to retain existing nuclear and new-build for incremental capacity. Ignoring existing assets doesn't make them disappear — it makes replacing them expensive.

Procuring from existing clean generation saves ~$21/MWh compared to an all new-build portfolio — but only while supply lasts. PJM has roughly 90 TWh of uncontracted merchant clean energy. As aggregate corporate demand approaches that ceiling, the cost advantage narrows and eventually disappears.

The economically rational strategy: secure existing clean generation through EAC premiums now while those premiums are low ($3–15/MWh), and complement with new-build where existing supply is insufficient. Attempting to procure only new-build accelerates wholesale price decline, threatens existing nuclear viability, and ultimately increases total system cost.

Clean premiums are not a subsidy — they're a market signal that existing clean generation has value beyond the wholesale energy market. Without that signal, the cheapest clean electrons in the system retire, and everyone pays more.
