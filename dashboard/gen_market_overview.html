How the U.S. Power Market Actually Works

Market design determines how—and whether—decarbonization investment happens.

The U.S. electricity sector is not a single market. It is a patchwork of fundamentally different ownership structures, regulatory regimes, and competitive dynamics. Roughly half of U.S. generation is produced by vertically integrated utilities operating under state regulation. The other half trades through competitive wholesale markets run by independent system operators (ISOs). These two models create radically different incentive structures for clean energy investment.

Vertically Integrated Utilities

Southern, Duke, Dominion, Entergy, BH Energy, Xcel, DTE, WEC, Evergy, PPL

Own generation, transmission, and distribution. Regulated by state public utility commissions (PUCs) with a guaranteed rate of return. Decarbonization is planned through Integrated Resource Plans (IRPs), with costs passed to ratepayers.

Advantages: Can plan across the full value chain. Long-term investment horizons with guaranteed cost recovery. Can make system-wide optimization decisions.

Challenges: Political pressure from ratepayers on cost increases. State regulatory environments vary wildly—from aggressive clean energy mandates to hostile opposition. No market price signal for clean energy. Slow decision cycles driven by IRP cadence (2–3 year cycles).

Competitive / Deregulated Markets

Constellation, Vistra, NRG, Talen, AES, PSEG, NextEra (competitive gen)

Generators sell into wholesale markets operated by ISOs (CAISO, ERCOT, PJM, NYISO, NEISO, MISO, SPP). Revenue depends on market clearing prices. No guaranteed cost recovery for capital investments.

Advantages: Market signals drive operational efficiency. Can respond quickly to economic opportunities. Innovation rewarded by margin capture.

Challenges: No centralized generation planning. Investment decisions driven by market economics, not emissions targets. Stranded asset risk from regulatory or market shifts. Wholesale price volatility creates investment uncertainty.

The IPP Challenge

Independent Power Producers: Vistra, NRG, Constellation, Talen, AES

IPPs operate as independent actors in competitive wholesale markets. They face a unique set of decarbonization constraints that neither regulated utilities nor policy-driven mandates address:

  • No guaranteed cost recovery for clean energy investments
  • Revenue depends on wholesale prices that don't value carbon reduction
  • Must compete against vertically integrated utilities with captive ratebases
  • Decarbonization driven by economics and voluntary commitments, not regulatory mandates

But also unique opportunities: IPPs are nimble, can move capital quickly, develop projects across multiple ISOs, and are less constrained by single-state regulators. This analysis focuses on how IPPs can approach decarbonization transition planning given their position as independent market actors.

America's Power Fleet Is Highly Concentrated—and Highly Diverse

Fleet composition determines decarbonization pathway. There is no one-size-fits-all strategy.

Just 15 companies generate roughly 1,683 TWh of electricity annually—about 40% of total U.S. generation. But their fleets couldn't be more different. Some are nuclear-dominated with already-low carbon intensity. Others burn coal at rates that would have been unremarkable a decade ago but are now increasingly untenable under every major climate framework.1

This diversity matters because a coal-heavy utility in the Midwest faces fundamentally different economics, politics, and physical constraints than a nuclear merchant generator in PJM or a diversified utility with growing renewables in the Southeast.

Sector context (MJ Bradley/ERM 2025): The 100 largest U.S. power producers emitted 1.15 billion tonnes CO₂ in 2024—down ~40% from the 2007 peak, but essentially flat since 2020. In a milestone year, 2024 was the first year solar + wind generated more electricity than coal. Zero-carbon sources now supply ~40% of U.S. electricity. Yet power sector CO₂ rose 3.8% in 2025 as higher gas prices drove a coal-generation rebound.4,20

Every Generator Falls Into One of Four Fleet Archetypes

The decarbonization pathway is determined by starting fleet composition. Four distinct archetypes, each with different economics, opportunities, and constraints.

Coal-Heavy

Evergy, AEP, BH Energy, Vistra, PPL

Coal ≥25% of generation. Highest current emissions intensity (454–572 kg/MWh). But coal retirement is the cheapest form of abatement: new renewables now undercut coal operating costs in most markets.5

The Fleet Composition Paradox

Coal-heavy generators may have the easier—and cheaper—decarbonization path.

Here is the counterintuitive insight at the heart of this analysis: the dirtiest generators may face the most straightforward decarbonization economics.

Coal-to-renewable substitution is now the cheapest form of new generation in most US markets. The levelized cost of new solar ($24–$96/MWh) and wind ($24–$75/MWh) undercuts existing coal operating costs in many regions.5 For a coal-heavy utility, retiring coal and building renewables + storage represents genuine, large-scale CO₂ reduction at costs that may actually lower system costs.

The counterintuitive math: A coal-heavy generator at ~500 kg CO₂/MWh could cut its intensity by 50% simply by replacing coal with wind in resource-rich regions. That's massive abatement at low cost. Meanwhile, a nuclear-led generator at ~180 kg/MWh would need to eliminate virtually all remaining gas generation—requiring expensive clean firm power, storage, or CCS—to achieve a comparable percentage reduction. The marginal cost per tonne rises sharply as fleet intensity falls.

But efficient CCGT + nuclear fleets face diminishing returns. Natural gas combined-cycle plants emit ~363–408 kg CO₂/MWh—already 60% less than coal. Going from gas to zero-carbon requires either CCS ($90–130/MWh with capture)6, new nuclear ($120–180/MWh)5, or massive overbuild of renewables + long-duration storage to cover all hours.

Coal-Heavy Archetype: 519 → 260 kg/MWh

  • Strategy: Replace coal fleet with Kansas wind + battery storage
  • Cost: New wind at $25–35/MWh undercuts coal operating costs ($35–50/MWh)
  • Abatement: ~$15–25/tonne CO₂
  • Barrier: Coal community transition, stranded assets
  • Net bill impact: Potentially negative (lower system costs)

Verdict: 50% reduction is economically rational even without a carbon price. IRA credits make it a no-brainer.

Nuclear-Led Archetype: 177 → 88 kg/MWh

  • Strategy: Must decarbonize ~130 TWh of gas CCGT generation
  • Options: CCS on gas ($90–130/MWh), new renewables+LDES ($80–120/MWh for last-mile matching)
  • Abatement: ~$80–150/tonne CO₂
  • Barrier: CCS at scale unproven; LDES still expensive
  • Net bill impact: Significant cost increase

Verdict: 50% reduction requires either CCS breakthrough, massive storage deployment, or carbon price >$80/tonne to be economic.
