The Fossil Fleet Doesn't Disappear — It Goes Idle

As clean energy procurement scales from 50% to 100% along the SBTi trajectory, fossil plants face two distinct fates. Coal plants physically retire as they become economically unviable — Vistra's Martin Lake and Coleto Creek in ERCOT, NRG's Parish and Limestone, Talen's Brandon Shores and Brunner Island in PJM all have announced exit dates before 2030. Once retired, that nameplate capacity is gone permanently.

Gas plants tell a different story. CCGTs and combustion turbines remain connected to the grid, backed by capacity market contracts, even as their dispatch hours collapse. The nameplate capacity persists — what changes is utilization. A 1,200 MW CCGT running at 65% capacity factor generates 6.8 TWh/yr. At 15% CF, that same plant generates 1.6 TWh/yr. Same iron in the ground, fraction of the output.

The critical question: When does capacity factor erosion become so severe that plants can't cover fixed O&M costs, triggering economic retirement even without regulatory mandates?

Capacity Factor Erosion: The Real Story

Gas CCGTs are the last fossil assets standing. Their nameplate capacity doesn't decline — they remain on the grid as dispatchable backup. But their utilization collapses as clean energy absorbs an ever-larger share of hourly demand.

The merit-order logic is straightforward: as clean procurement rises, fossil dispatch shrinks. Coal and oil are displaced first (highest marginal cost and emissions). Gas combustion turbines (peakers) follow — curtailed as solar+battery displaces their peak-hour niche. CCGTs, the most efficient gas plants, are last.

Economic retirement threshold: Below ~15% capacity factor, CCGT fixed O&M costs (~$15-20/kW-yr) exceed dispatch revenue. Without capacity market payments, plants at this utilization level cannot justify continued operation. In PJM, this threshold is reached around 2043-2045 (92.5-95% clean).

Do CCGTs Actually Retire, or Just Go Idle?

From 2034 onward (65% clean threshold), gas CT/peaker dispatch approaches zero in most regions. All remaining fossil generation falls on CCGTs. Their capacity factor begins a steady decline as clean energy claims more hours of every day:

  • 2030-2034 (50-65% clean): CCGT CF stable at ~65-70%. The decline in gas dispatch is absorbed by curtailing peakers and CTs. CCGTs barely feel the pressure.
  • 2035-2036 (70-75% clean): CTs fully curtailed. CCGT CF drops sharply from ~68% to ~57% as it now bears the full weight of gas dispatch decline.
  • 2037-2040 (80-90% clean): Steady erosion. CCGT CF falls from 46% to 24%. Plants are running less than one day in four.
  • 2043 (92.5% clean): CCGT CF reaches ~18% — at the edge of economic viability. Fixed costs exceed dispatch revenue for less efficient units.
  • 2045+ (95%+ clean): CCGT CF below 13%. Economic retirement is inevitable absent sustained capacity market subsidies.

The answer: CCGT nameplate capacity persists through ~2040-2043, when capacity factor erosion crosses the economic viability threshold. They don't retire because of emissions mandates — they retire because they can't cover fixed costs. Capacity market payments (PJM, NYISO) may extend their operating life by 2-5 years, but the trajectory is clear: stranded capacity, not stranded policy.

Methodology

Nameplate capacity from EIA Form 860 (2024) and PP6 LMP module installed capacity estimates. Coal retirement schedule from company announcements (Vistra, NRG, Talen, AES) and EPA GHG rule compliance analysis. Gas nameplate held constant — physical retirement modeled as economic, triggered when capacity factor drops below ~15%.

Dispatch from the Track 1 baseline merit-order retirement model (Step 4/PP4). Gas split between CCGT and CT via merit-order priority: CCGTs dispatch first (lower heat rate, ~7.0 MMBtu/MWh vs CT ~10.5), CTs curtailed first as total gas dispatch declines. Baseline CCGT share calibrated from EIA capacity factor data by ISO.

SBTi timeline mapping: 50% clean = 2030 through 100% clean = 2050. Capacity factors computed as annual dispatch (TWh) / (nameplate MW x 8,760 hours). Economic retirement threshold at ~15% CF reflects fixed O&M break-even for modern F-class CCGTs ($15-20/kW-yr).
