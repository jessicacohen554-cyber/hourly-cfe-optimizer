ERCOT stands alone as the only US power market where grid-based decarbonization is cheaper than DAC at every threshold tested. Its exceptional wind and solar resources (capacity factors of 35% and 28% respectively) mean that even at 99.9% clean energy, the marginal cost of the last MWh stays below the projected cost of carbon removal.

The key driver is simple: ERCOT’s renewable resource quality is unmatched in the continental US. Combined with moderate firm generation costs and efficient battery dispatch, the total system cost curve stays flat deep into the high-90s.

No-regrets investments for ERCOT include 18 GW of additional wind, 12 GW of solar, and 4 GW of battery storage — resources that appear in the optimal portfolio under all 5,832 cost sensitivity combinations.

Why does firm generation cost matter so much?

In constrained regions like NEISO and NYISO, firm generation (nuclear, CCS, geothermal) is the swing factor. Cheap nuclear extends the grid advantage deep into the 90s — NYISO reaches 99.9% when nuclear costs are low. But expensive firm generation can push the crossover as low as 84% (CAISO under high grid costs). The uncertainty band spans 15 percentage points in some regions, driven almost entirely by firm generation cost assumptions.

The Last Mile Is the Hardest

Moving from 90% to 99% clean energy costs more than moving from 0% to 90%. The marginal abatement cost curve is exponential — each additional percentage point of clean energy requires disproportionately more resources, more storage, and more firm generation.

This is where technology learning curves become decisive. Wright’s Law projections suggest that battery costs will decline 18–25% per doubling of cumulative production, while advanced nuclear (SMRs) could see 15–20% cost reductions. Today’s “last mile” premium may be tomorrow’s bargain.

The optimal strategy is to invest aggressively in no-regrets resources now, then reassess the last-mile approach as technology costs evolve. Locking in today’s firm generation costs for a 99% target would be a $12–47B mistake.
