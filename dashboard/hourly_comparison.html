Three Crediting Regimes

All three strategies require same-ISO hourly matching — every hour of buyer demand must be covered by clean generation within the same grid region. They differ only in how existing clean energy is credited against that obligation.

Strategy 2A

100% New Build

No credit for existing clean generation. Every MWh of hourly matching must come from new-build procurement — new PPAs for solar, wind, clean firm, or storage.

This is the most expensive approach but sends the strongest new-build signal. Treats existing clean as already serving someone else.

Strategy 2B

Grid Baseline

Existing clean generation at its actual 8,760-hour shape is credited for free as a grid baseline. Buyers only procure new-build for the gap above existing clean.

Cheapest when existing clean is high, but creates no incremental market signal for what already exists.

Strategy 2C

SSS + Premium Tranches

State-supported clean (SSS) credited at $0 pro-rata. Existing merchant clean available at EAC premium. Remaining gap filled by new-build PPAs.

Balances incentives: free SSS keeps costs manageable, merchant EAC pricing keeps plants economically viable, new-build signals preserved for the gap.

The core trade-off: 2A maximizes new-build investment signal but is prohibitively expensive. 2B minimizes cost but free-rides on existing assets. 2C threads the needle — SSS at $0 reflects policy intent (ratepayers already funded it), merchant clean at market price keeps plants economically viable, and the remaining gap drives new-build at the margin.

Nuclear Policy Rolloff: What Happens When SSS Expires?

State-level nuclear support programs (ZEC, CMC) have expiration dates. When they expire, the underlying plants don't retire — they shift from SSS (free) to the merchant clean pool (priced). This matters most in PJM, where NJ ZEC (2026) and IL CMC (2028) cover ~65 TWh of nuclear generation.

Why rolloff matters for Strategy 2C: Strategy 2C's cost advantage depends on SSS being free. When ZEC/CMC programs expire, that nuclear generation moves to the merchant pool at EAC market prices ($3–5/MWh). The cost impact is moderate at low thresholds but compounds at high CFE targets where the SSS share of the total procurement is larger. Strategy 2A is unaffected — it ignores existing clean entirely.

The Bottom Line

Which strategy generates optimal clean energy mixes?

Strategy 2C produces the most efficient and investable resource mixes because it correctly prices each supply tranche:

  • SSS at $0 reflects economic reality — ratepayers already funded these plants through ZEC/CMC/RPS programs. Double-charging via voluntary procurement would be double-counting.
  • Merchant clean at EAC premium keeps existing unsubsidized plants (and nuclear uprates) economically viable, preventing premature retirement of assets the grid needs.
  • New-build at PPA price sends an unambiguous build signal for the remaining gap — exactly the incremental clean generation the grid doesn't have yet.

The participation plateau problem

Across all three strategies, costs accelerate non-linearly once participation exceeds ~60–70% of C&I load. At high participation × high targets, the existing clean supply is exhausted and the marginal MWh must come from increasingly expensive new-build clean firm + storage. This isn't a modeling artifact — it reflects real physics: there are only so many good wind/solar sites and existing nuclear plants.

Market reform is the real solution. A carbon price that properly values clean firm generation would solve the cost-signal problem organically. But in the absence of US carbon pricing, Strategy 2C is the best available approximation — it preserves economic signals for existing assets while driving new-build investment at the margin. The participation plateau is a symptom of market failure, not a reason to abandon hourly matching.
