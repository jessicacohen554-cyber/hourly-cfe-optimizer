PJM Grid Buildout

Two solvers, two pathways, one 62.9-GW fossil fleet. How optimization structure and gas procurement rules reshape PJM's 25-year decarbonization trajectory.

PJM Interconnection anchors the largest competitive wholesale market in the world — 65 million customers across 13 states and 62.9 GW of existing fossil generation. Every model run in this analysis asks the same question: what does it cost to decarbonize this fleet to 90%, 95%, 99%, and 99.9% clean energy by 2050?

The answer depends on two structural choices. First, how you optimize: the path-dependent solver finds the cheapest continuous trajectory from 2026 to 2050, while the myopic solver optimizes each threshold independently. Second, how you treat gas: Pathway A builds gas freely with no penalty for future stranding, while Pathway B applies a shadow price that rises linearly from zero (2020) to full replacement cost (2050), discouraging gas lock-in.

Part 1

The Gas-Blind Baseline Path-Dependent

Pathway A, base cost, medium demand, gas mode 3 (gas-blind). This is the upper bound on gas procurement — no shadow price, no dispatch penalty for overbuilding.

When the optimizer faces no penalty for gas construction, it follows a rational but expensive strategy: build cheap gas to meet near-term targets, then replace it with clean resources as thresholds tighten. Gas capacity peaks at 136 GW around 2037 — more than double the existing fossil fleet — before the 95%+ targets force a rapid pivot to hydrogen storage and additional renewables. The cumulative 25-year system cost reaches $1,787B.

Part 2

The Shadow Price Effect: A vs. B Path-Dependent

All-low cost scenario. Both pathways see the same technology costs — the only difference is whether gas carries a stranding shadow price.

Under all-low cost assumptions, the shadow price in Pathway B produces a measurably different grid. Peak gas drops from 87 GW (Pathway A) to 69 GW (Pathway B) — a 21% reduction. Both pathways reach zero gas by 2050, but B gets there earlier and more smoothly. The cumulative savings: $65.5B over 25 years at medium demand. The mechanism is straightforward: the shadow price makes gas increasingly expensive to build in later vintages, so the optimizer front-loads clean firm capacity instead of deferring it.

Part 3

Independent Threshold Optimization: A vs. B Myopic

The myopic solver finds the cheapest resource mix at each threshold independently. Costs are per-threshold, not cumulative.

The myopic solver answers a different question: if you could build from scratch to hit exactly this threshold, what would it cost? Each row in the table below is an independently optimized grid. Pathway B dominates A across every scenario and gas mode, with cost advantages of 20–35% at 99.9% CFE. Gas mode 2 (cost-optimized dispatch) consistently beats mode 3 (gas-blind) by 10–25%, confirming that dispatch intelligence matters even when the optimizer chooses the mix independently at each target.

Part 4

Cross-Solver Comparison

What do the path-dependent and myopic solvers agree on — and where do they diverge?

Both solvers reach the same structural conclusion: Pathway B is cheaper than A. The shadow price on gas capacity produces lower system costs regardless of whether you optimize a 25-year trajectory or each threshold independently. The magnitude differs — path-dependent savings are $65B cumulative, while myopic per-threshold savings range from $5B to $25B — but the direction is unambiguous.

The cost metrics themselves are not directly comparable. Path-dependent cumulative cost includes every year of system operation from 2026 to 2050. Myopic cost is a single-year snapshot of the total system cost at each threshold's achievement year.
