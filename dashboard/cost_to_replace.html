The Nuclear Retirement Premium

What happens to clean energy costs if existing nuclear plants retire?
Isolating the economic value of nuclear in 7 U.S. ISOs

Key Finding

The Nuclear Retirement Premium Is Real — and Regional

If existing nuclear plants retire and must be replaced with new-build equivalents, the cost of clean energy rises significantly. The size of that premium depends on how much nuclear a region has — regions with large nuclear fleets pay the steepest price.

Existing nuclear operates at wholesale electricity prices ($25–42/MWh), far below the cost of building new clean firm capacity ($50–120/MWh). This gap — the nuclear retirement premium — quantifies the hidden economic value of keeping existing nuclear on the grid. All other existing clean generation (hydro, solar, wind) continues operating.

PJM

PJM: 32.1% nuclear means the highest retirement premium

PJM has the largest nuclear fleet of any ISO at 32.1% of clean generation (Limerick, Peach Bottom, Susquehanna, and 15+ other plants). These plants dispatch at wholesale rates (~$34/MWh), but new nuclear construction costs $75–120/MWh. If this fleet retires, the cost premium is the steepest in the country — existing solar (2.9%) and wind (3.8%) continue operating, but they can't replace firm dispatchable capacity. PJM illustrates why preserving existing nuclear is the single most cost-effective decarbonization strategy.

ERCOT: Small nuclear fleet means a small retirement premium

With only 8.6% nuclear capacity (South Texas Project, Comanche Peak), ERCOT has the least nuclear exposure of any ISO. If these plants retire, the cost increase is modest because the region's existing wind and solar fleet — which continues operating at wholesale — already supplies most clean energy. ERCOT shows that regions with small nuclear shares face minimal retirement risk.

CAISO

CAISO: Diablo Canyon retirement would widen the cost gap

California's 5.3% nuclear share (Diablo Canyon, since relicensed) provides firm baseload at wholesale rates. If it retires, the optimizer must replace that dispatchable capacity with new-build clean firm — at 2–4× the cost. Existing hydro (9.5%) and geothermal (2.6%) continue operating, dampening the premium. But at higher thresholds where firm capacity becomes critical, losing Diablo Canyon forces the grid toward expensive alternatives like new nuclear or CCS.

NYISO

NYISO: 18.4% nuclear makes retirement a high-stakes bet

New York's 18.4% nuclear fleet (Nine Mile Point, FitzPatrick, Ginna) provides firm dispatchable power at wholesale rates. Hydro (15.9% from Niagara Falls, St. Lawrence) continues operating regardless. But if nuclear retires, replacing that 18.4% with new-build clean firm at $75–120/MWh drives a significant premium. NYISO's already-high wholesale prices ($42/MWh) mean the relative premium is smaller than PJM's, but the absolute cost impact is substantial for a region with limited renewable resources.

NEISO: Millstone anchors the fleet — losing it would be catastrophic

New England runs 23.8% of its clean energy from nuclear (Millstone, Seabrook). These plants provide baseload capacity through harsh winters when gas pipeline constraints already spike energy costs. Replacing this nuclear capacity at new-build prices would push NEISO's already-steep cost curve even higher. The winter gas constraint means CCS-CCGT replacement carries an additional $13/MWh adder, compounding the premium. NEISO demonstrates that existing nuclear is not just cheap — it's irreplaceable in constrained grids.

What Drives the Nuclear Retirement Premium?

Nuclear: The Hidden Economic Anchor

Existing nuclear plants dispatch at wholesale electricity rates ($25–42/MWh) because their capital costs are sunk. New nuclear costs $75–120/MWh; CCS-CCGT alternatives run $55–85/MWh. This spread — the difference between operating existing nuclear and building new firm capacity — is the retirement premium. Existing hydro, solar, and wind continue operating regardless; this analysis isolates the cost impact of losing nuclear specifically. Allowing working nuclear plants to retire is the most expensive path to decarbonization.

Nuclear wholesale: $25–42/MWh · New-build firm: $55–120/MWh · Spread = retirement premium

Nuclear Is the Anchor

Regions with large nuclear fleets — PJM (32.1%), NEISO (23.8%), NYISO (18.4%) — show the largest replacement premiums. Nuclear new-build ($75–120/MWh) dwarfs wholesale pricing. Meanwhile, ERCOT (8.6% nuclear) shows minimal premium because its clean fleet is weighted toward renewables, where new-build costs are closer to existing PPA rates. The single largest driver of replacement cost is the share of nuclear in the existing fleet. Preserving these plants isn't sentimentality — it's the cheapest decarbonization resource available.
