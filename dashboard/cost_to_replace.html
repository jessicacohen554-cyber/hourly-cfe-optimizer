The Nuclear Retirement Premium

Why existing nuclear is irreplaceable — and what it would cost to try

The Central Paradox

The Grid Can't Afford to Lose Nuclear — But the Market Won't Pay to Keep It

Even when capacity market payments are included alongside wholesale energy revenue, the full revenue stream falls below nuclear viability at ~70% clean energy. Replacing retired nuclear with new-build clean firm costs 3–5× what it would take to keep it running. This isn't just about keeping plants afloat — nuclear needs enough earnings to reinvest in uprates, relicensing, and capacity expansion.

Capacity payments help but don't close the gap. At 90%+ clean energy, total market revenue (energy + capacity) drops to $8–17/MWh — well below the $38–44/MWh needed for nuclear viability. Many existing nuclear sites are already permitted for additional reactors, making them the best candidates to jumpstart Wright's learning curve for new nuclear. But that requires economic stability, not bare-minimum survival.

The Structural Market Failure

The Full Revenue Stream Still Falls Short

Capacity payments help but don't solve the problem. Even when stacked on top of wholesale energy revenue, the total market revenue stream falls below nuclear viability at high clean energy thresholds. By ~70% clean, no combination of energy + capacity revenue supports nuclear viability without additional out-of-market payments.

This isn't a temporary dip. The revenue decline is structural, not cyclical. Each increment of clean energy permanently displaces the marginal fossil unit that set the energy price. Capacity markets partially compensate but can't keep pace with the collapse in energy revenue. Nuclear, which runs regardless of price, sees its total revenue erode with no mechanism for recovery.

The Investment Case: Beyond Survival

Nuclear doesn't just need enough revenue to cover operating costs ($25–30/MWh). It needs enough to:

1. Reinvest in uprates — existing sites can expand capacity 5–20% through power uprates, adding GWs of firm clean energy at a fraction of greenfield cost.

2. Fund relicensing — 20-year license extensions require billions in safety upgrades, inspections, and capital improvements. These investments need revenue certainty.

3. Jumpstart Wright's learning curve — many existing nuclear sites are already permitted for additional reactors. Economic stability at these sites is the prerequisite for deploying next-generation designs that drive costs down through learning-by-doing.

The viable price ($38–44/MWh) isn't a luxury — it's the minimum for a fleet that can grow, not just survive.
