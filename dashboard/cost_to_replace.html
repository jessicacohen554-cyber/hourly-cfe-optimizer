The Nuclear Retirement Premium

Why existing nuclear is irreplaceable — and what it would cost to try

The Central Paradox

The Grid Can't Afford to Lose Nuclear — But the Market Won't Pay to Keep It

Wholesale prices collapse below nuclear viability at ~70% clean energy (~2033). But replacing retired nuclear with new-build clean firm costs 3–5× the wholesale price that caused the retirement. This isn't just about keeping plants afloat — nuclear needs enough earnings to reinvest in uprates, relicensing, and capacity expansion.

Many existing nuclear sites are already permitted for additional reactors, making them the best candidates to jumpstart Wright's learning curve for new nuclear. But that requires economic stability and profit for the existing fleet — not bare-minimum survival at operating cost. The market design that depresses wholesale prices is simultaneously undermining the fastest path to affordable new nuclear.

The Math: Viability Premium vs. Replacement Cost

Paying a small premium above wholesale to keep nuclear viable ($5–20/MWh depending on threshold) costs a fraction of building replacement clean firm capacity ($90–170/MWh). The viability premium isn't charity — it's an investment in uprate capacity, relicensing, and Wright's learning curve for new nuclear at already-permitted sites.

The Structural Market Failure

The Price Signal Is Broken

Wholesale prices collapse as clean energy grows — that's the intended outcome. But the same price signal that makes renewables competitive destroys the revenue base for existing nuclear. By ~70% clean, no fuel price scenario supports nuclear viability without out-of-market payments. The market design that accelerates decarbonization simultaneously undermines the cheapest firm clean energy source on the grid.

This isn't a temporary dip. The wholesale price decline is structural, not cyclical. Each increment of clean energy permanently displaces the marginal fossil unit that set the price. Nuclear, which runs regardless of price, sees its revenue collapse with no mechanism for recovery.

The Investment Case: Beyond Survival

Nuclear doesn't just need enough revenue to cover operating costs ($25–30/MWh). It needs enough to:

1. Reinvest in uprates — existing sites can expand capacity 5–20% through power uprates, adding GWs of firm clean energy at a fraction of greenfield cost.

2. Fund relicensing — 20-year license extensions require billions in safety upgrades, inspections, and capital improvements. These investments need revenue certainty.

3. Jumpstart Wright's learning curve — many existing nuclear sites are already permitted for additional reactors. Economic stability at these sites is the prerequisite for deploying next-generation designs that drive costs down through learning-by-doing.

The viable price ($38–44/MWh) isn't a luxury — it's the minimum for a fleet that can grow, not just survive.
