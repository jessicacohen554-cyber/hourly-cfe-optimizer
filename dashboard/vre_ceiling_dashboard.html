1

The cheap stretch: baseline to ~80% CFE

Wind and solar are genuinely cheap through the first 30–40 percentage points of additional clean energy. The question is what happens after.

Every U.S. grid already gets some clean energy for free — existing hydro, nuclear, and legacy renewables provide a 30–50% baseline. From that starting point, the first 30–40 percentage points of additional CFE come remarkably cheap. Wind and solar generation is complementary enough that curtailment stays below 10%, and average delivered cost sits under $90/MWh in most ISOs.

ERCOT, SPP, and MISO lead: their wind resources are so strong that average LCOE stays under $65/MWh through 80% CFE. Coastal and congested grids (NYISO, CAISO) are pricier but still manageable.

2

The elbow: ~80–90% CFE

Each additional percentage point costs dramatically more than the last. The marginal cost — not the average — is the metric that matters.

Around 80–85% CFE, the economics shift. Wind and solar output clusters in the same hours, so pushing beyond ~80% means building capacity that generates mostly during hours the grid already has surplus clean energy. Curtailment leaps from single digits to 30–40%.

The marginal cost of CFE — what it costs to buy the next percentage point — explodes. The elbow is not a cliff — it is an exponential curve.

Average vs. marginal LCOE

Average LCOE divides total cost by total clean energy delivered. Marginal LCOE measures the cost of the next increment of clean energy. When deciding whether to build more renewables or switch to storage, the marginal cost is the one that matters.

The crossover zone: where alternatives become cheaper

When the marginal cost of the next point of wind+solar CFE exceeds what batteries or clean firm could deliver, it is time to switch tools.

The crossover zone is where renewables-only marginal cost exceeds the effective cost of alternative clean technologies. When the marginal cost of the next point of wind+solar CFE exceeds these benchmarks, you would be better off buying that technology instead.

This crossover happens between 80% and 85% CFE depending on the ISO. Wind-rich grids (SPP, ERCOT, MISO) push the crossover higher; constrained grids (CAISO, NYISO) hit it sooner.

4

The offshore question

Does offshore wind push the economic ceiling meaningfully higher for coastal ISOs?

For four coastal ISOs, offshore wind offers a different capacity factor profile — it generates during hours when onshore wind and solar do not, reducing curtailment at high targets.

The answer depends on where you are. In NYISO and NEISO, adding offshore wind reduces cost at 95% CFE by ~40%. In PJM, the reduction is ~30%. In CAISO, where onshore wind is already strong, offshore adds only ~7%. Offshore wind does not eliminate the elbow — it shifts it a few percentage points to the right and lowers the slope.

5

The punchline

The renewables ceiling is economic, not physical. Here is where it falls for each ISO.

Every ISO can technically reach 98–99% CFE with wind and solar alone. But the cost of those last percentage points is 5–50× the cost of the first stretch, with 60–80% of generated energy curtailed.

The practical ceiling — the point where battery storage becomes cheaper on the margin — falls between ~73% and ~85% CFE depending on the grid. Getting from there to 100% requires a different toolkit: storage, clean firm, or demand flexibility.

The curtailment problem

At 95% CFE, over half of all generated renewable energy is curtailed in every ISO. The marginal MWh costs $200–700+/MWh — far more than any alternative. This is the economic case for portfolio diversification beyond renewables alone.
