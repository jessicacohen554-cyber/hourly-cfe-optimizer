The VRE-Only CFE Ceiling

How far can wind and solar alone take every US ISO?

The question this page answers is narrow on purpose: holding existing clean generation frozen at its 2025 terawatt-hour (TWh) baseline and adding only onshore wind, utility-scale solar, and (for the coastal independent system operators) offshore wind, what is the highest hourly carbon-free energy (CFE) match score the grid can physically reach? No new storage, no new clean-firm nuclear or carbon-capture, no hybrid projects — just variable renewable energy (VRE) scaled against the existing fleet.

The ceiling is higher than the conventional wisdom suggests. Every independent system operator (ISO) in the US lands above 98.8% hourly match. Getting there costs hundreds of billions per year and requires gigawatts (GW) of capacity that dwarf every project queue in the country — but the physics works.

Ground rules for every chart below

  • Existing clean frozen at 2025 TWh. Hydro, nuclear, and in-region clean imports are held at absolute 2025 terawatt-hours. As demand grows, that fixed baseline shrinks as a share of demand.
  • No storage, no clean-firm, no hybrids. The feasible set is strictly onshore wind plus solar, with offshore wind added for the four coastal ISOs.
  • Costs are undiscounted 2025 dollars. Annualized as added megawatt-hours per year times levelized cost of energy (LCOE) plus a transmission adder. No learning curves — solar and wind are mature-tech in this pipeline. Low, Medium, and High LCOE columns carry the sensitivity range.

Every ISO can reach 98.9–99.9% hourly CFE on wind and solar alone

With existing clean generation held at its 2025 baseline and only onshore wind plus solar added on top, the 2025-demand ceiling lands above 98.8% in every ISO.

The lift is largest for the ISOs that start low. The Midcontinent ISO (MISO) and New England ISO (NEISO) both open below 32% hourly match on their 2025 fleet and gain nearly 70 percentage points. The Southwest Power Pool (SPP), with its wind-heavy resource base, reaches 99.94% — close enough that the residual gap is measurement noise. None of this requires a new technology; it requires enough nameplate capacity to cover every shape of residual load, which is a different conversation.

What the ceiling costs: capacity and dollars

The physics works. The capacity bill to make it work is on the scale of three to four times the country's installed wind and solar fleet, concentrated in a handful of ISOs.

Added onshore wind and solar capacity to reach the ceiling, with annual cost — 2025 demand, Medium LCOE
Added wind capacity (GW) Added solar capacity (GW) Annual cost ($B/yr, Medium LCOE)

The spread is wide. NEISO's ceiling costs about $70 billion per year and needs roughly 175 GW of wind plus 263 GW of solar — smallest of the seven ISOs by a wide margin. PJM sits at the other end: 1,284 GW of wind and 1,699 GW of solar, $420 billion per year. That one ISO alone would need to build more wind than the entire US fleet three times over, plus more solar than the world had installed as of 2023. MISO is the runner-up at $269 billion per year.

Cost scales roughly with demand. PJM is the largest electricity market in the sample (about 950 TWh of demand in 2025), so the VRE-ceiling capacity and the annual cost both scale with that demand base. Smaller markets — NEISO at 118 TWh, NYISO at 147 TWh — post smaller totals but need the same proportional build relative to demand.

Diminishing returns: why neither resource gets there alone

In ERCOT — the cleanest example because the resource base is wind-rich and the existing fleet is simple — wind or solar by itself hits a ceiling well short of a CFE match. Only the joint build reaches the high-99s.

Why single-axis saturates

Solar alone caps at 62% match, saturating around 109 GW of added capacity. Every night hour in ERCOT is structurally un-dispatchable from solar — you can build a terawatt of panels and still deliver zero between 10 PM and 5 AM. The existing clean baseline (42.9%) plus solar's daylight contribution adds up to roughly 62% before marginal capacity adds nothing.

Wind alone caps at 99.1% match, saturating around 296 GW. ERCOT's wind resource is the best in the country, so wind alone closes most of the residual gap. What wind alone can't close is a handful of low-wind weeks each year, which is why the final percentage point requires a different resource.

Joint builds bend around 150–300 GW total. The diminishing-returns elbow — marginal CFE pickup per additional GW — kicks in early. The last percentage point from 99% to 99.5% takes roughly as much capacity as the first fifty. That's the cost curve the $173 billion per year price tag is paying for.

This is also why the task brief for this analysis excluded storage and clean-firm from the filter. A few GW of batteries or a slice of new nuclear would flatten the elbow and cut the capacity requirement by half or more — but the point of this chart is to isolate what variable renewable energy alone can do. When we add those options back in (separate pages elsewhere on this site), the same 99%+ ceiling becomes reachable with one-third the nameplate build.

4

Demand stress: the ceiling barely moves; the baseline collapses

Hold the ceiling question constant and vary demand along three compound-annual-growth-rate (CAGR) paths — Low, Medium, High — across 2025 through 2050. The surprise is which line holds and which line falls apart.

The punchline

The ceiling barely moves. Across every ISO, every demand level, and every year through 2050, the VRE-only ceiling drops by less than 0.6 percentage points. What collapses is the baseline — existing clean generation holds constant in TWh, so as demand grows, its share of demand shrinks. ERCOT's baseline slides from 43% in 2025 to 11% in 2050 under High demand (3.8× the 2025 load). The ceiling still sits at 99.36%. The required build scales roughly with demand.

Read the charts this way: the three solid lines nearly overlap at the top of every panel — that's the ceiling, essentially flat through 2050. The three dashed lines fan downward — that's the existing-clean share of demand as demand grows. The gap between solid and dashed is what new wind and solar have to close, and the capacity required to close it scales roughly one-for-one with demand. The ceiling doesn't care how big the grid gets; it only cares whether you built enough capacity to match each hour.

5

Offshore wind: closing the last percentage point — and sometimes the cost gap

For the four coastal ISOs where offshore wind is a real build option — NEISO, NYISO, PJM, and CAISO — adding it to the mix does two things: it closes the final 0.5–1.1 percentage-point gap to near-unity CFE, and in PJM's case it cuts the annual bill by about $80 billion per year by substituting high-capacity-factor offshore for some of the solar overbuild.

Why offshore changes the math

Offshore wind's capacity factor (CF) runs in the 45–55% range versus 30–40% for onshore wind and 20–27% for solar in these regions. That higher CF means one megawatt of offshore delivers roughly 1.5–2× the annual terawatt-hours of one megawatt of onshore, with a production shape that covers more hours of the day — particularly evening and overnight, when solar has already rolled off.

The effect is most visible in PJM. Onshore-only costs $420 billion per year with nearly 1,700 GW of solar. Adding 201 GW of offshore cuts solar overbuild substantially, and the total annual cost drops to $342 billion per year — $80 billion per year cheaper for a higher final match score (99.31% → 99.98%). In the other coastal ISOs, offshore is slightly more expensive than the pure-onshore path, but closes the final gap to effectively 100%.

Scene 5 is the narrow case where adding a single technology reshapes the bill. The broader point across all five scenes is that the VRE-only ceiling is not the interesting design question — it's the existence proof. Every US ISO can physically reach near-complete hourly CFE on wind and solar alone, and for most of the country the build required to do it is so large that the practical design conversation is really about how storage, clean-firm, and hybrid projects cut the capacity bill by half or more.
