Pure Consequential vs Hourly Matching

Two procurement strategies. One chases the cheapest $/tCO2 at each step — locking in resources that can't be unbuilt. The other invests in clean firm technologies along the FOAK→NOAK learning curve — less early on when costs are high, gradually increasing deployment as Wright's Law drives costs down. Same physics, same feasible mixes — radically different outcomes.

The Path-Dependent Trap

The sequential path's clean firm deployment is erratic — forced into large buildouts at certain thresholds where the inherited renewable base simply can't achieve hourly matching. The hourly matching path shows a steady ramp: small but deliberate clean firm investment early (FOAK pricing) that seeds the learning curve, growing into large-scale deployment as costs decline toward NOAK. The sequential path has to compensate for a renewable-heavy legacy; the hourly matching path builds the right technologies from the start, just at a pace that tracks the learning curve.

The FOAK→NOAK Learning Curve: Why Hourly Matching Gets Cheaper

Scenario B assumes new nuclear and clean firm are deployed along Wright's Law learning curves. Costs start at FOAK levels ($135–170/MWh) and decline ~40% after 10–20 deployments, converging on NOAK pricing ($68–75/MWh) by the late 2030s — while gas costs rise with fuel prices and carbon pricing.

The learning curve is the mechanism behind the hourly matching scenario. Scenario B's Low clean firm costs ($68–75/MWh) aren't hypothetical — they correspond to the NOAK endpoint of documented learning curves. DOE's Liftoff report projects ~40% cost reduction after 5–10 reactors per design, reaching $3,600/kW and ~$66/MWh. NREL ATB 2024 models nuclear learning at 8–9.5% per doubling. CCS follows a parallel trajectory (DOE OCED: ~25% CAPEX reduction FOAK→NOAK). The hourly matching strategy is an investment thesis: pay the FOAK premium today to unlock NOAK pricing for all subsequent deployments.

Bottom Line: Pure Consequential Fails — Hourly Matching Wins

Investing in clean firm along the learning curve dramatically outperforms the sequential path. The hourly matching strategy pays more early on — FOAK clean firm is expensive — but those early investments seed the learning curve. By mid-range thresholds (80-90%), costs have declined enough for the optimizer to deploy significant clean firm, and by 95% the learning curve delivers near-NOAK pricing. The sequential path, locked into renewable overbuilds, pays far more to achieve the same CFE targets.

The mechanism is path dependency vs. learning investment. The sequential strategy builds massive renewable portfolios because they're cheapest at each step (clean firm is expensive at High LCOE). Those locked-in renewables saturate the sunny/windy hours. At higher thresholds, the hard hours — winter nights, low-wind weeks — need firm generation, but the optimizer can't unwind the overbuilding. The hourly matching path invests in clean firm from the start — less early on, more as costs decline — building a balanced portfolio where firm generation covers the hours that no amount of renewables can reach.

Methodology

How this comparison is constructed and what it does (and doesn't) model.

Same Physics, Different Procurement Strategies

Both scenarios draw from an identical pool of ~33,000 physics-feasible resource mixes produced by the 8,760-hour dispatch model (Step 1 PFS Generator). Each mix is a validated combination of clean firm, solar, wind, CCS-CCGT, hydro, 4-hour battery, 8-hour battery, and 100-hour LDES that achieves a specific hourly CFE threshold across all 8,760 hours of 2025 demand and generation data. The physics are fixed — what changes between scenarios is the procurement strategy: sequential path-dependent optimization vs. targeting a single high threshold directly.

Scenario A: Pure Consequential

The consequential scenario models a buyer who procures the cheapest resources at each threshold step, with prior deployments locked in as committed infrastructure:

  1. At 50% CFE: find the cheapest feasible mix from the existing grid floor. Deploy those resources.
  2. At 60% CFE: resources deployed at 50% are now the floor — they can't be unbuilt. The optimizer can only add on top. Among all feasible mixes at 60% that meet the floor constraint (every resource ≥ what was deployed at 50%), select the cheapest.
  3. Continue sequentially through 70%, 75%, 80%, ... 100%. Each step inherits all prior commitments.
  4. Resources remain priced at their original LCOE — committed capacity does NOT shift to wholesale pricing. The sunk cost creates path dependency but doesn't create a pricing advantage.

When the discrete feasible-mix space is too sparse for strict monotonicity, the optimizer uses a soft floor: each mix is scored by its total floor violation (TWh shortfall across all resources), and the cheapest mix with minimum violation is selected. The floor ratchets up using the element-wise maximum of the old floor and new deployment.

Scenario B: Hourly Matching

The hourly matching scenario models graduated clean firm deployment along the FOAK→NOAK learning curve. At each threshold, clean firm / CCS / LDES / geothermal costs are interpolated between High (FOAK) and Low (NOAK) based on the threshold's position on the SBTi timeline, using a convex ramp function: frac = 0.05 + 0.95 × ((t−50)/50)1.8. Mature technologies (solar, wind, battery) remain at Low cost throughout — they're already down their learning curves.

At each threshold, the optimizer independently selects the cheapest feasible mix given the interpolated costs. Early thresholds see less clean firm (costs are near FOAK), but the 5% floor ensures some investment begins — enough to seed the learning curve. As cumulative deployment drives Wright's Law doublings, costs decline and the optimizer naturally selects more clean firm at each subsequent milestone. There is no path-dependency floor; the increasing deployment emerges from the interaction of declining costs and increasing physics requirements for firm generation at higher CFE thresholds.

Cost Function (Both Scenarios)

Effective system cost = total procurement cost / hourly match fraction, including:

  • Existing generation at wholesale price; new generation at LCOE + transmission adder
  • Clean firm tranche pricing: uprates first, then geothermal (CAISO), then cheapest of nuclear/CCS
  • Storage costs: battery (4hr/8hr) and LDES at scenario-specific LCOE
  • Gas backup: resource adequacy requirement (15% margin above peak) minus clean capacity credits, priced at fixed O&M (existing) or full annualized cost (new build)

The cost function is identical between scenarios. What differs is (a) the cost toggle levels, (b) the optimization strategy (sequential with floor constraints vs. single-target), and (c) the resulting optimal resource mix.

Gas Backup & Capacity Credits

Gas capacity is not a choice variable — it's derived from resource adequacy constraints. Each clean resource receives a capacity credit (clean firm: 100%, CCS: 90%, LDES: 90%, battery: 95%, hydro: 50%, solar: 30%, wind: 10%). The remaining gap between clean capacity and the RA requirement (peak demand × 1.15) must be filled by gas. Clean firm's 100% capacity credit displaces gas MW-for-MW; wind's 10% credit means 10 MW of wind displaces only 1 MW of gas.

Marginal Abatement Cost

Stepwise MAC is computed between consecutive thresholds using the dispatch-stack fossil retirement model: coal retires first (highest emissions + marginal cost), then oil, then gas. The marginal emission rate of displaced fossil shifts from coal-heavy (~1.0 tCO2/MWh) at low thresholds to gas-only (~0.4 tCO2/MWh) at high thresholds. MAC = Δ(system cost × demand) / ΔCO2 displaced between threshold steps.

SBTi Timeline Mapping

CFE thresholds are mapped to SBTi-aligned deployment years: 50%→2025, 60%→2027, 70%→2029, 75%→2030, 80%→2032, 85%→2035, 87.5%→2037, 90%→2040, 92.5%→2042, 95%→2045, 97.5%→2047, 99%→2049, 100%→2050. This maps the static threshold comparison onto a temporal deployment trajectory.

What This Analysis Does Not Model

  • Dynamic learning curves — costs are fixed within each scenario. The hourly matching scenario assumes the FOAK→NOAK learning curve has driven costs to Low levels; it does not model the investment-to-learning feedback loop endogenously.
  • Construction timelines — all resources are assumed available at each threshold. In practice, the 99% cliff in the consequential path would face multi-year nuclear/CCS construction lead times.
  • Demand growth — base 2025 demand is used throughout. Real demand growth (1-3.5%/yr by region) would amplify all effects shown here.
  • Inter-regional transmission — each ISO is optimized independently. Cross-ISO power flows could moderate some regional cost differences.
  • Gas stranding economics — gas backup is costed at annual fixed O&M / annualized capital, not lifecycle NPV. The economic loss from building-then-stranding gas is understated.
