The Reliability Tax

What ratepayers pay when wind, solar, and storage are asked to cover reliability on their own.

Wind, solar, and batteries can deliver clean energy. They cannot, on their own, cover the worst hour of the worst week — the hour grid planners are legally obligated to keep the lights on for. Something has to stand behind them. Absent new clean firm generation, that something is gas. The capex, fixed costs, and eventual write-off of that backup fleet land on customer bills. That’s the reliability tax.

1

Why a clean-only grid still builds gas

As clean-energy targets rise, the grid doesn’t need more firm capacity. It needs firm capacity that’s used less. That’s an expensive shape to buy.

70% clean
Moderate solar. Gentle net-load curve. Firm gas still runs a broad share of hours.
95% clean
Enough solar to saturate midday (curtailment). Same sharp evening peak — still gas territory.
Demand (load) Solar dispatched Residual gap (gas territory)

Between 70% and 95% clean, solar and storage scale up faster than the underlying load changes shape. Midday fills in — often spills over, forcing curtailment — but the evening peak barely moves. The gap that firm capacity has to cover doesn’t shrink evenly; it gets narrower and sharper. A few hundred hours a year still demand something that burns fuel, and those hours now land in a tighter, pricier window.

We size new-build gas against that sharper gap directly from hour-by-hour dispatch. No rule-of-thumb capacity credits — a flat 30% credit for solar or 95% for batteries smooths over the worst week and systematically under-builds for it. Instead, gas is sized to the hour that 8,757 other hours exceed (roughly 2.6 hours of tail risk per year), which is the bar planners actually have to clear. That’s the physical rule behind every MW on the rest of this page.

How much new gas gets built

On a wind-and-solar-only path, new gas capacity peaks somewhere in the middle of the clean-energy journey — not at the end. This is the shape of that build-up.

New-gas capacity built, by clean-energy target
 
The build-out peaks in the middle, not the end. Most ISOs top out their new-gas fleet at moderate clean-energy targets and then flatten or decline as wind, solar, and storage absorb the later increments. ERCOT peaks around 80% clean at ~134 GW; PJM at ~80% and ~106 GW; MISO at 90% and ~70 GW. Vintages built for that mid-journey peak rarely earn their book value back — by 2050 they’re running under 15% of the hours they were sized for, which in practice means the capital invested in them is written off.

How much of it gets stranded

Gas built for the mid-journey peak runs less and less as clean energy keeps scaling. When a plant’s capacity factor drops below 15% for two years in a row, the rest of its book value gets written off. The write-off lands on ratepayers.

ERCOT and SPP don’t have capacity markets. That doesn’t make the reliability tax disappear — it just gets recovered through scarcity pricing during tight hours instead of stable capacity payments. Either way, the total cost lands on customer bills.
5

Five pathways, five bills

Every way past 90% clean hands customers a bill — but the size of that bill is deeply regional. In wind-rich grids it barely matters which pathway you pick. In wind-constrained grids, committing to clean firm up front cuts the reliability tax by 50–70%.

Two findings to watch:
  1. ERCOT: wind-only ≈ proactive clean firm. At every target, the two pathways land within ~1% on total cost. When a grid is already winning on cheap wind, clean firm has no room to beat it — the optimizer picks the same mix either way.
  2. PJM: proactive clean firm cuts the bill by two-thirds. At 90% clean, total system cost drops from $7.17T to $2.39T. The curtailment bar ($275B on wind-and-solar-only) disappears when ~79% of the mix comes from clean firm instead of overbuilt wind and solar.
How to read the cards. The top number is what customers pay per MWh, gross of any capacity-market credits. The MW number underneath is new-build gas that fell below 15% capacity factor for two years running — i.e., gas that got built and then written off before earning its capital back. In ERCOT, all five cards look roughly the same. In PJM, proactive clean firm collapses to a thin bar while wind-only stays tall. Same lever, very different outcome depending on how much wind a grid already runs on.
6

Where the stranded capital lands

Every new-build gas plant that runs out of hours — below 15% capacity factor for two years in a row — feeds into a single pot of unrecovered capital. Vintages built for the 2035–2045 peak are the most exposed; they don’t have enough years left before 2050 to pay themselves back.

Stranded gas capital by pathway
 
Every dollar on the right side of this chart is capital that ratepayers put into new gas that the plant will never earn back. The write-off trigger is simple: two years in a row running below 15% of the hours it was sized for. Once that happens, whatever hasn’t been recovered at that point becomes stranded.

The cost of waiting

If clean firm isn’t committed up front, every year of delay adds to the bill. The curve below shows the reliability tax a 95%-clean grid would carry in 2050, read against the year the clean-firm commitment finally gets made.

Even committing in 2026 costs customers more than committing in 2025. By 2035, the first mid-journey gas vintages have already fallen below the 15% capacity-factor line and half the cost-decline runway for clean firm has already been spent. By 2045, the grid is effectively locked into the onshore wind-and-solar path regardless of intent — there’s no way to pay down the stranding bill once the capital is already in the ground.
