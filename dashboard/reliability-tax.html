The Reliability Tax

What ratepayers pay when VRE+storage is forced to cover reliability.

VRE and storage can deliver clean energy. They cannot, on their own, deliver reliability — not at the 99.97th-percentile worst hour that planners are obligated to cover. Something has to stand behind them for those hours, and absent new clean firm generation, that something is gas. The capex, fixed O&M, and eventual stranding of that backup fleet land on customer bills. That is the reliability tax.

Reliability tax = (annualized new-gas capex + new-gas FOM + carried existing-gas FOM + priced VRE curtailment + annualized VRE/storage overbuild capex) ÷ total demand MWh. Reported gross — capacity-market payments and similar transfers are internal to the ratepayer–developer system, not a cost reduction, and are not netted against this number.

As CFE rises from 70% to 95%, solar and storage get added faster than the underlying load shape changes. Midday demand is covered — often oversupplied, forcing curtailment — but the evening peak moves barely at all. The residual gap doesn’t shrink uniformly; it gets narrower and sharper. A few hundred hours a year still need firm capacity, and those hours are now concentrated in a tighter window.

The reliability tax sizes new-build gas against that sharper residual. Following SPEC §24.5, we do not use ELCC credits — a fixed 0.30 solar credit or 0.95 battery credit averages across the year and systematically under-sizes the worst hour. Instead, gas is sized to the 99.97th percentile of the hourly margin-on-demand residual, computed directly from the 8,760 dispatch. That percentile leaves roughly 2.6 hours a year of tail risk above the sized gap — consistent with the NERC / PJM 1-day-in-10-years LOLE benchmark. Every MW of gas on the page below was sized by that physical rule, not by a credit factor.

The Hump

How much new gas gets built on the way to a clean grid — and how much of it ends up stranded.

 
The hump. New-gas build rises as pathways chase the medium-CFE thresholds (roughly 80–90%), then plateaus or falls as the grid pushes into the last mile. Most of the gas built to get to 85–90% CFE gets abandoned once the system climbs past 90%. The capex has been paid; the revenue window closes. That write-off is one leg of the reliability tax.

The Abandonment

Most of the gas built at medium CFE thresholds gets used less and less each year. When a vintage’s capacity factor falls below 15% for two consecutive years (SPEC Card F′), its unrecovered book value is written off as stranded capital.

ERCOT and SPP have no capacity markets. Ratepayers still pay the reliability tax — it’s recovered through scarcity-priced energy during tight hours rather than through stable capacity payments. Either way, the total cost lands on customer bills.

Five pathways, five taxes

Every pathway that reaches 95% CFE hands customers a reliability bill. The size of that bill — and the MW of new gas stranded getting there — depends entirely on when (and whether) clean firm shows up in the plan.
