The Exit: Clean Firm Cost Trajectory

Clean firm power is expensive today because it's first-of-a-kind. Learning curves and rising gas costs change that equation within a decade.

Build clean firm today.
It's cheaper in the long run.

Vogtle Units 3&4 came in at ~$15,000/kW. The DOE Liftoff report targets $3,600/kW after 5–10 deployments per design — roughly $66/MWh. MIT’s 2024 analysis puts AP1000 NOAK at $4,625/kW ($66/MWh unsubsidized). Meanwhile, gas plant capital costs have tripled to $2,400/kW (GridLab 2025), fuel prices are rising, and carbon pricing is tightening.

Every energy technology follows Wright’s Law: costs decline with deployment. Solar achieved a 24% learning rate per doubling. Wind: 15%. NREL ATB 2024 models nuclear learning at 8–9.5% per doubling. CCS-CCGT follows at 3–5% per doubling (NETL), with the 45Q credit ($85/ton) reducing near-term costs to ~$92/MWh. As both technologies descend their learning curves, the clean firm portfolio converges on $68–75/MWh by the late 2030s.

An important caveat: these projections assume positive learning rates that Western nuclear has not historically achieved. Grubler (2010) documented cost escalation in the French program; Vogtle 3&4 came in at $30–36.8B vs. $14B estimate. Our High cost toggle captures pessimistic outcomes within the NREL ATB range, but does not model cost escalation beyond those bounds.

Diversifying beyond VRE + storage derisks grid decarbonization and prevents wasted investment solidifying the future of fossil fuels via redundant gas backup. The FOAK premium lasts one deployment cycle — roughly 8–12 years. Gas lock-in lasts the economic life of the plant — 25–40 years.

Wright's Law: Learning Curves & Critical Mass

Every energy technology follows Wright's Law. The question is when enough buyers commit to trigger the first doubling.

The Critical Mass Question

Wright's Law predicts that technology costs decline by a fixed percentage each time cumulative production doubles. Solar panels followed this pattern — falling 99% since 1976. But clean firm generation (advanced nuclear, CCS-CCGT) hasn't hit the first doubling yet. The question isn't whether costs will fall, but when enough buyers commit to trigger the learning curve.

This analysis models what happens when C&I electricity buyers collectively adopt hourly CFE matching (Strategy 2C) at different participation levels. Below a critical mass threshold, new-build clean firm stays at FOAK pricing. Above it, Wright's Law activates and costs plummet toward NOAK levels — benefiting every participant.

State RPS mandates consume the lion's share of buildable clean energy capacity in constrained ISOs. The pipeline available for voluntary corporate procurement — and therefore the feasibility of reaching Wright's Law critical mass — is regionally gated. What matters isn't total renewables capacity; it's what's left after compliance obligations.

Technology Learning Curves: FOAK to NOAK

Wright's Law cost trajectories for CCS-CCGT and advanced nuclear, gated by critical mass. Below the first-doubling threshold (8 GW CCS, 5 GW nuclear), costs stay at FOAK levels. After critical mass, they follow the standard learning curve toward NOAK pricing.

The doubling threshold matters. CCS-CCGT needs ~8 GW of global deployment to trigger its learning curve (down from $--/MWh to $--/MWh). Nuclear needs ~5 GW (down from $--/MWh to $--/MWh). At 25% C&I participation targeting 90% CFE, both thresholds are crossed.

Critical Mass: Participation Required by CFE Threshold

Higher CFE targets require more clean firm generation per participant, meaning fewer participants are needed to collectively cross the first-doubling threshold. At 99% CFE, just 5% participation triggers learning. At 90% CFE, it takes 25%.

Ambitious targets are self-reinforcing. The companies that set the highest CFE targets contribute the most new-build capacity per MWh of demand — reaching critical mass faster and unlocking cost declines for everyone, including latecomers at lower thresholds.

Synthesis

The economic case for clean firm power in two frames.

The stranding risk is already here

145 GW of gas backup capacity across 7 ISOs at 90% clean energy. This incumbent fleet earns diminishing returns as clean targets rise. Without clean firm power, this gas dependency is unavoidable — a permanent annual cost on every region's ratepayers.

145 GW gas backup · Diminishing utilization · Permanent cost

The FOAK premium buys a permanent cost decline

Lazard (2025) puts unsubsidized new nuclear at $141–220/MWh — consistent with Vogtle ($15,000/kW) and NuScale ($20,000/kW) experience. But DOE’s Liftoff report targets $3,600/kW after 5–10 deployments per design; MIT (2024) models AP1000 NOAK at $66/MWh unsubsidized. NREL ATB 2024 applies 8–9.5% learning rates per doubling. Geothermal is moving even faster — Fervo cut drilling costs 50% in 3 years. CCS follows suit: NETL documents 3–5% learning per doubling, with DOE OCED projecting 25% CAPEX reduction to NOAK. The gas cost it displaces never declines.
