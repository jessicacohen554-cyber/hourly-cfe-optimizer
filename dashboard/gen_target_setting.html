SBTi Framework

SBTi Power Sector v2: A Leading Framework for Corporate Climate Targets

The Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi) is the most widely adopted single-pathway framework for corporate climate target-setting. Its Power Sector v2 guidance, updated in September 2025, defines one approach for electricity generators to align with limiting warming to 1.5°C. While highly ambitious, the framework's uniform convergence approach raises important questions about whether a singular metric can adequately capture the complexity of decarbonization across diverse fleet compositions, regional constraints, and economic realities.

Core Requirements

  • Absolute contraction approach based on the remaining global carbon budget — company-level intensity pathways derived from sector-wide allocation
  • Near-term targets (5–10 years) and long-term targets (net-zero) both required
  • Offsets and unbundled RECs cannot count toward near-term targets — actual generation changes required
  • Shift from "renewable" to "zero-carbon energy" — nuclear now explicitly counts under v2
  • Full value chain coverage: Scope 1 + Scope 2 + relevant Scope 3 emissions
  • CCS at facility level may count if independently verified, but the framework does not validate specific technologies

Key Milestones

SBTi 2030 benchmark: ~118 kg CO₂/MWh (power sector specific). The pathway requires approximately 85% intensity reduction by 2035 from a 2020 baseline, with net-zero by 2040 — a full decade ahead of most other sectors.

IEA NZE Alignment

OECD power sector must reach net-zero by 2035. Global power net-zero by 2040. The SBTi pathway is calibrated to these benchmarks, requiring the fastest decarbonization of any sector.

Phased Hourly Matching

v2 introduces phased hourly matching requirements: 50% by 2030, 90% by 2040 for qualifying companies (≥10 GWh in a single region). This moves beyond annual RECs toward temporal granularity.

Limitations of a Single-Pathway Approach

  • Prescriptive pathway may not account for starting fleet composition — a nuclear-led generator at 180 kg/MWh faces the same percentage requirement as a coal-heavy generator at 500 kg/MWh
  • Steep marginal costs for already-clean generators — remaining emissions come from efficient gas CCGTs with marginal abatement costs of $88–165/tCO₂, compared to $17–28/tCO₂ for coal-to-wind substitution
  • No credit for system-wide decarbonization — providing clean baseload or enabling grid reliability doesn't count if your own intensity target isn't met
  • Binary pass/fail — no partial credit for meaningful progress that falls short of the benchmark
  • Economic competitiveness tension — emissions reduction ceilings set without regard to market dynamics can undercut the long-term viability of companies that are actively investing in clean energy innovation
The deeper question: Alignment with a single linear convergence pathway is not the only way to measure climate ambition. Power companies should be evaluated on the composition of their current fleets and where they take them next — catalyzing innovation toward reliable, affordable, and clean grids while remaining competitive and delivering long-term shareholder value. Decarbonization unfolds within a system-wide lens of highly complex supply, demand, policy, and economy-wide dynamics — not a singular metric. SBTi can be a valid approach under the right fleet conditions and policy incentives, but the sector needs more sophisticated ways of thinking about and approaching decarbonization that enable practical, climate-backed decision-making with strategic business sense.

SMARTargets

EPRI SMARTargets: Company-Specific, Scenario-Based Target Setting

EPRI's SMARTargets (Science-based, Multi-objective, Actionable, Risk-informed Targets) methodology, announced at COP28 (December 2023) and published as a 107-page framework in July 2025, was designed specifically for the power sector to address limitations of generic cross-sector frameworks like SBTi.

The core insight: the same emissions reduction level may be achievable by one company but not another due to differences in fleet composition, regional grid characteristics, available technology, market structure, and regulatory context.

Two Types of Targets

Aspirational Targets (ATs)

Generic Paris-aligned reduction levels that represent what the power sector needs to achieve for 1.5°C alignment. Not company-specific — derived from integrated energy models (NEMS, ReEDS, GenX).

AT Milestones

-56% by 2030 • -82% by 2035 • -88% by 2040 • -94% by 2045 • Net-zero by 2050

Qualified Targets (QTs)

Company-specific best-case achievable targets considering real-world constraints. Represent what's achievable with full enabling conditions — but no miracles.

QTs Account For

Starting fleet • Regional grid • Technology access • Market structure • Affordability

The 8-Step SMARTargets Process

  1. Characterize company fleet — Assemble verified GHG inventory and base year emissions profile
  2. Define scenario dimensions — Carbon price, technology cost, demand growth, policy landscape
  3. Set aspirational targets — Select Paris-aligned ATs from standardized reference trajectories
  4. Model fleet transformation — Run transition risk analysis under each scenario combination (the core analytical engine)
  5. Identify convergence zones — Find scenarios where outcomes cluster and extract robust strategies
  6. Extract qualified target — Set QT as the best achievable outcome given company-specific constraints
  7. Compare QT to AT and identify enabling conditions — Document the gap and what policy/market changes would close it
  8. Monitor and adjust — Periodic reassessment as conditions evolve
Step 4 is where the science happens. EPRI recommends using US-REGEN, a detailed regional economy-wide model. Our enhanced approach (Section 8) replaces this with 8,760-hour dispatch modeling from the CFE optimizer for higher temporal resolution.

Advantages Over SBTi for IPPs

Company-Specific

Recognizes that a nuclear-led IPP faces fundamentally different challenges than a coal-heavy utility. A QT for Constellation looks nothing like a QT for Evergy — and it shouldn't.

Scenario-Based

Models real-world uncertainty through multiple scenarios rather than prescribing a single pathway. Produces P10/P50/P90 outcome distributions instead of pass/fail.

Identifies Enabling Conditions

Explicitly maps what policy, market, and technology changes are needed to close the AT-QT gap. Turns abstract targets into concrete action requirements.

Constructive, Not Punitive

Shows what's possible vs. what's mandated. Companies can demonstrate ambition even when full Paris alignment requires conditions outside their control.

Challenges

  • Less widely recognized than SBTi — ~12 major US utilities vs. ~40 power companies globally with SBTi targets
  • Complexity — requires detailed company-specific modeling for each participant, not a standardized lookup table
  • Risk of "gaming" — companies could set easy QTs; critics (Ceres, NYC Comptroller) warn of potential greenwashing
  • No external validation process — unlike SBTi's third-party review, SMARTargets relies on self-assessment with EPRI framework guidance
The credibility debate: Ceres (representing $45T+ in AUM) has cautioned that "industry-led frameworks risk weakening climate ambition." NYC Comptroller Brad Lander warned SMARTargets could enable "greenwashing at scale." EPRI counters that SBTi's one-size-fits-all approach ignores practical reality — a Southeast utility with weak renewable resources faces fundamentally different constraints than a Pacific Northwest hydro utility.

Where they diverge: Both aim for near-complete decarbonization, but differ fundamentally in how they define the path. The question isn't which framework sets higher ceilings, but which approach produces the most practical, investable, and ultimately effective decarbonization outcomes.
Emissions Accounting

GHG Protocol Scope 2: How Accounting Rules Shape Clean Energy Markets

The GHG Protocol's Scope 2 guidance defines how organizations account for electricity-related emissions. For IPPs, this framework doesn't directly apply (they generate, not consume), but it profoundly shapes the market for their product.

Two Accounting Methods

Location-Based

Uses the grid-average emission factor for the consumer's location. Reflects what actually happens on the physical grid. A company in PJM uses PJM's average intensity regardless of contracts.

Strength: physical reality. Weakness: no credit for clean energy procurement.

Market-Based

Uses contractual instruments (RECs, PPAs, green tariffs) to assign emission factors. A company with 100% renewable PPAs can claim zero Scope 2 even on a coal-heavy grid.

Strength: rewards procurement choices. Weakness: can decouple from physical grid impact.

Why This Matters for IPPs

  • IPPs don't have Scope 2 in the traditional sense — they generate electricity, not consume it. Their emissions are Scope 1 (direct combustion) and customers' Scope 2.
  • Customer Scope 2 accounting drives demand — market-based accounting is the engine behind corporate PPA demand. When Microsoft, Google, or Meta set Scope 2 targets, they need to buy clean energy from IPPs.
  • Hourly vs. annual matching — the frontier of Scope 2 accounting is moving from annual REC-based matching to hourly temporal matching. This directly connects to our CFE optimizer work — IPPs that can provide clean energy with temporal granularity have a competitive advantage.
  • Market-based accounting creates premium opportunities — IPPs with time-matched, location-specific clean generation can charge PPA premiums over unbundled RECs.
The dual reporting requirement creates both opportunity and confusion. Companies must report both location-based and market-based Scope 2 numbers. When these diverge significantly — a company claims zero market-based Scope 2 while location-based shows 400 kg/MWh — it raises questions about additionality and real-world impact. The push toward hourly matching and EACs (Energy Attribute Certificates) is designed to close this gap.

GHG Protocol Updates

The GHG Protocol is currently undergoing revisions that may tighten market-based accounting rules. Key proposals under consideration include requiring temporal matching (beyond annual), geographic proximity requirements for RECs, and additionality criteria for market-based claims. These changes would significantly increase the value of temporally-matched clean energy — exactly the product that our modeling helps IPPs optimize.

Other Frameworks

The Broader Landscape: Additional Decarbonization Frameworks

IPCC AR6 (1.5°C)

Power sector CO₂ must fall ~48% by 2030 and ~99% by 2050 vs. 2019 (1.5°C, no/limited overshoot). Climate Action Tracker benchmark: 48–80 gCO₂/kWh by 2030. Developed countries reach net-zero electricity by 2035. Coal without CCS reduced 67–82% by 2030.

IEA Net Zero by 2050

Power sector emissions decline ~60% by 2030 from 2019. OECD achieves 100% clean power by 2035. Global power sector reaches net-zero by 2040. 2025 update acknowledges slower near-term progress; temperature peaks ~1.65°C around 2050.

Climate Action 100+

World's largest investor engagement initiative ($60T+ AUM). Net-zero in generation by 2040 globally, 2035 in advanced economies. 50%+ decarbonization by 2030. Explicitly says avoid carbon offsets and minimize CCUS reliance.

24/7 Carbon-Free Energy

Emerging standard pioneered by Google/Microsoft. Goes beyond annual RECs to require hourly matching of consumption with clean generation. Directly relevant to utility-scale procurement. This is the methodology used in our hourly CFE optimizer.

Our Approach

Enhanced Modeling: Building on SMARTargets with 8,760-Hour Dispatch

Neither a singular convergence pathway nor a purely qualitative assessment can capture the full complexity of power sector decarbonization. Our analysis adopts the SMARTargets company-specific, scenario-based approach as its foundation — then enhances it with hourly dispatch modeling, co-optimized resource mixes, and multi-ISO fleet analysis. The goal: evaluate generators on where their fleets are today and the concrete steps they're taking to catalyze innovation toward reliable, affordable, and clean grids — within the system-wide context of supply, demand, policy, and economy-wide dynamics that actually govern the transition.

Key Enhancements Over Standard SMARTargets

1. Hourly CFE Optimizer Integration

Instead of simplified capacity planning, we use 8,760-hour dispatch modeling from the CFE optimizer to determine actual hourly matching and resource requirements. Every hour of the year is modeled explicitly — capturing seasonal patterns, diurnal cycles, and weather variability.

2. Co-Optimized Resource Mixes

Resources aren't selected independently. We co-optimize cost and clean energy fraction across 5,832 sensitivity scenarios per ISO (17,496 for CAISO). Different cost assumptions produce different optimal mixes — cost drives resource selection.

3. Multi-ISO Fleet Modeling

For IPPs operating across multiple ISOs, we model each ISO's grid characteristics independently — generation profiles, demand curves, existing fleet — and aggregate at the company level. No averaging across regions.

4. Dynamic Fossil Dispatch

Coal and gas retirement is modeled through dispatch dynamics, not simple capacity removal. Renewables + storage reduce fossil dispatch hours before physical retirement occurs — reflecting how economics drive the transition.

5. 270-Scenario Parametric Sweep

Each company evaluated under 270 scenarios (5 dimensions × multiple levels) producing P10/P50/P90 outcome distributions. Not a single "best guess" but a full probability distribution of achievable outcomes.

6. Enabling Conditions Quantification

Each scenario maps to specific policy conditions — carbon price level, IRA availability, technology cost trajectory, transmission build rate. This allows precise identification of what's needed to close the AT-QT gap.
