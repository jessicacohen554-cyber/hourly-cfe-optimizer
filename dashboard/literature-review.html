Section 1

The Accounting Revolution: From Annual RECs to Hourly Matching

The intellectual foundation for hourly clean energy matching begins with the GHG Protocol Scope 2 Guidance (WRI, 2015) [1], which established dual reporting: a location-based method using average grid emission factors and a market-based method using contractual instruments such as Renewable Energy Certificates. This framework enabled the modern voluntary clean energy market but also created a structural loophole.

The inadequacy of annual renewable energy certificates for verifying actual grid decarbonization has been widely documented. Xu et al. (2024) demonstrate that annual matching can overstate clean energy coverage by 15–40% in regions with high solar penetration, because midday surplus certificates mask nighttime fossil consumption [2]. Bjørn, Lloyd, Brander, and Matthews (2022) showed the consequences at scale: across 115 companies with science-based targets, the widespread use of RECs led to an inflated estimate of mitigation effectiveness — companies reported a 31% reduction in scope 2 emissions from 2015–2019, but stripping out REC-based claims revealed an actual reduction of only ~10%. If this trend continued, 42% of committed scope 2 reductions would not result in real-world mitigation [3]. This landmark paper directly catalyzed the ongoing GHG Protocol Scope 2 revision.

The proposed Scope 2 revision (GHG Protocol, Standard Development Plan, December 2024; Phase 1 public consultation launched October 2025, closing January 2026) represents the most consequential shift in corporate carbon accounting since 2015. The revision proposes hourly matching requirements for market-based instruments, geographic deliverability constraints, and residual mix reform using fossil-only emission factors where residual data is unavailable. The timeline targets a revised standard by 2027 [4].

Brander (2022) and Brander and Bjørn (2023) have articulated the theoretical underpinning: attributional methods allocate emissions within a boundary and can be summed to equal total global emissions, while consequential methods quantify system-wide changes caused by decisions. Mixing the two, as the current market-based method does, produces "incoherent results" [5] [6]. Bjørn et al. (2024, Nature Communications) further demonstrated that buyer-level marginal claims can sum to 2–3× actual grid reductions [7].

This debate has organized two camps. The hourly matching camp, led by Google, EnergyTag, and the Climate Group's 24/7 Carbon-Free Coalition (launched September 2024), prioritizes time- and location-matched energy procurement. The carbon matching camp, led by the Emissions First Partnership (founded 2022; members include Amazon, Meta, Intel, Salesforce, General Motors), uses marginal operating emission rates (MOERs) to maximize total emissions reductions regardless of location.

Google's 24/7 CFE program, the largest corporate hourly matching initiative, reported achieving 64% average hourly CFE across its global operations in 2022 (rising to 66% in 2024), with significant regional variation [8]. Their published methodology has become a de facto industry standard. The UN 24/7 CFE Compact, launched at COP26, provides a multilateral framework for scaling hourly matching globally [9].

WattTime provides the foundational data for marginal emissions analysis. Building on Siler-Evans, Azevedo, and Morgan (2012) — which established that average emission factors can grossly misestimate avoided emissions [10] — WattTime now produces MOERs every 5 minutes across 210 countries. The VERACI-T validation working group (2023) compared six MEF models across US ISOs, finding WattTime (−1.3% average error) and Siler-Evans (−2.7%) significantly more accurate than alternatives [11].

Alignment: The site's 10-strategy procurement comparison framework — comprising 3 consequential, 3 hourly, and 4 annual matching strategies — directly operationalizes this accounting debate. By modeling all approaches simultaneously across identical scenarios, the site enables empirical comparison of outcomes rather than theoretical argument. This multi-strategy comparison is unique in the literature.
Section 2

Granular Certificates and Institutional Infrastructure

EnergyTag, the independent nonprofit, published its Granular Certificate (GC) Scheme Standard V2 in December 2024 and Matching Standard V1 in March 2024. GCs represent energy production during intervals of one hour or less and can be implemented through three configurations: direct issuance by local EAC bodies (Config 1), independent GC issuance alongside existing EAC systems (Config 2), or conversion of monthly EACs using hourly meter data (Config 3, most widely available today). In June 2025, EnergyTag accredited its first two GC schemes — Energinet (Denmark's TSO) and a second scheme — marking a milestone for operational granular certification [12].

Google's portfolio conversion demonstrates feasibility at scale: Flexidao converted Google's global portfolio to hourly format using Config 3, covering 10.5 TWh across 7 countries and 100+ sites in 2025. In the US, registries including PJM GATs, M-RETS, and NEPOOL are developing GC capability.

The SBTi Corporate Net-Zero Standard V2.0 (second consultation draft, November 2025) codifies hourly matching into corporate target-setting: companies must achieve 100% low-carbon electricity by 2040, with hourly matching required starting 2030. Geographic matching within the same grid region is mandatory, and additionality requires facilities commissioned within 10 years (shifting to 5 years by 2035) [13]. The separate SBTi Power Sector Net-Zero Standard (first draft, September 2025) sets a net-zero target year of 2040 for power companies [14].

EPRI's SMARTargets framework (announced COP28 December 2023; public consultation closed August 2025) offers a contrasting approach — company-specific "Qualified Targets" reflecting unique transition constraints alongside "Aspirational Targets" from global 1.5°C pathways [15]. Ceres (August 2025) criticized the approach as potentially allowing less ambitious targets [16].

Alignment: The site's ISO-specific analysis across 7 US regions reflects the SMARTargets philosophy that company-specific context matters. Divergence: The site goes beyond both SBTi's phased requirements and EPRI's qualitative framework by quantifying exact cost curves for every matching threshold from 90% to 100% in 2.5% increments.
Section 3

The Princeton Finding That Annual Matching Fails

The most consequential finding in the hourly matching literature comes from Princeton's ZERO Lab. Xu, Ricks, Manocha, Patankar, and Jenkins (2024, Joule) enhanced GenX to compare three voluntary procurement strategies — annual matching, temporal (hourly) matching, and emissions matching — across US regions. Their central finding: annual/volumetric matching produces "zero or near-zero" system-level CO₂ reductions in the long run, because wind and solar procurement largely displaces other clean energy rather than fossil fuels. Only temporal (hourly) matching consistently lowered system-wide emissions [17]. In a separate analysis, Xu et al. showed that when both RPS compliance and voluntary C&I demand draw on the same regional supply curve, costs escalate non-linearly as combined demand approaches the buildable capacity frontier [18].

Riepin and Brown (2024, Energy Strategy Reviews) extended this to Europe using PyPSA: a 98% CFE target costs 54% more than annual matching, and the last 2% (98% → 100%) more than doubles costs [19]. In a 2025 Joule commentary, Riepin, Jenkins, Swezey, and Brown demonstrated that 24/7 CFE matching accelerates technology learning curves [20].

RMI's "Clean Power by the Hour" (Dyson, Shah, and Teplin, July 2021) assessed hourly matching across 7 markets and identified a three-stage cost escalation: stable costs to 30–80% hourly matching, significant increase to ~85%, then sharp escalation above 85% [21]. The IEA's analysis further validates this three-phase pattern [22].

TransitionZero (2025, series funded by Google.org) produced the most geographically diverse 24/7 CFE modeling, studying India, Japan, Singapore, and Malaysia. The cross-cutting finding — that moderate hourly matching (70–90%) can be cheaper than annual matching at system level — challenges the assumption that hourly matching necessarily costs more [23].

The "last few percent" phenomenon is well-documented: Denholm et al. (2021) show that costs increase non-linearly as clean electricity targets approach 100% [24]. Gillenwater (2008) provides the foundational supply-demand framework for voluntary REC markets [25]. O'Shaughnessy et al. (2025) document 319 million MWh in voluntary procurement (44% of non-hydro renewable sales), with long-term PPAs as the primary channel through which corporate demand signals new capacity [26].

Extension: The 2.5% threshold granularity in the 90–100% inflection zone is finer than any published study, providing practitioners the precision needed for target-setting in the most cost-sensitive zone. The scarcity model operationalizes these supply-demand insights: RPS-mandated build consumes the cheapest tiers of the regional supply stack first, with voluntary corporate demand riding on top.
Section 4

Clean Firm Power: The Technologies That Close the Hourly Gap

The economic case for clean firm power is anchored by Sepulveda, Jenkins, de Sisternes, and Lester (2018, Joule), who evaluated nearly 1,000 cases and found that firm low-carbon technologies reduce electricity costs by 10–62% across fully decarbonized scenarios [27]. Long et al. (2021) — the landmark EDF/CATF California study — found portfolios with clean firm power 32–53% cheaper than renewables-and-batteries-only pathways [28].

The Princeton Net-Zero America study (Larson et al., 2021) identifies firm, dispatchable clean generation as essential for cost-effective deep decarbonization [29]. Jenkins et al. (2018) demonstrate that removing firm low-carbon resources increases system costs by 10–62% [30]. Spokas and Ricks (CATF, February 2026) concluded that diversified systems with clean firm generation are "significantly less expensive (often by tens of percent)" than variable-renewables-only systems [31].

Nuclear Cost Trajectories

Nuclear power, the largest existing source of firm clean generation, faces economic pressure from cheap natural gas and subsidized renewables. Existing fleet operating costs of $28–35/MWh remain competitive [32]. The DOE "Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Advanced Nuclear" (September 2024) projects NOAK cost targets of $3,600/kW with a committed orderbook of 5–10 deployments [33]. MIT estimated AP1000 NOAK at ~$4,625/kW with unsubsidized LCOE of $66/MWh [34]. INL identified pathways achieving 45–60% cost reductions between first and third plants [35].

The NREL ATB 2024 nuclear module models learning rates of 8% (large reactor) and 9.5% (SMR) per doubling of cumulative capacity [36] [37], though historical US/Western experience shows "negative learning." NuScale's UAMPS project saw costs escalate 75% before cancellation [38]. Vogtle Units 3&4 cost $30–36.8 billion versus the original $14 billion estimate [39].

Learning Curves and Wright's Law

Wright's Law (1936) posits that unit costs decline by a fixed percentage with each doubling of cumulative production [42]. Bolinger, Wiser, and O'Shaughnessy (2022, iScience) confirmed: solar PV exhibits a 24% learning rate per doubling and onshore wind 15% [43]. Way et al. (Oxford) demonstrated Wright's Law has been the most accurate predictor of solar cost trajectories [44].

Current Cost Benchmarks

NREL ATB 2024 reports utility-scale solar at $24–42/MWh and onshore wind at $26–50/MWh, with 4-hour Li-ion storage at $115–165/MWh LCOS [41]. Lazard LCOE+ v18.0 (June 2025) provides broader benchmarks: solar $38–78/MWh, wind $37–86/MWh, gas CC $48–109/MWh, nuclear $141–220/MWh [45]. Regional PPA variation is substantial: LBNL documents solar from $30–40/MWh (Southwest) to $60–90/MWh (Northeast) [46], and wind from $20–35/MWh (Great Plains) to $55–80/MWh (New England) [47].

GridLab/EFG/Halcyon (September 2025) revealed CCGT projects routinely exceeding $2,000/kW — while EIA AEO assumes only $921/kW [48].

Enhanced Geothermal

Fervo Energy's Cape Station targets 100 MW online by October 2026 and 500 MW by 2028, with drilling times fallen 70% year-over-year. DOE targets EGS LCOE of $45/MWh by 2035 [49].

Extension: The critical mass analysis for Wright's Law learning curve activation is novel — no published study quantifies how many corporate procurement decisions are needed to trigger specific learning rate milestones for advanced nuclear or enhanced geothermal at the ISO level.
Section 5

Why LCOE Misleads: System Cost, Cannibalization, and Value Deflation

Joskow (2011, American Economic Review) established the foundational critique: LCOE is "seriously flawed" because it treats all MWh as homogeneous [50]. Hirth (2013) quantified the consequence: wind value factors drop to ~0.7 at 30% market share and solar to ~0.7 at only 10–15% penetration [51]. Hirth, Ueckerdt, and Edenhofer (2015) decomposed integration costs into profile costs (~25–35 €/MWh at 30–40% wind), balancing costs (~2–4 €/MWh), and grid costs [52]. Ueckerdt et al. (2013) formalized System LCOE = Generation LCOE + Integration Costs [53].

The empirical reality of cannibalization is now starkly visible. López Prol et al. (2020) documented both absolute and relative cannibalization in CAISO [54]. By 2024, solar capture rates in CAISO's SP15 zone had plummeted to less than 30%. Negative pricing doubled from ~530 hours in 2023 to ~1,180 hours in 2024 (~13% of all hours) [55]. CAISO data shows over 2.4 TWh of renewable curtailment in 2023 [56].

Extension: The stranded investment analysis — comparing interim vs. long-term target procurement decisions — is not found in the academic capacity expansion literature, which typically optimizes for a single future year.
Section 6

The Last Mile: Storage Economics, Grid Resilience, and Resource Adequacy

Battery storage enables temporal shifting of clean energy. NREL ATB 2024 reports 4-hour Li-ion round-trip efficiency of 85% [41], with installed costs declining from $380/kWh in 2020 to ~$250/kWh in 2024 [58]. BloombergNEF reports pack prices at $108/kWh in 2025 (down 93% since 2010) [59]. The EIA reports 16 GW of utility-scale storage installed as of 2024 [60].

However, 4-hour storage cannot address multi-day deficits. Dowling et al. (2020) show required storage duration scales nonlinearly with matching targets [61]. Cole et al. (2021) demonstrated costs increase nonlinearly for the last few percent [62]. Mai et al. (2022) concluded no single strategy is sufficient for the last 10% [63].

Long-Duration Energy Storage

Form Energy's iron-air battery targets <$20/kWh for 100+ hours [64]. The DOE LDES Shot targets 90% cost reduction [65]. DOE Liftoff for LDES estimates $10–20 billion/year savings in net-zero pathways [66]. LDES Council/McKinsey concluded LDES is most competitive beyond 6–8 hours [67].

Resource Adequacy and Dunkelflaute

PJM adopted marginal ELCC (FERC-approved January 2024); solar incremental capacity credit in CAISO expected to drop to 6% by 2026 [68]. Kittel et al. (2024) quantified 2–10 Dunkelflaute events per year in northern Europe [69].

Extension: The MAC vs. DAC crossover analysis with "no-regrets" resource identification represents a novel decision framework not found in the storage or grid resilience literature.
Section 7

The Interconnection Bottleneck and Market Design Challenges

Berkeley Lab's "Queued Up" (Rand et al., 2025) documents ~2,300 GW of capacity (10,300 projects) seeking grid connection. Only 13% from 2000–2019 reached commercial operations; 77% was withdrawn. Median wait times have doubled to over 4 years [75].

FERC Order No. 2023 (July 2023) replaced first-come-first-served with cluster-based studies [76]. Lo Prete, Palmer, and Robertson (RFF, June 2025) reviewed 11 proposed market designs and concluded existing designs are poorly suited for the evolving resource mix [77].

PJM's July 2024 capacity auction cleared at $269.92/MW-day, a nearly 10-fold increase [78]. The "missing money" problem is worsening — Ela et al. (NREL, 2016) found net revenues insufficient to justify new thermal/nuclear investment [79]. Hogan (2017) argued out-of-market capacity mechanisms create "misallocated money" [80].

The DOE National Transmission Needs Study (October 2023) concluded interregional capacity must grow more than fivefold [81].

Extension: No prior study systematically compares procurement optimization outcomes across all 7 ISOs simultaneously, or models how inter-ISO differences in market design affect optimal resource mix selection for hourly matching.
Section 8

Corporate Procurement Enters the Firm Power Era

BloombergNEF data shows corporations signed ~62 GW of clean power PPAs in 2024 before declining to 55.9 GW in 2025 — the first drop in nearly a decade. Critically, 5.2 GW of 2025 deals were "baseload-like" products — a structural shift [83].

The nuclear deals are unprecedented: Microsoft signed a $1.6 billion PPA with Constellation for TMI Unit 1 restart (835 MW) [84]. Google signed the first corporate SMR fleet deal with Kairos Power (up to 500 MW) [85]. Amazon is pursuing nearly 2 GW from Talen Energy's Susquehanna [86]. Meta signed 1.1 GW with Constellation's Clinton plant [87]. Combined, Big Tech contracted for 10+ GW of possible new nuclear in 2024–2025.

IRA Section 45V (final regulations January 2025) established hourly matching required starting January 1, 2030 for hydrogen production tax credits — the first federal hourly matching mandate [88].

Section 9

Relationship to Capacity Expansion Models

The dominant tools for grid planning — GenX [91], ReEDS [92], SWITCH [93], and PyPSA [94] — are capacity expansion (CE) models. They solve a unified optimization: given cost assumptions, find the least-cost portfolio subject to reliability and policy constraints. They are powerful system-planning instruments. This optimizer is not one of them, and the distinction is deliberate.

CE models answer a system planner's question: "What should the grid build?" They co-optimize capacity investment and operational dispatch, typically compressing 8,760 hours into 50–200 representative periods [95].

This optimizer answers a procurement strategist's question: "What does it cost to match my load cleanly at every hour, and what drives that cost?"

Full Temporal Resolution Without Compression

Hourly CFE matching is inherently a tail-risk problem. The hours that matter most — winter evening peaks, multi-day wind droughts — are precisely the hours representative-period algorithms underweight. A portfolio scoring 95% CFE on representative days may score 88% on the actual 8,760-hour profile. This optimizer simulates every hour.

Physics-First Feasibility, Then Economic Evaluation

CE models couple physics and economics into a single objective, producing one optimal point per scenario. This optimizer inverts the sequence: Step 1 exhaustively enumerates the Physics Feasible Space (PFS) — all resource mixes capable of achieving each target — without reference to cost. Step 3 evaluates every feasible mix under thousands of cost scenarios. Because physics and economics are decoupled, changing cost assumptions requires only re-evaluation (minutes), not re-solving dispatch (hours).

The full PFS reveals that multiple qualitatively different portfolios can achieve the same matching target. A CE model returns whichever is cheaper under assumed inputs; this optimizer returns both and maps their relative economics across the entire sensitivity space.

Parametric Sensitivity at Marginal Cost

The cached PFS of ~21.4 million physics-validated mixes enables exhaustive parametric sensitivity across 5,832 cost scenarios per region and threshold (17,496 for CAISO). This enables ANOVA-style sensitivity decomposition — identifying which cost toggles dominate variance — which would be impractical in a CE framework.

What This Approach Does Not Do

The tradeoffs are real. This optimizer does not model unit commitment, spinning reserves, or ramp-rate constraints. It treats each ISO as a single node. Storage dispatch follows a deterministic priority rule rather than being co-optimized per cost scenario. CO₂ emission rates are static annual averages per ISO (sourced from EPA eGRID). The deployment queue is an economic ordering, not a representation of FERC interconnection queue position or permitting timelines.

These are appropriate simplifications for the question being asked. The two approaches are complementary: CE models generate the technology cost projections that inform this optimizer's inputs; this optimizer applies them to hourly load matching at a granularity CE models are not designed to achieve.

Section 10

Novel Contributions of The 8,760 Problem

Across all research domains reviewed, the following nine contributions are not found in any prior published work:

  1. Co-optimization across 5,832 sensitivity scenarios per ISO — The parametric breadth enables ANOVA-style sensitivity decomposition identifying which cost toggles dominate variance at each threshold and region.
  2. The four-pool supply model (SSS / Corporate-Contracted / Merchant / New-Build) — operationalizes the distinction between existing and new clean resources to reflect actual procurement market structure.
  3. The 10-strategy procurement comparison framework (3 consequential, 3 hourly, 4 annual) — the most comprehensive side-by-side evaluation documented to date.
  4. Critical mass analysis for Wright's Law learning curve activation — quantifies how many corporate procurement decisions trigger specific learning rate milestones at the ISO level.
  5. ~33,000 physics-feasible resource mixes per ISO (21.4 million total) — exhaustive enumeration of the Physics Feasible Space exposing structure that single-objective optimization conceals.
  6. Stranded investment analysis — interim vs. long-term target comparison not found in capacity expansion literature.
  7. Synthetic LMP model (150,000+ scenarios per ISO) — extends beyond existing temporal mismatch analysis to comprehensive price distributions.
  8. MAC vs. DAC crossover analysis — identifies where hourly matching exceeds direct air capture costs, with "no-regrets" resource identification.
  9. 2.5% threshold granularity in the 90–100% inflection zone — finer than any published study, providing precision for target-setting in the most cost-sensitive zone.
