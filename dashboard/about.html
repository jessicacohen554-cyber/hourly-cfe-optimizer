Research Gaps

What Existing Research Misses

Most clean energy procurement research treats the buyer as a passive accounting entity: offset consumption with certificates, report the result. This project treats procurement as an active systems problem, connecting hourly demand to grid physics, cost uncertainty, and regional generation fleets. Five gaps in existing work motivate the analysis.

Gap 1: Cost is treated as fixed, not as a variable

Most hourly matching studies assume a single cost for each technology. In practice, solar LCOE ranges from $40 to $120/MWh depending on region and assumptions. This project co-optimizes the resource mix under 5,832 cost scenarios per region, making cost uncertainty a first-class input. Different cost assumptions produce different optimal portfolios. What you need depends on what things cost.

Gap 2: Regional variation is underappreciated

A 90% hourly target in ERCOT (wind-rich, no RPS competition) requires a different portfolio than in PJM (nuclear fleet under data center pressure) or ISO-NE (transmission-constrained, $37–40/MWh Tier I RECs). National averages obscure the decisions buyers actually face. This project models seven ISOs independently using real 2025 hourly generation profiles and regional cost tables.

Gap 3: The “last 5%” cost inflection is under-studied

Moving from 75% to 90% hourly matching is relatively affordable. Moving from 95% to 99.9% can cost 5–10x more per marginal MWh. This inflection zone is where the most consequential procurement decisions occur, and this project covers it at 2.5% threshold granularity.

Gap 4: EAC scarcity is emerging but unquantified

PJM Tier I REC prices have hit $37–40/MWh, historic highs, before hourly matching mandates even take effect. This project quantifies the clean premium by ISO region, modeling how corporate participation rates at 10–50% penetration drive scarcity.

Gap 5: Policies aren’t evaluated against physical constraints

The 45Q CCS tax credit incentivizes gas plants to maximize runtime for capture credits, regardless of grid need. Annual REC standards create no demand signal for firm clean resources during the hardest hours. This project evaluates policy mechanisms against actual hourly grid physics, revealing where incentive structures diverge from decarbonization goals.

Framework

Analytical Framework

Hourly clean energy procurement operates across six overlapping layers: grid economics, generation fleets, corporate demand, policy, accounting rules, and climate targets. This project models their interactions to identify where procurement decisions have the greatest impact.

01

System-Level Grid Economics

The grid dispatches generators by marginal cost, cheapest first. Renewables push fossil plants off the margin during sunny and windy hours, but the last 5–20% of hours (evenings, winters, low-wind weeks) are the most expensive to decarbonize, creating a steep last-mile cost curve.

02

Power Generation & Fleet Decisions

Utilities and IPPs are deciding whether to retire coal, extend nuclear, or invest in CCS and long-duration storage. A region with 30 GW of nuclear (PJM) faces a very different transition than one dominated by gas (ERCOT) or reliant on hydro imports (NYISO).

03

Voluntary Corporate Buyers

Tech companies, data center operators, and Fortune 500 firms are the primary demand signal for new clean energy. Their procurement commitments (virtual PPAs, 24/7 CFE targets, hourly matching goals) shape which resources get built, where, and at what price.

04

State & National Policy

Tax credits (ITC, PTC, 45Q), clean energy mandates, and capacity market rules set the floor for deployment. Policies designed for annual compliance create different incentive structures than those designed for hourly matching.

05

Accounting & Reporting Frameworks

The GHG Protocol Scope 2 revision (expected ~2027) and SBTi power sector pathways are moving toward hourly targets. These frameworks define what counts as "clean" and determine whether corporate spending drives real emissions reductions or redistributes paper credits.

06

Global & National Goals

Paris Agreement targets, IEA Net Zero by 2050, and national clean electricity goals set the destination. The gap between those aspirations and the practical question of how to affordably match clean energy in every hour of 2025 is where this analysis operates.

Results

Key Findings

Connecting grid physics, cost uncertainty, and regional generation fleets in a single hourly model produces results that do not emerge from simpler approaches. Six findings stand out.

FINDING 01

Cost Drives the Optimal Resource Mix

Different cost assumptions produce different optimal portfolios, not just different prices. High renewable costs shift the mix toward nuclear; high firm costs shift toward solar plus storage. Across 16,200 independent co-optimizations, the cost scenario is as determinative as the target threshold.

FINDING 02

Last-Mile Costs Escalate Non-Linearly

Between 90% and 99.9% hourly matching, marginal costs rise steeply. The resources needed for the hardest hours (winter evenings, multi-day low-wind periods) are the most expensive and least utilized.

FINDING 03

Region Determines Strategy, Not Just Cost

ERCOT’s optimal portfolio differs from PJM’s in kind, not just in price. Wind-rich regions need less storage; nuclear-heavy regions need less new clean firm capacity.

FINDING 04

Existing Clean Assets Are Undervalued

Nuclear uprates, existing hydro, and operating geothermal provide the cheapest clean firm capacity. Data center PPAs and state mandates are consuming this supply, creating scarcity for voluntary corporate buyers.

FINDING 05

45Q Creates a Gas Plant Persistence Incentive

The $85/ton 45Q credit makes CCS-equipped gas plants economically incentivized to maximize runtime for capture credits, regardless of grid need. This model captures the effect by treating CCS as baseload.

FINDING 06

EAC Scarcity Is Already Emerging

Before hourly matching mandates take effect, REC prices in PJM and ISO-NE have hit historic highs. Modeling corporate participation at 10–50% penetration reveals clean premiums of $20–55/MWh in these regions.
