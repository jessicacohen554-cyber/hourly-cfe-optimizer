EPRI SMARTargets Scenario Analysis

Applying the SMARTargets transition risk framework to independent power producers

The SMARTargets Methodology

EPRI's framework for setting grounded, actionable climate targets aligned with the Paris Agreement

What is SMARTargets?

EPRI's SMARTargets (Science-based, Multi-objective, Actionable, Risk-informed Targets) is a methodology for companies to set GHG emissions reduction targets that are grounded in relevant science, actionable given company-specific opportunities and constraints, and aligned with the Paris Agreement goals of limiting warming to well below 2°C.

Unlike SBTi's sector-specific benchmarks, SMARTargets recognizes that the same emissions reduction level may be achievable by one company but not another due to differences in fleet composition, regional markets, technology access, and regulatory context. The methodology uses scenario-based transition risk analysis to explore the range of plausible futures and identify both aspirational targets and the conditions that would enable or constrain their achievement.

Two Types of Targets

  • Aspirational Targets (ATs) — Generic Paris-aligned reduction levels that all companies evaluate: -56% by 2030, -82% by 2035, -88% by 2040, -94% by 2045, and net-zero (-100%) by 2050, all from a 2015 base year.
  • Qualified Targets (QTs) — Company-specific targets reflecting the best a company can achieve given realistic conditions without enabling conditions being in place. QTs are always less than or equal to ATs, and the company must identify what enabling conditions would bridge the gap.

The 8-Step Process

  1. GHG Inventories & Base Year — Assemble verified emissions inventory
  2. Practical Issues & Target Groupings — Assess control, materiality, regulatory coverage
  3. Aspirational Targets — Select Paris-aligned ATs from standardized table
  4. Transition Risk Analysis — Run scenario modeling (the core analytical engine)
  5. Strategy & Qualified Target Alignment — Set QTs based on scenario results
  6. Documentation & Communication — Report targets, conditions, and strategies
  7. Validation & Verification — Internal and external review
  8. Monitoring & Adjusting — Periodic reassessment as conditions evolve
Step 4 is where the science happens. EPRI recommends using US-REGEN, a detailed regional economy-wide model that combines electricity capacity expansion, end-use technology choice, fuel markets, and emissions constraints to produce integrated transition pathways. The scenario results identify potential future states, robust strategies, enabling conditions, and risk management options.

The 12-Scenario Framework

A 3-dimensional matrix of decarbonization incentives, technology options, and external conditions

Scenario Design Concept: The 2×2 Matrix

SMARTargets uses a parsimonious scenario design built on three uncertainty dimensions:

  1. Decarbonization Incentive Scope — Power sector only vs. economy-wide net-zero by 2050. This determines whether electrification creates new demand for clean power (economy-wide) or the power sector decarbonizes in isolation.
  2. Decarbonization Options — Fuller vs. More Limited technology portfolios. Fuller includes CCS, new nuclear, CDR (DAC + BECCS), hydrogen, fossil capacity reserves. More Limited restricts to renewables, storage, existing nuclear, and electrolytic hydrogen only.
  3. Other Conditions — Facilitating vs. Challenging external factors. Facilitating means lower costs, easier permitting, reference load growth. Challenging means higher costs, constrained builds, data-center-driven load growth (+12% by 2030).

The required scenarios pair "best case" conditions (Fuller + Facilitating) with "worst case" conditions (More Limited + Challenging) for each incentive scope, plus reference scenarios without emissions constraints. The recommended scenarios fill in the cross-combinations.

The 12 Scenarios: Detailed Narratives

What each scenario represents as a "state of the world" and what REGEN modeling inputs would look like

R1 Reference — Facilitating Conditions
Required

State of the world: No new emissions policy beyond what's already enacted. Current IRA incentives, state RPS mandates, and EPA regulations remain in place but nothing new is added. Technology costs fall faster than expected, permitting is streamlined, and load grows at historical rates. This is the "everything goes right but nobody mandates deeper cuts" future.

This scenario establishes the lower bound of what happens without policy intervention. REGEN results typically show 25-40% emissions reductions by 2050 from market forces alone — coal retirement driven by economics, gas CCGT expansion, and continued renewable buildout under IRA credits.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • No CO₂ emissions constraint imposed
  • Current federal + state policies only (IRA PTCs/ITCs, state RPS)
  • Reference load growth (~1% annually from population/GDP)
  • Default technology cost projections with reference learning curves
  • No constraints on economic capacity or transmission additions
  • Equilibrium fossil fuel prices (no supply disruptions)
R2 Reference — Challenging Conditions
Required

State of the world: Same as R1 (no new emissions policy) but the external environment is hostile to the energy transition. Data center / AI demand surges electricity consumption. Supply chain constraints slow renewable deployment. Permitting reform stalls. Fossil fuel prices spike from geopolitical disruptions. This is the "headwinds but no mandates" future.

Establishes the reference emissions floor under adverse conditions. May show modest reductions (15-25%) or even emissions increases if load growth outpaces renewable deployment.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • No CO₂ emissions constraint imposed
  • Current federal + state policies only
  • Faster load growth (+12% by 2030 from data centers; ~2% annually thereafter)
  • Higher technology cost projections; slower battery learning curves
  • Capacity additions constrained to historical build rates
  • Transmission additions constrained to historical build rates
  • Higher fossil fuel prices (supply constraints, pipeline limits)
S1 Power Sector NZ 2050 / Fuller Options / Facilitating
Required

State of the world: The best-case decarbonization scenario for power-sector-only policy. A federal or regional clean electricity standard mandates net-zero power by 2050, but other sectors (transport, industry, buildings) are not directly constrained. All technologies are available: CCS gets permitted, new nuclear clears NRC review, DAC scales commercially. Costs fall fast, permitting is reformed, and load stays manageable.

This represents the upper bound of what a power company can achieve with sector-specific policy and full technology access. REGEN typically shows a smooth transition: coal exits by ~2030, gas declines steadily, wind/solar/storage dominate, with CCS and nuclear providing firm clean power. System costs increase modestly (NPV ~$260-280B for a Midwest region).

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Power sector net CO₂ emissions must reach zero by 2050 (linear or cost-optimal glide path)
  • No economy-wide carbon price or cross-sector incentive
  • Full technology portfolio: renewables, new nuclear, gas w/ CCS, BECCS, DAC, H₂, fossil capacity reserves
  • Reference load growth, faster cost improvements, economic build rates
  • Lower fossil fuel prices
S2 Power Sector NZ 2050 / More Limited Options / Challenging
Required

State of the world: The worst-case decarbonization scenario for power-sector-only policy. The same net-zero-by-2050 mandate exists but CCS fails to scale commercially, new nuclear is blocked by permitting and cost overruns, and CDR remains too expensive. Meanwhile, AI-driven load growth surges, supply chains are constrained, and transmission buildout hits NIMBY opposition. The power sector must decarbonize using mostly renewables and storage while serving rapidly growing demand.

This is the "stress test" scenario — what happens when the ambition is high but the tools and conditions are limited. REGEN shows massive solar/wind/storage buildout (potentially 5× current capacity), steep electricity price increases (potentially +370% by 2035), and significant reliability challenges from high variable renewable penetration.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Power sector net CO₂ emissions must reach zero by 2050
  • No economy-wide carbon price
  • Limited technology: renewables, existing nuclear extensions, storage, electrolytic H₂ only
  • No CCS, no CDR, no new nuclear, fossil capacity reserves decline to zero by 2050
  • Faster load growth, slower cost improvements, constrained build rates, higher fuel prices
S3 Economy-Wide NZ 2050 / Fuller Options / Facilitating
Required

State of the world: The most aggressive decarbonization future. An economy-wide carbon price or equivalent policy drives all sectors toward net-zero by 2050. Buildings electrify. Transport shifts to EVs. Industry adopts clean hydrogen and CCS. This creates massive new demand for clean electricity, pulling investment into the power sector and accelerating its decarbonization — potentially reaching net-zero power by ~2035, well ahead of the 2050 target.

This is the most impactful scenario for power companies because economy-wide policy creates the revenue stream that finances the transition. New clean electricity demand from electrification drives capacity additions of 280-380 GW regionally. System costs are highest ($350-376B NPV) but offset by revenue from serving growing clean power demand.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Economy-wide net CO₂ emissions must reach zero by 2050
  • Cross-sector carbon price or equivalent policy applied to all sectors
  • Full technology portfolio including CCS, new nuclear, CDR, hydrogen
  • End-use electrification driven by carbon price (EVs, heat pumps, industrial electrification)
  • Reference load growth, faster cost improvements, economic build rates
S4 Economy-Wide NZ 2050 / More Limited Options / Challenging
Required

State of the world: Economy-wide net-zero mandate exists, but the transition is painful. CCS and new nuclear are blocked. DAC remains uneconomic. Load growth is extreme (data centers + electrification). Supply chains are constrained. This is the "hardest possible path to net-zero" — maximum ambition meets maximum friction.

REGEN results show the most extreme buildout requirements: potentially 5-7× current renewable capacity, 600+ GW of storage, wholesale electricity price increases of 300-400% by 2035. This scenario identifies the upper bound of transition cost and risk and highlights the value of having more technology options available.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Economy-wide net CO₂ emissions must reach zero by 2050
  • Cross-sector carbon price, but no CCS, no new nuclear, no CDR
  • Renewables, existing nuclear, storage, electrolytic H₂ only
  • Faster load growth (data centers + electrification compounding)
  • Slower cost improvements, constrained build rates, higher fuel prices
Q1 Qualified Targets (X, Y, Z%) / Fuller Options / Facilitating
Required (QT)

State of the world: Same favorable conditions as S1, but instead of mandating net-zero, the company evaluates incremental reduction levels (e.g., 30%, 50%, 80% below 2015 by 2050) to understand the marginal cost and system implications of increasing ambition. These results let the company identify the highest reduction level they can commit to without enabling conditions.

By plotting incremental costs, prices, and capacity needs against reduction levels, the company constructs a marginal abatement curve for their own system. The QT is typically set where marginal costs begin to accelerate sharply — often around 60-80% reduction under Fuller/Facilitating conditions.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Multiple runs at power sector CO₂ reduction targets: 30%, 50%, 80% from 2015 levels by 2050
  • No economy-wide policy (power sector only)
  • Full technology portfolio available
  • Reference load, faster costs, economic builds
  • Results compared to Reference (R1) and AT (S1) to construct incremental implication curves
Q2 Qualified Targets (X, Y, Z%) / More Limited / Challenging
Required (QT)

State of the world: Same incremental reduction analysis as Q1 but under worst-case conditions. This identifies what the company can achieve when nothing goes right — limited technology, hostile external conditions, but the company still wants to reduce emissions as much as prudently possible.

The QT under challenging conditions is typically lower than under facilitating conditions (e.g., 40-60% vs. 60-80%). The gap between the Fuller/Facilitating QT and the More Limited/Challenging QT defines the company's "ambition range" — the zone between what's achievable at best and worst.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Multiple runs at power sector CO₂ reduction targets: 30%, 50%, 80% from 2015 levels by 2050
  • No economy-wide policy
  • Limited technology: renewables, existing nuclear, storage only
  • Faster load growth, slower costs, constrained builds, higher fuel prices
  • Results compared to Reference (R2) and AT (S2)
S5 Power Sector NZ 2050 / Fuller Options / Challenging
Recommended

State of the world: Net-zero mandate with full technology access, but the external environment is difficult. CCS and nuclear are available but face headwinds: high costs, slow permitting, constrained supply chains. Load grows fast. This tests whether having the right tools matters even when conditions are hostile.

Results typically show that Fuller options significantly reduce transition costs even under Challenging conditions — highlighting the risk management value of keeping technology options open.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Power sector net-zero by 2050
  • Full technology portfolio
  • Faster load growth, slower cost improvements, constrained builds, higher fuel prices
S6 Power Sector NZ 2050 / More Limited Options / Facilitating
Recommended

State of the world: Net-zero mandate without CCS or new nuclear, but conditions are favorable — costs fall fast, builds are unconstrained, load is manageable. This tests whether favorable conditions can compensate for limited technology.

Results show that even with favorable conditions, removing CCS/nuclear/CDR significantly increases system costs compared to S1, though the transition is more manageable than S2.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Power sector net-zero by 2050
  • Limited technology (no CCS, no new nuclear, no CDR)
  • Reference load, faster costs, economic builds, lower fuel prices
S7 Economy-Wide NZ 2050 / Fuller Options / Challenging
Recommended

State of the world: Maximum policy ambition (economy-wide NZ) with full technology but hostile conditions. This is the "ambitious policy meets real-world friction" scenario. Load growth is extreme because electrification + data centers compound. But CCS and nuclear help manage the transition.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Economy-wide net-zero by 2050; cross-sector carbon price
  • Full technology portfolio
  • Faster load growth, slower cost improvements, constrained builds
S8 Economy-Wide NZ 2050 / More Limited Options / Facilitating
Recommended

State of the world: Maximum policy ambition but without CCS/nuclear, offset by favorable conditions. Tests whether an economy-wide transition is feasible on renewables and storage alone when everything else goes right.

REGEN Modeling Inputs
  • Economy-wide net-zero by 2050; cross-sector carbon price
  • Limited technology (renewables, storage, existing nuclear only)
  • Reference load, faster costs, economic builds

Applying SMARTargets to IPP Fleets

How conditions differ for Vistra, Constellation, NRG, and other merchant generators

Why IPPs Are Different from Regulated Utilities

The SMARTargets methodology was designed primarily for vertically integrated utilities that control generation, serve retail load, and operate under regulatory compact. IPPs face a fundamentally different challenge:

  • No captive load: IPPs sell into wholesale markets. They can't pass transition costs to ratepayers — they must recover costs through market prices or PPAs.
  • Market-driven dispatch: IPPs don't control which plants run when. Merit-order dispatch determines whether their gas CCGTs operate at 60% CF or 20% CF as renewables grow.
  • Multi-ISO exposure: Unlike a regional utility, IPPs like Vistra and Constellation operate across 3-5 ISOs with different market structures, fuel mixes, and regulatory environments.
  • No obligation to serve: IPPs can retire, mothball, or sell assets. Their "transition" is as much about portfolio management as about building new clean resources.
Key implication: For IPPs, "Facilitating" and "Challenging" conditions are primarily about market economics — wholesale power prices, capacity market revenues, PPA availability, and regulatory treatment of existing assets. A regulated utility's "Challenging" scenario (constrained builds) has a different character than an IPP's (collapsing capacity factors and stranded assets).

Can Our Model Approximate REGEN Outcomes?

Mapping the hourly CFE optimizer's capabilities to SMARTargets scenario requirements

What REGEN Does That We Can Approximate

US-REGEN is a full economy-wide capacity expansion model with endogenous technology choice, fuel markets, and cross-sector interactions. Our hourly CFE optimizer is a physics-based dispatch and procurement model. They solve different problems, but there are meaningful overlaps:

Merit-Order Fossil Dispatch

Strong match. Our Step 5A CO₂ model uses the same merit-order retirement logic as REGEN: coal retires first, then oil, then gas. We model dispatch decline curves across all 7 ISOs with 8,760-hour granularity.

Technology Portfolio Variation

Strong match. Our 5,832-scenario sensitivity framework already varies renewable costs (L/M/H), firm generation costs, storage costs, and CCS availability. This maps directly to the Fuller/More Limited dimension.

Regional Heterogeneity

Strong match. We model all 7 ISOs independently with regional generation profiles, emission factors, and resource mixes. This captures the regional variation REGEN models at the state/NERC level.

Emissions Reduction Pathways

Strong match. Our 21 CFE thresholds (10% to ≥99.99%) directly map to emissions reduction levels. Each threshold produces a specific emissions intensity reduction and total CO₂ abatement.

Cost-Optimal Resource Mix

Strong match. Our Step 3 co-optimization finds the minimum-cost resource mix for each threshold × scenario combination. This is analogous to REGEN's capacity expansion optimization.

Demand Growth Scenarios

Strong match. Our Track 2/3 (newbuild/cost-to-replace) model 25-year demand growth at 3 ISO-specific rates (Low/Medium/High) calibrated from EIA AEO 2025, NERC 2024 LTRA, and ISO/RTO planning forecasts. Rates range from 0.9%/yr (NEISO Low) to 5.5%/yr (ERCOT High). High scenarios incorporate data center and AI load growth projections; Low scenarios reflect baseline population/economic growth only. This maps directly to EPRI's Facilitating (reference load) vs. Challenging (faster load growth) dimension.

Economy-Wide Interactions

Gap. We don't model cross-sector interactions (transport electrification driving power demand, industrial hydrogen demand, building electrification). REGEN's economy-wide scenarios (S3/S4/S7/S8) capture effects we can only approximate through demand growth assumptions.

Wholesale Price Projections

Partial match. Our Step 5B synthetic LMP model captures merit-order price formation, but doesn't model capacity market revenues, scarcity pricing dynamics, or carbon price pass-through that REGEN endogenizes.

Key insight: Our model's primary advantage over generic REGEN runs is company-level granularity. REGEN models regional markets; we can apply those regional outcomes to each company's specific plant portfolio using our fleet survival data. For each scenario, we can calculate: (1) which plants survive dispatch merit-order changes, (2) what the company's emissions trajectory looks like, and (3) what the cost of transition is at each reduction level.

What We Can Produce for Each Company

  • Emissions uncertainty fans (P10/P50/P90) across all 12 scenarios, from 2025 to 2050
  • Fleet composition projections showing which plants retire/convert/strand under each scenario
  • Incremental cost curves (marginal $/tCO₂) at each reduction level to identify the QT "elbow"
  • Stranded asset exposure (MW at risk of uneconomic dispatch) under Challenging vs. Facilitating
  • Enabling conditions gap analysis — what would need to change for each company to move from their QT to the AT

What We Cannot Do (Requires REGEN)

  • Endogenous electricity demand response to carbon pricing (economy-wide feedback loops)
  • Cross-sector fuel market equilibrium (gas price impacts from reduced industrial gas demand)
  • Capacity expansion optimization with transmission topology (our model uses ISO-level aggregation)
  • Endogenous technology learning curves (our costs are exogenous L/M/H assumptions)

Key context: National electricity demand forecasts have been revised upward for three consecutive years. The Grid Strategies 2025 report projects 166 GW of peak load growth nationally by 2030 — a six-fold increase over the "flat growth" assumption that prevailed through 2022. Roughly 90 GW of this is attributable to data centers and AI workloads. Our High scenarios capture this structural break; our Low scenarios represent the pre-2023 baseline where demand was essentially flat.

Next Steps: Building the Analysis

What we'd need to do to produce SMARTargets-style results for each IPP

Implementation Roadmap

  1. Map fleet data to scenario inputs. For each company, identify which plants fall into each ISO's dispatch merit order and how their CFs change under each threshold/scenario combination.
  2. Define company-specific QT reduction levels. Using our Step 3 marginal cost curves, identify the "elbow" point where costs accelerate for each company under Fuller/Facilitating and More Limited/Challenging conditions.
  3. Generate emissions trajectory fans. For each of the 12 scenarios, compute year-by-year emissions using our SBTi timeline mapping (thresholds → years) and P10/P50/P90 dispatch decline curves.
  4. Identify enabling conditions. For each company, compute the gap between their QT and the AT and express it in terms of specific enabling conditions: CCS availability, capacity market reform, transmission investment, etc.
  5. Produce the visualization. Fan charts showing emissions uncertainty bands, marginal cost curves for QT identification, fleet composition waterfall charts showing the transition pathway under each scenario.
Scope estimate: This analysis leverages existing pipeline outputs (Steps 3-5) and fleet survival data. The primary new work is the scenario mapping logic and visualization. No new optimizer runs are needed — we're reinterpreting existing results through the SMARTargets lens.
