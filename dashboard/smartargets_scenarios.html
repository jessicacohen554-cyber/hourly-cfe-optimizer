EPRI SMARTargets Scenario Analysis

Applying the SMARTargets transition risk framework to independent power producers

The SMARTargets Methodology

EPRI's framework for setting grounded, actionable climate targets aligned with the Paris Agreement

What is SMARTargets?

EPRI's SMARTargets (Science-based, Multi-objective, Actionable, Risk-informed Targets) is a methodology for companies to set GHG emissions reduction targets that are grounded in relevant science, actionable given company-specific opportunities and constraints, and aligned with the Paris Agreement goals of limiting warming to well below 2°C.

Unlike SBTi's sector-specific benchmarks, SMARTargets recognizes that the same emissions reduction level may be achievable by one company but not another due to differences in fleet composition, regional markets, technology access, and regulatory context. The methodology uses scenario-based transition risk analysis to explore the range of plausible futures and identify both aspirational targets and the conditions that would enable or constrain their achievement.

Two Types of Targets

  • Aspirational Targets (ATs) — Generic Paris-aligned reduction levels that all companies evaluate: -56% by 2030, -82% by 2035, -88% by 2040, -94% by 2045, and net-zero (-100%) by 2050, all from a 2015 base year.
  • Qualified Targets (QTs) — Company-specific targets reflecting the best a company can achieve given realistic conditions without enabling conditions being in place. QTs are always less than or equal to ATs, and the company must identify what enabling conditions would bridge the gap.

The 8-Step Process

  1. GHG Inventories & Base Year — Assemble verified emissions inventory
  2. Practical Issues & Target Groupings — Assess control, materiality, regulatory coverage
  3. Aspirational Targets — Select Paris-aligned ATs from standardized table
  4. Transition Risk Analysis — Run scenario modeling (the core analytical engine)
  5. Strategy & Qualified Target Alignment — Set QTs based on scenario results
  6. Documentation & Communication — Report targets, conditions, and strategies
  7. Validation & Verification — Internal and external review
  8. Monitoring & Adjusting — Periodic reassessment as conditions evolve
Step 4 is where the science happens. EPRI recommends using US-REGEN, a detailed regional economy-wide model that combines electricity capacity expansion, end-use technology choice, fuel markets, and emissions constraints to produce integrated transition pathways. The scenario results identify potential future states, robust strategies, enabling conditions, and risk management options.

The 8-Scenario Framework

Four scenario families × two condition sets: Facilitating vs. Challenging

Scenario Design: Three Uncertainty Dimensions

Each scenario represents a different set of market conditions — not a different target. The question each scenario answers: "How clean does the grid get when the market looks like this?"

The framework is built on three uncertainty dimensions:

  1. Decarbonization Incentive — None (reference/qualified) or power sector net-zero by 2050 (aspirational). Reference and Qualified scenarios use current policy only — no forcing mechanism. Aspirational scenarios apply a carbon price or equivalent constraint. The Qualified Target level emerges from each company's cost tolerance threshold under reference conditions.
  2. Decarbonization Technology Options — Fuller vs. More Limited technology portfolios. Fuller includes DAC, CCS, new nuclear (SMR/advanced), LDES, and Green H₂. More Limited restricts to renewables, batteries, and existing nuclear only — nuclear stalls, CCS fails, LDES stays pre-commercial.
  3. Market Conditions — Facilitating vs. Challenging. Facilitating means low clean LCOE, reformed interconnection queues (~5-8 GW/yr/ISO), fast learning curves, and moderate demand growth. Challenging means high clean costs, constrained queues (~2-4 GW/yr/ISO, status quo), slow learning, and high demand growth from AI/data centers.

Each scenario pairs a technology portfolio with a condition set: Fuller options always get Facilitating conditions (best case) and More Limited always gets Challenging (worst case). This bounds the decision space between "what's achievable at best" and "what you're limited to at worst."

The 8 Scenarios: Detailed Narratives

What each scenario represents as a "state of the world" and what our market simulation models

R1 Reference — Facilitating Conditions

"Everything goes right, but no one's pushing for decarbonization."

State of the world: No new emissions policy beyond what's already enacted. Current IRA incentives (45Y new-build PTC, 45U existing nuclear PTC through 2032, 45Q CCS credit), state RPS mandates, and REC revenue all remain in place. Technology costs start low and fall fast via aggressive learning curves. Interconnection queues are reformed (FERC Order 2023 takes effect), allowing ~5-8 GW/yr/ISO of new capacity. Demand grows at a moderate pace. ESG lending constraints and siting opposition create moderate friction for new gas builds.

This scenario establishes the upper bound of what the market does on its own — economics alone, no carbon price. Developers build whatever's profitable at market LMPs. The question is: how clean does the grid get when the wind is at your back but nobody's mandating deeper cuts?

Scenario Inputs
  • Carbon price: $0
  • Policy: Current — 45Y (new-build PTC ~$26/MWh), 45U (existing nuclear $15/MWh through 2032), 45Q ($85/ton CCS), state RPS + REC revenue
  • Demand growth: Medium (region-specific: ~1.4% CAISO, ~1.9% ERCOT, ~2.4% PJM)
  • Interconnection: Reformed queues (~5-8 GW/yr/ISO)
  • Learning curves: Fast (high learning rate per capacity doubling)
  • Starting LCOE: Low (NREL ATB optimistic projections)
  • New gas: Moderate friction (ESG lending, siting opposition)
R2 Reference — Challenging Conditions

"Everything's an uphill battle, and no one's pushing either."

State of the world: Same current policies as R1, but the external environment is hostile to the transition. AI/data center demand surges electricity consumption. Interconnection queues remain broken (~80% project dropout, 5-year average wait). Supply chain constraints and tariffs keep clean LCOE high. Learning curves are slow. Gas builds freely where profitable — no ESG or permitting friction. 45Q stays intact, keeping CCS an option at the margin.

This is the "headwinds without mandates" scenario. High demand + constrained queues + slow learning could mean extended fossil dominance. The question: does high demand growth pull in clean energy (because there's more to build) or extend fossil plant lifetimes (because clean can't connect fast enough)?

Scenario Inputs
  • Carbon price: $0
  • Policy: Current (45Q stays intact — CCS still gets its credit)
  • Demand growth: High (region-specific: ~2.5% CAISO, ~5.5% ERCOT, ~3.6% PJM, ~4.4% NYISO)
  • Interconnection: Constrained queues (~2-4 GW/yr/ISO, status quo)
  • Learning curves: Slow (supply chain bottlenecks, regulatory friction)
  • Starting LCOE: High (tariffs, supply chain premiums)
  • New gas: Low friction (builds freely where profitable)
S1 Power Sector NZ 2050 / Fuller Options / Facilitating

"Full commitment to power sector net-zero with every tool available."

State of the world: A carbon price or equivalent forcing mechanism (federal CES, cap-and-trade) mandates power sector net-zero CO₂ by 2050 — but other sectors (transport, industry, buildings) are not directly constrained. All technologies are available: CCS-CCGT is viable with 45Q intact, SMR/advanced nuclear clears NRC review, LDES (iron-air) and Green H₂ reach commercial scale. DAC is available and should be leveraged at the crossover point from our optimal target exercise — the grid pushes clean until marginal abatement cost exceeds DAC cost, then DAC handles residual emissions.

This represents the upper bound of what's achievable with sector-specific policy and full technology access. The transition is smooth: coal exits by economics, gas declines via carbon pricing, wind/solar/storage dominate mid-range thresholds, CCS and nuclear provide firm clean power for the last mile.

Scenario Inputs
  • Carbon price: Yes ($50-100 range, or equivalent CES mandate)
  • Policy: Current + carbon pricing mechanism
  • Demand growth: Medium (region-specific Low/Medium rates)
  • Interconnection: Reformed queues
  • Learning curves: Fast
  • Starting LCOE: Low
  • DAC: Available at scenario cost (Low $150 / Med $250 / High $400 per ton) — used at marginal MAC crossover
  • CCS: Viable, 45Q intact, CO₂ infrastructure builds out
  • Nuclear: SMR/advanced pathway open
S2 Power Sector NZ 2050 / More Limited Options / Challenging

"Same NZ mandate, but the toolkit is constrained and the headwinds are real."

State of the world: The same net-zero-by-2050 forcing mechanism exists, but CCS fails to scale commercially (storage site constraints, pipeline opposition, 45Q expires). New nuclear is blocked by cost overruns and NRC delays. DAC is not available or prohibitively expensive ($600+/ton) — the grid must decarbonize deeper without offset options. Meanwhile, AI-driven demand growth surges, interconnection queues remain broken, and supply chains keep costs high.

This is the "stress test" — what happens when NZ is mandated but the tools are limited and conditions are hostile. The grid must get there on renewables, batteries, and existing nuclear alone. The cost premium over S1 quantifies the value of keeping technology options open.

Scenario Inputs
  • Carbon price: Yes (same mechanism as S1, biting harder through headwinds)
  • Demand growth: High (region-specific High rates)
  • Interconnection: Constrained queues (status quo)
  • Learning curves: Slow
  • Starting LCOE: High
  • DAC: Not available ($600+/ton — grid must decarbonize without offsets)
  • CCS: Limited (storage constraints, 45Q at risk)
  • Nuclear: Stalls (cost overruns, NRC delays)
S3 Economy-Wide NZ 2050 / Fuller Options / Facilitating

"The whole economy goes net-zero. The grid is the backbone."

State of the world: An economy-wide carbon price drives all sectors toward net-zero by 2050. Buildings electrify (heat pumps). Transport shifts to EVs. Industry adopts clean hydrogen and CCS. This creates massive new demand for clean electricity that's not optional — it's policy-mandated electrification.

S3 differs from S1 in three specific ways, not just "more demand":

  1. Demand growth is mandatory and high even under facilitating conditions (~High region-specific rates) — economy-wide NZ requires electrifying everything. This isn't "maybe demand grows" — it's policy-required.
  2. DAC budget is shared across the whole economy — in S1, the grid uses DAC for its residual emissions and that's it. In S3, industry (steel, cement), transport (aviation, shipping), and agriculture also need DAC for their residual emissions. The grid's "share" of available DAC capacity shrinks, meaning the grid may need to push to a higher clean percentage.
  3. Green H₂ production load — economy-wide NZ requires green hydrogen for steel, chemicals, and shipping fuel. H₂ electrolysis consumes clean electricity, adding ~20-30% demand on top of direct electrification.
Scenario Inputs
  • Carbon price: Economy-wide (higher signal than power-only)
  • Demand growth: High (~High region-specific rates — mandatory electrification)
  • H₂ load: Significant — electrolysis demand on top of direct electricity load
  • Interconnection: Reformed queues (economy-wide urgency accelerates reform)
  • Learning curves: Fast (economy-wide deployment accelerates learning across sectors)
  • Starting LCOE: Low
  • DAC: Available, but shared — grid gets a fraction of total DAC capacity
S4 Economy-Wide NZ 2050 / More Limited Options / Challenging

"The hardest scenario. Everything electrifies, nothing's easy."

State of the world: Economy-wide NZ mandate exists, but the transition is painful. CCS and new nuclear are blocked. DAC is not available — the grid must go nearly all the way without offsets. Demand growth is extreme (data centers + mandatory electrification + H₂ production compounding under constrained grid). Supply chains are constrained. Interconnection queues remain broken — the worst combination: massive demand + can't build fast enough.

This scenario tests whether economy-wide NZ is even physically achievable under adverse conditions, or if something has to give. The cost premium over S3 reveals the value of technology optionality and enabling conditions.

Scenario Inputs
  • Carbon price: Economy-wide
  • Demand growth: Very high (~High region-specific rates + H₂ + aggressive electrification)
  • Interconnection: Constrained queues (massive demand, can't build fast enough)
  • Learning curves: Slow
  • Starting LCOE: High
  • DAC: Not available — grid must decarbonize without offsets
  • CCS/Nuclear: Limited / stalled
  • H₂ load: Same or higher (no alternative to green H₂ under economy-wide NZ)
Q1 Qualified Target — Best Achievable Without New Policy / Facilitating
Qualified Target

"What can we credibly commit to today, without waiting for new policy?"

State of the world: Identical to R1 — current policy, no carbon price, no new mandates. The difference is the question being asked. R1 asks "what does the market do?" Q1 asks "what can the company commit to shareholders, based on what the market will do?"

QTs are NOT partial ATs with a weaker forcing mechanism. They represent the best a company can achieve under current/realistic conditions without new enabling conditions. No new carbon price, no new CES, no new mandates beyond what exists today. The QT level emerges from the same market simulation as R1 — it's the market equilibrium formalized as a corporate commitment.

How the QT is determined: Emissions reduction ranges are tested at 5% intervals (50%, 55%, 60%, ... 95%) to identify the precise crossover point where further reductions become uneconomic.

  1. Run the market simulation under R1 facilitating conditions, forcing each 5% reduction target
  2. The model outputs how wholesale prices, capacity needs, and retirement schedules must shift to meet each target
  3. The company identifies the strictest target its fleet can align with while remaining profitable and competitive
  4. The gap between QT and AT (S1/S2 NZ target) is the key output — it defines the enabling conditions the company must report

Utility vs. IPP framing: For a vertically integrated utility, the QT balances reductions against consumer rates — "how far can we push without unacceptable rate increases?" For an IPP, the premise shifts to competitive market survival:

  • Regional view: Without a captive rate base, forcing emission targets simulates the entire regional wholesale market's least-cost response
  • Market viability: The model reveals how wholesale prices and capacity needs shift at each 5% step
  • Portfolio scaling: The IPP scales macro-level market shifts down to evaluate its own asset viability
  • Setting the target: The QT is the strictest reduction where the fleet remains profitable — balancing reductions against asset stranding and merchant risk
Scenario Inputs (Same as R1)
  • Carbon price: $0 (no new forcing mechanism)
  • Policy: Current only — 45Y, 45U, 45Q, state RPS + REC revenue
  • Demand growth: Medium (region-specific, same as R1)
  • Interconnection: Reformed queues (same as R1)
  • Learning curves: Fast (same as R1)
  • Starting LCOE: Low (same as R1)
  • Key output: Maximum credible commitment level under best-case current conditions. Plus: the enabling conditions gap (QT → AT).
Q2 Qualified Target — Best Achievable Without New Policy / Challenging
Qualified Target

"What can we commit to when the market works against us?"

State of the world: Identical to R2 — current policy, no carbon price, challenging conditions. Nuclear stalls, LDES stays pre-commercial, CCS is limited but 45Q remains. High demand growth compounds with constrained queues and slow learning.

Q2 defines the QT floor — the minimum credible commitment even when everything's hard. Same 5% interval testing (50%, 55%, ... 95%) but under adverse conditions, the crossover hits earlier — higher costs per reduction increment push the viable threshold lower. The range between Q1 and Q2 results defines the company's "ambition range": what they can commit to at best vs. worst under current policy.

The AT-QT gap under challenging conditions is larger — meaning more enabling conditions are needed to close it. This is the risk signal: "If conditions deteriorate, we need even more help to reach NZ."

Scenario Inputs (Same as R2)
  • Carbon price: $0 (no new forcing mechanism)
  • Policy: Current only (45Q stays intact)
  • Demand growth: High (region-specific, same as R2)
  • Interconnection: Constrained queues (same as R2)
  • Learning curves: Slow (same as R2)
  • Starting LCOE: High (same as R2)
  • Technology: More Limited (same constraints as R2)
  • Key output: Minimum credible commitment under adverse conditions. The AT-QT gap here shows the full enabling conditions needed.

Applying SMARTargets to IPP Fleets

How conditions differ for Vistra, Constellation, NRG, and other merchant generators under our 8-scenario framework

Why IPPs Are Different from Regulated Utilities

The SMARTargets methodology was designed primarily for vertically integrated utilities that control generation, serve retail load, and operate under regulatory compact. IPPs face a fundamentally different challenge:

  • No captive load: IPPs sell into wholesale markets. They can't pass transition costs to ratepayers — they must recover costs through market prices or PPAs.
  • Market-driven dispatch: IPPs don't control which plants run when. Merit-order dispatch determines whether their gas CCGTs operate at 60% CF or 20% CF as renewables grow.
  • Multi-ISO exposure: Unlike a regional utility, IPPs like Vistra and Constellation operate across 3-5 ISOs with different market structures, fuel mixes, and regulatory environments.
  • No obligation to serve: IPPs can retire, mothball, or sell assets. Their "transition" is as much about portfolio management as about building new clean resources.
Key implication: For IPPs, "Facilitating" and "Challenging" conditions are primarily about market economics — wholesale power prices, capacity market revenues, PPA availability, and regulatory treatment of existing assets. A regulated utility's "Challenging" scenario (constrained builds) has a different character than an IPP's (collapsing capacity factors and stranded assets).

Our Market Simulation vs. REGEN

How our hourly dispatch model maps to the SMARTargets methodology — and where it extends beyond REGEN

EPRI Recommends US-REGEN — We Build Something Complementary

EPRI's SMARTargets methodology recommends using US-REGEN, a full economy-wide capacity expansion model that combines electricity dispatch, end-use technology choice, fuel markets, and emissions constraints. Our model is a market simulation built on hourly physics-based dispatch across 7 ISOs with 8,760-hour granularity. They ask different questions but produce comparable outputs for the power sector scenarios.

Where REGEN results are available, they provide useful calibration benchmarks. REGEN typically shows:

  • R1/R2 (Reference): 25-40% emissions reductions by 2050 from market forces alone — coal retirement driven by economics, gas CCGT expansion, continued renewable buildout under IRA credits. Under challenging conditions (R2), potentially only 15-25% reductions if load growth outpaces clean deployment.
  • S1 (Fuller/Facilitating NZ): Smooth transition — coal exits by ~2030, gas declines steadily, CCS and nuclear provide firm clean power. System costs increase modestly.
  • S2 (Limited/Challenging NZ): Massive solar/wind/storage buildout (potentially 5× current capacity), steep electricity price increases (potentially +370% by 2035), significant reliability challenges from high VRE penetration.
  • S3 (Economy-wide Fuller): Power sector potentially reaches NZ by ~2035 (ahead of 2050 target) because economy-wide carbon price creates the revenue stream that finances the transition. Capacity additions of 280-380 GW regionally.
  • S4 (Economy-wide Limited): Most extreme buildout: 5-7× current renewable capacity, 600+ GW of storage, wholesale prices up 300-400% by 2035. Tests whether NZ is even physically achievable.
Merit-Order Fossil Dispatch

Strong match. Our Step 5A CO₂ model uses the same merit-order retirement logic: coal retires first, then oil, then gas. 8,760-hour dispatch across all 7 ISOs. Comparable to REGEN's power sector dispatch module.

Technology Portfolio Variation

Strong match. Our 5,832-scenario sensitivity framework (17,496 for CAISO) varies renewable costs, firm gen costs, storage, CCS, and transmission across L/M/H. Maps directly to Fuller/More Limited dimension.

Regional Heterogeneity

Strong match. 7 ISOs modeled independently with regional generation profiles, emission factors, resource mixes, and region-specific demand growth rates. Captures regional variation REGEN models at state/NERC level.

Cost-Optimal Resource Mix

Strong match. Step 3 co-optimization finds minimum-cost resource mix for each threshold × scenario. Analogous to REGEN's capacity expansion, with the advantage of 21 clean energy thresholds (10% to ≥99.99%).

Demand Growth & Learning Curves

Strong match. Region-specific demand growth rates (e.g., ERCOT High = 5.5%/yr, PJM High = 3.6%/yr) already calibrated to AI/electrification scenarios. Endogenous Wright's Law learning curves (deployment-based, not time-based) for nuclear, CCS, LDES, H₂.

Interconnection Queue Constraints

New capability. REGEN uses "constrained to historical build rates" as a proxy. Our model explicitly models max GW/yr/ISO queue throughput with project prioritization by profit margin. More granular than REGEN's approach.

LMP & Revenue Feedback

Partial match. Step 5B synthetic LMP from merit-order fossil stack captures price formation and revenue cannibalization. REGEN additionally endogenizes capacity market clearing prices and cross-sector fuel market equilibrium.

Economy-Wide Interactions

Gap. We don't model cross-sector feedback loops (transport electrification → power demand, industrial H₂ demand, building electrification → load shape changes). S3/S4 economy-wide effects are proxied through high demand growth rates + H₂ load adders.

S1 (Power NZ / Fuller / Facilitating) Carbon price forcing + Low LCOE + fast learning + reformed queues + Medium demand. Full toolkit (CCS, nuclear, DAC at crossover). DAC leveraged at marginal MAC crossover point from optimal target exercise. High

S3 (Economy NZ / Fuller / Facilitating) Economy-wide carbon price + High demand (mandatory electrification) + H₂ load adder + shared DAC budget + Low LCOE + fast learning + reformed queues. Economy-wide effects proxied through demand + H₂. Medium — economy-wide feedback loops not captured
S4 (Economy NZ / Limited / Challenging) Economy-wide carbon price + very high demand + H₂ load + no DAC + High LCOE + slow learning + constrained queues. The feasibility stress test. Medium — same economy-wide gap
Q1 (QT / Fuller / Facilitating) Same as R1 conditions (no forcing mechanism). Run market simulation, then sweep company-specific marginal cost curve across thresholds to find the "tolerable" reduction level — where incremental costs become prohibitive without new policy. The QT is this crossover point. High
Q2 (QT / Limited / Challenging) Same as R2 conditions. Same marginal cost sweep but under adverse conditions. QT floor — minimum credible commitment. The AT-QT gap here defines the full enabling conditions needed. High
Key advantage over REGEN: Our model's primary strength is company-level granularity at hourly resolution. REGEN models regional aggregate markets; we can apply regional outcomes to each company's specific plant portfolio. For each scenario, we calculate: (1) which plants survive dispatch merit-order changes, (2) what the company's emissions trajectory looks like, (3) cost at each reduction level, and (4) fleet stranding risk. Additionally, our endogenous Wright's Law learning curves and explicit interconnection queue modeling add dynamics that REGEN handles with simpler assumptions.

What We Can Produce for Each Company

  • Emissions uncertainty fans (P10/P50/P90) across all 8 scenarios, from 2025 to 2050
  • Fleet composition projections showing which plants retire/convert/strand under each scenario
  • Incremental cost curves (marginal $/tCO₂) at each reduction level to identify the QT "elbow"
  • Stranded asset exposure (MW at risk of uneconomic dispatch) under Challenging vs. Facilitating
  • Enabling conditions gap analysis — what would need to change to move from QT to AT
  • Market equilibrium clean level per ISO — where deployment stalls without further policy (R1/R2)
  • DAC crossover analysis — where marginal MAC exceeds DAC cost, identifying portfolio-optimal clean level

What We Cannot Do (Requires REGEN)

  • Endogenous electricity demand response to carbon pricing (economy-wide feedback loops)
  • Cross-sector fuel market equilibrium (gas price impacts from reduced industrial gas demand)
  • Capacity expansion optimization with transmission topology (our model uses ISO-level aggregation)
  • End-use technology choice modeling (EV adoption curves, heat pump diffusion, industrial process switching)

Next Steps: Building the Analysis

What we'd need to do to produce SMARTargets-style results for each IPP

Implementation Roadmap

  1. Build the market simulation module. ~30-50 lines of glue code on top of existing Step 5D zone-delta structure. Revenue calculator, profit metric, stopping rule, price feedback, learning curve integration. All existing infrastructure — we're re-ranking by profit instead of MAC.
  2. Map fleet data to scenario inputs. For each company, identify which plants fall into each ISO's dispatch merit order and how their CFs change under each scenario's market conditions.
  3. Run all 8 scenarios. Each produces per-ISO: market equilibrium clean level, resource mix, cost breakdown, revenue decomposition, cumulative GW deployed. ~60 seconds total compute.
  4. Define company-specific QT reduction levels. Using marginal cost curves from Q1/Q2, identify the "elbow" where costs accelerate under Fuller/Facilitating vs. More Limited/Challenging.
  5. Generate emissions trajectory fans. For each of the 8 scenarios, compute year-by-year emissions using SBTi timeline mapping (thresholds → years) and P10/P50/P90 dispatch decline curves.
  6. Identify enabling conditions. For each company, compute the gap between their QT and the AT. Express in terms of specific enabling conditions: interconnection queue reform, CCS availability, nuclear pathway, learning curve acceleration.
  7. Produce the visualization. Fan charts, marginal cost curves for QT identification, fleet composition waterfalls, market equilibrium comparison across scenarios.
Scope estimate: The market simulation module is lightweight (~30-50 lines of new code) built on top of existing Step 5D zone metrics, Step 5B LMP engine, and Step 8D learning curves. All 8 scenarios run in ~60 seconds total. No new optimizer runs needed for the core physics — we're adding a revenue/profit layer on top of existing dispatch and cost infrastructure.
