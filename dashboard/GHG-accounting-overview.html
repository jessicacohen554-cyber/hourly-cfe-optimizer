How you count carbon determines where capital flows — and how it reshapes the grid

Three Ways to Count Carbon: How Each Shapes Grid Decarbonization

The GHG Protocol Scope 2 revision has sparked a three-way debate about how corporations should account for electricity emissions. The answer determines where hundreds of billions in clean energy capital flows — and whether it actually decarbonizes anything.

Annual RECs are the status quo. The proposed revision adds hourly matching with deliverability constraints — still attributional, but aligned to grid physics. A parallel track develops consequential accounting: optimize for the cheapest $/tCO₂ displaced anywhere on the grid, independent of temporal or spatial matching to the buyer's load. We model 10 procurement strategies across 3 families to assess how each shapes investment incentives across 7 ISO/RTO regions.

Across 7 ISO/RTOs (~2.7 million GWh), the three accounting families — consequential, hourly, and annual — create distinct investment incentive structures. Consequential directs capital cross-regionally toward the cheapest marginal abatement; hourly matching constrains procurement to the buyer's own grid on an 8,760-hour basis; annual matching allows temporal flexibility that simplifies compliance but weakens signals for firm clean power and storage. Each framework involves real tradeoffs between cost efficiency, verifiability, and grid transformation.
2–5×
The last-mile cost escalation reveals a real tradeoff
Hourly matching from 90% → ≥99.99% costs 2–5× more per MWh. These hard hours — nights, low-wind winter days — require firm clean power and storage that VRE alone cannot provide. The consequential critique that this marginal dollar could buy more abatement elsewhere is valid at the margin; the hourly matching argument is that these costs drive the technology learning curves (nuclear, CCS, LDES) needed for system-wide deep decarbonization.
Dual Effect
Hourly attribution produces both inventory accounting and real emissions displacement
Because hourly matching requires procurement aligned to grid physics and deliverability, it produces accurate consumption-based inventory accounting while also driving local clean energy deployment. This dual effect means the attributional framework also generates consequential outcomes — though at higher cost than purpose-optimized consequential approaches at low-to-moderate CFE targets.

Annual, hourly, and consequential are distinct frameworks
Annual attribution (current Scope 2) and hourly attribution (proposed revision) are both inventory accounting. Consequential is a separate paradigm — estimating marginal impact rather than tracking consumption. The GHG Protocol is consulting on both tracks simultaneously.
Coordination
Cross-regional optimization raises collective action questions
When buyers independently optimize for cheapest $/tCO₂ regardless of location, procurement concentrates on the same low-cost opportunities. This raises questions about whether local grids receive sufficient investment — particularly gas-dependent grids where abatement costs more but is still necessary for system-wide decarbonization.

Consequential accounting relies on estimating counterfactual outcomes — what would have happened without the intervention. Marginal emissions rates vary by hour, model, and baseline assumption. Three Methods, Three Philosophies

The debate isn't just "attributional vs. consequential." It's a three-way contest between fundamentally different theories of how carbon accounting should drive decarbonization — and each creates radically different investment incentives.

1. Annual Attribution Current

The status quo. Buy RECs equal to your annual consumption. No requirement for when, where, or whether the clean energy was actually delivered to your grid.

  • GHG Protocol Scope 2 market-based method (2015)
  • Annual matching: total RECs ≥ total consumption
  • No temporal or locational granularity required
  • A New England company buys Texas wind RECs at 2 AM to cover a winter 6 PM gas peak
  • Near-zero consequential emissions impact (Xu et al. 2024, WattTime 2024)

2. Hourly Attribution Proposed

The proposed Scope 2 revision. Match consumption with CFE hour-by-hour within deliverability regions. Still attributional inventory accounting — but aligned to grid physics.

  • Proposed GHG Protocol Scope 2 revision (expected ~2027)
  • Hourly matching: CFE supply = demand each hour
  • Deliverability constraints: same grid region / market boundary
  • Reveals need for firm clean power (nuclear, CCS, geothermal) + storage
  • Strong consequential impact as a byproduct of physics-aligned accounting

Explore hourly matching analysis →

3. Consequential / Impact Impact-Based

Championed by WattTime and the Emissions First Partnership. Estimate marginal emissions impact of procurement decisions. Optimize for cheapest $/tCO₂ abated — anywhere, anytime.

  • Separate GHG Protocol consultation track (parallel to Scope 2 revision)
  • No requirement to match load temporally or spatially
  • Metric: tCO₂ displaced per dollar, measured via marginal emissions rates
  • Philosophy: pursue the cheapest abatement wherever it exists on the grid
  • Optimizes for lowest-cost abatement; does not require temporal or spatial matching to buyer load

The Core Tradeoff

Hourly attribution is attributional — it tracks what you consume and what you procure. Because it aligns accounting to grid physics and deliverability, it also produces consequential impact: you can't match every hour without deploying resources that actually serve your grid. Consequential accounting takes a different approach — it explicitly optimizes for the cheapest $/tCO₂ displaced anywhere on the grid, regardless of the buyer's consumption profile. The central question in the GHG Protocol debate is how to weigh these tradeoffs: hourly matching's physics alignment and verifiability vs. consequential's cost efficiency and global optimization. Both frameworks aim for decarbonization but create different investment incentive structures at scale.

The Evolving Landscape

The GHG Protocol is running two parallel consultations: one revising Scope 2 attributional accounting (proposing hourly matching), another developing a new consequential framework. 800+ stakeholders have weighed in. The outcome will reshape corporate clean energy investment globally.

2020
Google Commits to 24/7 CFE by 2030
First major company to commit to matching electricity consumption with carbon-free energy every hour of every day, on every grid where it operates. Published the CFE% metric for hourly tracking.
2021
UN 24/7 CFE Compact Launched
140+ signatories including governments, corporates, and NGOs committed to advancing hourly carbon-free energy. The U.S. Government joined at COP28 in 2023, directing federal facilities toward 50% 24/7 CFE by 2030.
2022–23
EnergyTag & Granular Certificates
EnergyTag develops standards for hourly energy attribute tracking. Time-based Energy Attribute Certificates (T-EACs) enable granular trading — not just "how much" but "when and where" clean energy was generated.
2024–26
GHG Protocol Dual-Track Consultation
Two parallel consultations (Oct 2025 – Jan 2026): Scope 2 revision proposing hourly matching + deliverability for attributional accounting, and a separate consequential accounting framework for impact-based disclosure. 800+ stakeholder responses. WattTime and Emissions First Partnership advocate for the consequential track; EnergyTag and 24/7 CFE supporters push for hourly attribution.
~2027
New Standards Expected
The revised Scope 2 standard and consequential guidance are scheduled for publication. Whether hourly attribution, consequential impact, or both become the corporate reporting default will determine the trajectory of hundreds of GW in clean energy procurement.

Ten Strategies We Assess

The same corporate dollar can displace dramatically different amounts of carbon depending on how and where it's deployed. We model 10 procurement strategies across 3 families, each with distinct rules about what counts as "clean," where investment goes, and how emissions are attributed. Click each family below for the full deep dive.

1

Consequential Accounting (Strategies 1A, 1B & 1C)

Cross-regional deployment queue — build clean energy wherever the marginal abatement cost is lowest, regardless of where the buyer sits.

3 variants
▼

How It Works

Consequential procurement builds clean energy wherever the marginal abatement cost is lowest, regardless of the buyer's location. This creates a cross-regional deployment queue — a merit order of decarbonization opportunities ranked by $/tCO₂ displaced. At each threshold step, the optimizer deploys resources sequentially across all 7 ISOs, locking in prior deployments as committed infrastructure. The deployment queue follows the fossil fuel merit order: coal displacement comes first (highest emissions per MWh), then oil, then gas.

Strategy 1A

Grid-Average Baseline

Credits emission reductions against the full grid emission rate — total emissions divided by total generation (including existing clean energy in the denominator). This produces a lower baseline rate, making displacement appear cheaper per ton. A grid already 40% clean has a lower "average" rate than its fossil fleet actually emits. Lower bar for claiming displacement, but dilutes the signal.

Strategy 1B

Fossil-Average Baseline

Credits reductions against only the fossil fleet's emission rate. Uses actual marginal dispatch rates from 8,760-hour models where available; falls back to fossil-fleet average as proxy. At 80% C&I participation, 1B attributes ~33% more emissions to corporate load than 1A — setting a higher bar for displacement and providing a more honest picture of what fossil generation is actually being displaced.

Strategy 1C

Marginal Emission Baseline

Credits reductions against marginal emission rates — the emission rate of the specific generator that would have run next absent the clean intervention. Sets the highest bar for displacement claims: in MISO, marginal rates are ~17% higher than the fossil fleet average; in SPP, ~22% higher. Each MWh of clean energy displaces more CO₂ under marginal accounting, but the baseline is harder to clear. In gas-dominated grids (CAISO, NEISO), marginal and fossil-average rates are nearly identical since gas CCGTs are the marginal unit in most hours.

1A vs 1B vs 1C: All three strategies deploy identical resources in the same cross-regional sequence — the deployment queue is the same. The difference is purely in how emissions are attributed. 1A uses the full grid average (lowest bar), 1B uses the fossil fleet average (higher bar), and 1C uses the marginal emission rate (highest bar in coal-heavy grids). Same physics, different accounting — the choice of baseline determines how much credit each buyer can claim per MWh procured.

The Coal Wall

Consequential deployment follows the fossil fuel merit order: coal is displaced first (highest emissions per MWh, ~2,200 lb CO₂/MWh), then oil, then gas (~850 lb/MWh). The "coal wall" is the threshold where all coal generation has been retired from the dispatch stack. In most ISOs, this happens at roughly 70% CFE. Beyond the coal wall, every additional clean MWh only displaces gas — cutting the marginal carbon benefit nearly in half and sharply increasing the marginal abatement cost. CAISO, with zero coal in its dispatch stack, is entirely gas + oil from day one.

Considerations at Scale

Five dynamics emerge when consequential accounting scales from individual buyer analysis to a system-wide procurement framework:

1
Marginal Signal Degradation. At low volumes, marginal rates are meaningful. At 5–15% C&I participation, each new PPA displaces a different marginal unit — but every buyer still reports the original rate. First PPA displaces coal at 2,200 lb/MWh; by the tenth, it's CCGT at 850. Bjorn et al. (2024) show buyer-level claims sum to 2–3× actual grid reductions when aggregated.
2
Saturation & Diminishing Returns. Every grid has a finite stock of high-emission generators available for displacement. Consequential concentrates capital on this stock until it's exhausted — coal-heavy grids saturate first because they have the cheapest abatement. Once coal generation is retired from the dispatch stack, every additional clean MWh only displaces gas, cutting the marginal carbon benefit roughly in half and raising the effective $/tCO₂.
3
Firm Clean Underinvestment. By directing marginal clean energy capital toward the cheapest abatement opportunities (primarily coal displacement), consequential may delay investment in firm clean alternatives for gas-dependent grids (NEISO, NYISO, CAISO). Without specific demand signals for nuclear, CCS, or LDES, developers have less incentive to build these technologies in regions where gas is the primary fossil fuel. Consequential proponents counter that system-wide emissions reduction is more efficient than requiring every region to develop its own firm clean resources simultaneously.
4
Geographic Clustering. Because consequential optimizes for cheapest $/tCO₂ cross-regionally, investment concentrates in grids with the largest coal fleets (e.g., SPP, MISO) while gas-dependent grids (NEISO, NYISO, CAISO) receive proportionally less. Corporate buyers in these gas-heavy regions may fund abatement in other grids while their own grids see limited clean energy deployment.
5
Collective Action Dynamics. Each buyer optimizing for their own cheapest $/tCO₂ is individually rational. But the aggregate effect may under-invest in each grid's need for firm clean power — because cheaper abatement exists elsewhere, no individual buyer has an incentive to fund the more expensive local resources. Hourly matching addresses this by construction: when procurement must match load hour-by-hour within a region, investment stays local. Consequential proponents argue that system-wide optimization is more efficient than requiring every buyer to solve their own grid independently.
The Role of Consequential Analysis: These dynamics suggest consequential accounting may be most effective as a complementary analytical lens — measuring displacement impact alongside a procurement standard — rather than as the sole framework governing procurement decisions. As a primary system-wide framework, the scaling dynamics above introduce coordination challenges. As an analytical overlay on hourly or annual procurement, it provides valuable insight into marginal grid impact.
2

Hourly Matching (Strategies 2A, 2B & 2C)

Match clean generation to consumption every hour within the buyer's own ISO — the most rigorous temporal standard.

3 variants
▼

How It Works

Hourly matching requires clean energy generation to equal consumption every hour of every day within the buyer's own ISO/RTO region. No annual averaging, no cross-regional credit. This is the most temporally granular accounting standard, and it reveals the full cost structure of deep decarbonization — because night hours and low-wind winter days can't be covered by solar and wind alone. The hourly constraint creates demand signals for firm clean power (nuclear, CCS, geothermal) and long-duration storage.

Strategy 2A

Pure New-Build (100% Additionality)

Every MWh of clean energy must come from new-build resources contracted by the buyer. No credit for existing grid clean energy. Highest cost, but the strongest investment signal — every dollar drives new capacity. This variant models what happens when corporations can't claim credit for existing nuclear, hydro, or renewable generation. It produces the highest resource mix costs but also the highest marginal grid impact.

Strategy 2B

Grid Baseline Credit

Buyers receive credit for the existing clean energy already embedded in their grid's generation mix. The hourly matching requirement applies only to the incremental clean energy above the grid baseline. In a grid that's already 40% clean, the buyer needs to procure resources for the remaining 60% of hours where grid supply doesn't match demand. Lower cost than 2A, but weaker additionality signal — existing clean counts for free.

Strategy 2C

Pro-Rata Allocation with SSS (GHG Protocol Proposed)

The model aligned with the GHG Protocol's proposed Scope 2 revision. The grid's clean energy supply splits into four distinct pools — each with different pricing, availability, and incentive effects. Standard Supply Service (SSS) — policy-supported clean energy — is free to all ratepayers. Corporate buyers wanting to claim beyond SSS must procure additional clean energy hour-by-hour. This analysis also includes a roll-off sensitivity (Strategy 2C Roll-Off) that models the impact of scheduled nuclear support program expirations — see below.

The Four-Pool Supply Model (Strategy 2C)

What is Standard Supply Service (SSS)?
SSS is not just "existing grid clean." It's specifically: publicly owned, rate-based, or policy-supported clean energy. Under GHG Protocol revisions, SSS is the clean energy all consumers automatically get credit for — it's embedded in their utility supply. Corporate buyers wanting to claim beyond SSS must procure additional clean energy hour-by-hour.

SSS Stability vs. Roll-Off: This analysis uses a 2025 snapshot that treats all current nuclear support policies as stable. In practice, several key programs have scheduled expirations:
  • NJ ZEC — expires 2026 (−15 TWh from PJM SSS)
  • IL CMC — expires 2028 (−50 TWh PJM, −15 TWh MISO)
  • NY ZEC Tier 3 — expires 2030 (−42 TWh from NYISO SSS)
Strategy 2C includes a roll-off sensitivity variant that models these expirations. Critically, rolled-off plants do not retire — they continue operating under the federal 45U PTC ($15/MWh) and capacity market revenue, moving from the SSS pool (Pool 1, free) to the merchant pool (Pool 3, ~$4–5/MWh EAC premium). The impact is cost reallocation across supply pools, not capacity loss. See the Procurement Deployment dashboard for the interactive roll-off toggle.
The Hourly Constraint and Investment Signals: At 80–90% CFE targets, over 4,000 hours per year require firm clean or storage — every night plus every low-wind winter day. Hourly matching signals a need for 25+ TWh of firm clean that annual matching would not reveal. The cost is higher in the short run, but learning curve effects from early firm investment may reduce system costs by 2040–2045.

Considerations & Tradeoffs

Hourly matching is the most demanding standard. At high CFE targets (>95%), costs escalate steeply — 2–5× the cost at the inflection zone — because firm clean power and storage are required for every nighttime and low-wind hour. This creates real concerns about cost accessibility: smaller buyers or those in expensive, supply-constrained grids (NEISO, NYISO) may find high hourly targets prohibitively expensive. The consequential critique — that the same marginal dollar buys more abatement when deployed cross-regionally — has real force, particularly at the margin. Hourly matching also depends on the availability of firm clean technologies (nuclear, CCS, LDES) that are not yet commercially mature in all markets.

Annual Matching (Strategies 3A, 3B, 3C & 3D)

Match total annual clean generation to annual consumption — simpler accounting, but temporal mismatches weaken investment signals for firm clean power.

4 variants
▼

How It Works

Annual matching requires total clean energy generation to equal or exceed total annual consumption — with no requirement to match on an hourly basis. Summer solar surplus can offset winter gas peaks. This is the simplest accounting standard and the current GHG Protocol Scope 2 market-based method. Because temporal mismatches are permitted, the approach has the lowest barrier to entry but generates weaker investment signals for firm clean power and storage compared to hourly matching.

Strategy 3A

Same-ISO New Build (Additionality)

Annual matching within the buyer's own ISO with an additionality requirement: only newly built clean energy counts toward the corporate claim. This drives real capital investment within the buyer's region, but the temporal mismatch means it can claim 100% annual matching while running on gas every winter evening. Cost is moderate (new VRE is cheap), but the annual flexibility means solar/wind surplus covers all hours on paper — creating limited signal for firm clean power or storage.

Strategy 3B

Cross-Regional New Build (No Additionality)

Annual matching from any US ISO with no additionality requirement — existing clean generation counts. Can purchase bundled RECs from existing generators at low cost. Cross-regional flexibility keeps costs low, but the lack of additionality means procurement may not drive any new clean energy deployment.

Strategy 3C

Same-ISO + No Additionality

Annual matching constrained to the buyer's own ISO region, with existing clean energy counting toward the claim. Limits geographic arbitrage (can't claim cross-regional RECs), but still allows temporal mismatch and provides no additionality signal. The same-ISO constraint means costs vary significantly by region — cheap in ERCOT/SPP (abundant merchant clean), expensive in supply-constrained NEISO/NYISO.

Strategy 3D

Cross-Regional + No Additionality (Status Quo)

The loosest standard and the de facto status quo for most corporate procurement today. Buy unbundled RECs from any ISO, any vintage, existing or new — equal to annual consumption. Cheapest option by far ($1–5/MWh for unbundled RECs vs. $30–80/MWh for new-build hourly-matched CFE). Near-zero consequential emissions impact (Xu et al. 2024). A data center in Virginia buys West Texas wind RECs at 2 AM to cover a January 6 PM gas peak — and claims "100% renewable."

The Temporal Gap Problem

Annual matching's fundamental weakness is temporal mismatch. At 95% annual matching, a typical PJM buyer might have:

  • Summer days (May–Aug): 130–160% surplus solar generation. Excess is curtailed or sold.
  • Winter evenings (Nov–Feb, 5–9 PM): 15–30% clean coverage. Fossil gas fills the gap.
  • Low-wind weeks: Multi-day periods with <20% clean coverage regardless of season.

The annual total balances — but the grid runs on fossil fuel for thousands of hours. No investment signal for firm clean power. No storage deployment. No learning curves for next-generation technologies. The accounting gives a passing grade to a strategy that leaves the hard hours permanently unsolved.

The Physical Limit: Annual matching faces a practical ceiling around ~95% — beyond this point, massive VRE overbuild and curtailment are required, but 4,000+ night and low-wind hours still cannot be covered by solar and wind alone regardless of annual surplus. Bridging these hours requires firm clean power or long-duration storage — resources that annual matching, by design, does not specifically incentivize. Hourly matching and consequential accounting address this gap through different mechanisms: hourly matching by creating direct procurement demand for these resources, and consequential by directing capital to wherever the next marginal ton of CO₂ can be displaced most cheaply.
