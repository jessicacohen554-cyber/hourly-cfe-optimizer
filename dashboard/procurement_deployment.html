Overview

The Deployment Divergence

Corporate buyers are rational economic actors. Under any procurement strategy, they optimize cost within the rules — chasing the cheapest available clean energy that qualifies. At low CFE targets (50–70%), this doesn't matter much: cheap VRE (solar and wind) dominates every strategy, every region. The strategies look nearly identical.

At higher targets — 85%, 90%, 95% and beyond — the strategies diverge dramatically. The rules of each strategy determine what gets built, where capital flows, and which resources receive bankable demand signals. This is the core insight: procurement strategy choice is market design. It shapes the aggregate capital allocation that determines whether the grid actually decarbonizes.

Use the sliders above to see this divergence in real-time. Start at 50% and slide to 95% — watch how the jars fill differently as the physics of high-reliability matching force different resource mixes.

The benchmark: reliable, affordable, feasible, clean grids everywhere — to the extent that marginal abatement cost makes sense. At higher CFE targets, clean firm energy (nuclear, CCS, LDES) becomes MAC-justified because the rest of the economy needs a clean grid to decarbonize.
Strategies 1A & 1B

Consequential's Cross-Border Arbitrage

Under consequential strategies (1A grid-average, 1B fossil-average), rational buyers chase the cheapest $/tCO₂ wherever it exists. Capital flows to SPP wind, ERCOT solar, and NYISO nuclear uprates — regardless of where the buyer sits. The buyer's local grid sees no new capacity.

In aggregate, this creates massive capital concentration in a few ISOs with the cheapest marginal sources. At low participation (2–5%), cheap cross-regional sources are abundant. But as participation climbs past 10–15%, those sources exhaust and buyers are forced up the cost curve — eventually matching or exceeding hourly matching costs, but without the same grid-transformative impact.

The glow effect on the matrix above shows which ISOs are sources under cross-regional strategies. Notice how capital concentrates in 2–3 ISOs while other regions receive nothing. This is economically efficient in the narrow sense — but it doesn't build the clean grids that the rest of the economy needs to decarbonize.

Strategies 2A, 2B & 2C

Hourly Matching: The Firm Clean Energy Signal

Hourly matching is a physics constraint, not just an accounting choice. At 90%+ CFE, you cannot match demand hour-by-hour with VRE alone — solar doesn't generate at night, wind is intermittent. The hourly constraint forces procurement of firm clean energy: nuclear, CCS-CCGT, and long-duration storage.

This is the only strategy family that creates bankable, long-term demand signals for the firm clean resources the grid needs. An IPP considering a $10B nuclear investment needs 20+ years of contracted offtake. Hourly matching at scale provides exactly that signal.

How the three variants differ

2A (New-Build Only) — Maximum additionality. Every MWh is new capacity. Expensive at low targets but creates the strongest deployment signal. No credit for existing clean.

2B (Grid Baseline) — Buyers take free credit for existing clean on the grid. Cheapest hourly variant. But existing nuclear and hydro receive no revenue signal — creating stranding risk at scale.

2C (SSS + Tranches) — Buyers first claim their pro-rata share of system supply, then walk up a merit-order tranche stack: existing nuclear (45U credits) → uprates → existing VRE/hydro (EAC) → new VRE → new firm. This reflects rational buyer behavior — cheapest first — while ensuring existing clean assets get real revenue.

Key finding: At 90%+ CFE targets, hourly strategies procure 3–10× more firm clean energy than consequential or annual strategies. This is the demand signal that enables the firm clean investment pipeline the grid needs.
Asset Risk

Existing Asset Viability

The US grid already has substantial clean energy — existing nuclear, hydro, and increasingly wind/solar. How procurement strategies treat these assets determines whether they stay online.

Strategy 2B: The free-rider problem

Under 2B, existing clean gets "free" credit. Rational buyers love this — it's the cheapest hourly strategy. But in aggregate, existing nuclear plants receive no incremental revenue from corporate procurement. At scale, this accelerates merchant revenue erosion and raises stranding risk for the very assets keeping the grid clean today.

Strategy 2C: Preserving existing clean

Under 2C, existing nuclear and hydro are available as premium tranches — buyers pay a small premium (above wholesale but below new-build cost) to claim these EACs. This creates a sustainable revenue stream that keeps existing clean assets viable long-term.

Strategies 3A/3B: New-build indifference

Annual matching (3A/3B) with new-build-only rules ignores existing assets entirely. No revenue signal, no stranding protection, but also no free-riding. The capital all flows to new capacity.

The stakes: ~800 TWh of existing US nuclear generation is at risk over the next two decades. If procurement strategies don't create revenue signals for these assets, replacing them with new clean capacity will cost $200B+ more than preserving them.
IPP Enabling Conditions

The Market Signal IPPs Need

Independent power producers making capital allocation decisions — AES, NextEra, Constellation, Vistra — need bankable demand signals to justify firm clean investment. A 20-year nuclear build or CCS retrofit requires contracted offtake, not speculative wholesale market exposure.

Hourly matching at scale (>10% C&I participation) triggers Wright's Law learning curves for firm clean energy. As aggregate demand for nuclear SMRs, advanced CCS, and LDES grows, manufacturing scales, supply chains mature, and costs bend down. This is the same dynamic that drove solar PV costs down 90% — but it requires sufficient aggregate demand to initiate.

The critical mass threshold — the participation level at which learning curves bend costs enough to make firm clean MAC-justified against DAC/SCC benchmarks — varies by technology. For nuclear SMRs, it's approximately 15–20% C&I participation at 90%+ targets. For advanced CCS, it's lower (~10%) due to existing industrial learning.

The virtuous cycle: Enough buyers choosing hourly matching → enough firm clean demand → cost curves bend via Wright's Law → firm clean becomes competitive → MAC stays below DAC/SCC → deeper decarbonization becomes affordable → more buyers join. The question is whether enough corporate buyers choose the strategy that initiates this cycle.
Synthesis

Which Strategy Builds Clean Grids?

The aggregate market outcome depends on which strategy framework is adopted. Every strategy "works" in the narrow sense of matching buyer demand with clean energy certificates. But only some strategies build the clean, reliable, affordable grids that the rest of the economy needs to decarbonize.

Strategy 2C (Hourly SSS + Tranches) creates the strongest aggregate signal for deep grid decarbonization:

  • Preserves existing clean revenue — SSS allocation + premium tranches keep existing nuclear and hydro viable
  • Forces firm clean investment — hourly physics constraint at 90%+ CFE demands nuclear, CCS, and LDES
  • Triggers learning curves at scale — >10–15% participation unlocks Wright's Law cost reductions
  • MAC-justified at higher targets — clean firm costs are below DAC/SCC benchmarks when learning curves kick in
  • Builds local grid capacity — same-ISO matching means capital stays in the buyer's region

Consequential strategies (1A/1B) are cheaper at low targets but create cross-regional arbitrage that doesn't transform local grids. Annual strategies (3A/3B) lose the hourly constraint that forces firm clean investment. Strategy 2B is a tempting cost optimization that free-rides on existing assets.

Bottom line: Strategy choice is market design. The procurement framework determines what the aggregate capital allocation looks like — and whether it builds the reliable, affordable, feasible, clean grids that the rest of the economy needs to decarbonize. Strategy 2C aligns rational buyer incentives with the grid's actual needs.
