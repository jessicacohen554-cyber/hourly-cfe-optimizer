Consider a CAISO buyer who procures enough wind and solar to cover 100% of annual load. Spreadsheet says: mission accomplished. But look at any single hour—solar generates nothing at midnight, and wind can’t fill the gap alone.

Act 3

The VRE-Gas Trap

Every megawatt of solar and wind added to the grid pushes wholesale electricity prices lower. Past a tipping point, gas plants lose money most hours of the year—but the grid still needs them at night. They can’t retire. They can’t profit. They’re trapped.

Structural Lock-in
At 95% clean energy, gas plants lose money 6,350 hours per year—73% of the time. But the grid still needs them for the remaining 2,410 hours. They can’t shut down without blackouts. They can’t stay open without subsidies. This is the structural trap at the heart of the energy transition.

The VRE-gas trap isn’t a market failure—it’s a physics constraint. Solving it requires firm clean resources that can fill the gap without the carbon.

Act 4

The Stranded Asset Cliff

In regions like PJM, you might successfully deplete the coal wall with cheap wind. But you’re left with a massive gas fleet and redundant VRE that eventually become stranded.

The marginal abatement cost (MAC)—what it costs to eliminate one more ton of CO₂—stays manageable up to about 85% clean energy. Then a cliff appears. The last 15% of emissions are trapped in hours where no cheap substitute exists, and costs explode past every benchmark: the social cost of carbon, the EU carbon market, even direct air capture.

Grid costs now exceed direct air capture. Past the cliff, every additional percentage of hourly clean energy costs more per ton of CO₂ avoided than pulling carbon directly from the atmosphere. The rational strategy shifts from grid decarbonization to portfolio diversification.

The cliff isn’t a failure of ambition—it’s physics meeting economics. The hours that remain fossil-dependent after 90% are the hardest, most expensive hours on the grid. Solving them requires a fundamentally different toolkit: firm clean generation, long-duration storage, or accepting that the last few percent may be cheaper to abate elsewhere.
