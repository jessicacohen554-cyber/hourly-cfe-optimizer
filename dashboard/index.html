The Thesis

The Grid is Not a Bank

The grid is a live, interconnected machine—not a vault where megawatt-hours can be deposited and withdrawn at will.

We can’t store MWhs in a vault to be distributed later, so we can’t account for clean energy that way either. Incentive structures and policies that don’t consider grid physics have adverse consequences—and the difference between the illusion of paper accounting and grid reality is significant.

What you count, where you start, and where you’re going matter profoundly in a complex spatial, temporal, and physical system like the grid. Throughout this analysis, toggle between Illusion (the paper accounting view) and Reality (what actually happens on the grid) to see why.

Act 1

Every Hour is Different

Carbon intensity and demand both vary dramatically by the hour and by region. Annual averages hide these differences completely.

A solar MWh generated at noon in California displaces almost no fossil fuel—the grid is already clean at that hour. That same MWh at 8 PM in PJM displaces coal. Annual accounting treats them as identical. Hourly data reveals the truth.

Act 2

The 100% Clean Illusion

Annual accounting matches abundant daytime solar with nighttime demand. The math works on paper, but physically, the company relies entirely on the dirty grid after dark.

Consider a CAISO buyer who procures enough wind and solar to cover 100% of annual load. Spreadsheet says: mission accomplished. But look at any single hour—solar generates nothing at midnight, and wind can’t fill the gap alone.

Act 3

The VRE-Gas Trap

Adding solar and wind without considering when and where the grid needs power doesn’t just leave gaps—it creates a structural dependency on the very fossil plants you’re trying to replace.

When you procure clean energy on an annual basis without matching hourly demand, you flood the grid with power it can’t use at midday and starve it during evening peaks. Wholesale prices collapse during surplus hours, but gas plants still have to run when renewables can’t. You haven’t displaced gas—you’ve made it unprofitable and indispensable.

VRE deployment in a vacuum creates structural lock-in for gas.

Every hour of surplus solar that crashes prices to zero is an hour you chose not to invest in firm clean resources that could have displaced gas permanently. The VRE-gas trap isn’t inevitable—it’s the cost of ignoring the grid’s temporal and spatial requirements.

Act 4

The Stranded Asset Cliff

PJM’s Journey to 95% Hourly Clean Energy

Two strategies, same destination: PJM’s grid from 38% clean to 95%. The difference is the journey—what gets built, what gets stranded, and what it costs everyone in the end.

Strategy 1B deploys the cheapest resources first. Solar and wind saturate the easy hours, but scarcity hours still need gas. IPPs respond to that signal by building CCGT capacity that’s needed at 60% clean but stranded by 90%. That locked-in gas capex becomes a political anchor—an industrial complex that will fight to protect its investments.

Strategy 2C co-optimizes hourly matching from day one. Clean firm and storage deploy early, driving Wright’s Law learning curves that make each subsequent GW cheaper. Gas retires steadily. No stranded assets. No lock-in. The transition costs more upfront for buyers—but costs less for everyone.

This isn’t about ambition—it’s about sequencing. Building VRE without firm clean for the hard hours forces gas buildout that becomes unnecessary at high clean targets. The capital is spent. The lobbying apparatus is built. The rates are higher. And the people who pay those rates see “more renewables” and “higher bills” and draw the obvious conclusion. Strategy 2C avoids the trap by investing in clean firm early—when it’s expensive but catalytic—so that by the time the grid needs it most, learning curves have made it affordable.

Strategy 1B — Annual Matching

Strategy 2C — Hourly Matching

Cheap for the buyer ≠ cheap for the system. Strategy 1B reduces corporate emissions on paper at $12–22/MWh—by externalizing the cost of gas buildout needed for resource adequacy. IPPs build gas to cover the scarcity hours VRE can’t. That gas capex gets stranded as clean targets tighten, but the capital is already spent, the plants are already built, and the political constituency to protect them is already organized. The result: a climate transition that’s more expensive for everyone, rate increases that erode public support, and an industrial complex locked into defending fossil assets. Strategy 2C pays more upfront but builds the infrastructure that actually finishes the job.
