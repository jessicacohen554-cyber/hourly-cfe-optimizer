Who Generates America’s Electricity?

Market Structure Generation Ownership March 2026

The U.S. power sector is split between two fundamentally different ownership models. Vertically integrated utilities—companies like Duke, Southern, AEP, and Dominion—own generation, transmission, and distribution under state-regulated cost-of-service frameworks. Independent power producers (IPPs) and merchant generators—like Constellation, Vistra, and NRG—sell into competitive wholesale markets with no captive retail customers.

This distinction matters for decarbonization because the two models respond to entirely different incentive structures. Regulated utilities earn a guaranteed return on capital assets, making them path-dependent on existing fleet composition. IPPs respond to wholesale price signals, making their decisions more volatile but also more market-responsive. PJM is the nation’s largest power market by both generation and geographic reach, spanning 13 states and D.C. Its ~520 TWh of regulated utility generation reflects the dominance of large vertically integrated operators in Virginia (Dominion), the Ohio Valley (AEP, FirstEnergy), and the Carolinas (Duke). The competitive side is anchored by Constellation’s massive nuclear fleet—the largest in North America—concentrated in Pennsylvania and Illinois. PJM’s capacity market (RPM) is a critical revenue stream for generators in deregulated states, while regulated utilities in Virginia, West Virginia, and North Carolina earn through state-approved rate base.

MISO is overwhelmingly regulated territory. Nearly three-quarters of generation comes from vertically integrated utilities and their affiliates. The footprint stretches from Manitoba to the Gulf Coast, encompassing some of the most coal-dependent states in the country (Indiana, North Dakota) alongside states making aggressive clean energy transitions (Minnesota, Iowa). Entergy’s Gulf South fleet, DTE and Consumers in Michigan, and Xcel in the upper Midwest are the backbone of MISO’s generation. IPP presence is limited mostly to wind farms (NextEra, Invenergy) selling under long-term PPAs to the utilities themselves. MISO’s lack of a forward capacity market makes merchant investment challenging.

ERCOT is America’s most market-driven grid. Texas deregulated generation in 2002, and the result is an energy-only market dominated by merchant generators. Vistra (which absorbed Luminant) is the largest, followed by NRG and a rapidly growing renewable IPP fleet. The ~40% “regulated” share comes primarily from municipal utilities (CPS, Austin Energy, LCRA) and co-ops that were exempt from deregulation. Texas’s lack of a capacity market means generators earn only from energy and ancillary services—creating boom-bust cycles but also strong incentives for low-cost wind and solar. Wind now produces more electricity than coal in ERCOT.

California is a unique hybrid. The state’s three investor-owned utilities (PG&E, SCE, SDG&E) are regulated but were forced to divest much of their generation during deregulation in the late 1990s. As a result, the IOUs own relatively little generation directly and instead procure power through long-term contracts with IPPs. Constellation (via its Calpine subsidiary) is the state’s largest merchant generator by capacity. LADWP and SMUD, as municipal utilities, remain vertically integrated. California’s aggressive RPS mandate (100% clean by 2045) and storage mandate have made it the largest market for solar, wind, and battery IPPs in the country. The duck curve—surplus midday solar depressing prices followed by steep evening ramps—is the defining market dynamic.

SPP is the most utility-dominated ISO in the country. Roughly 80% of generation comes from vertically integrated utilities, cooperatives, and municipal/public power entities. The region’s traditional reliance on coal is being displaced by wind at a remarkable pace—SPP regularly achieves 70%+ instantaneous wind penetration and has set records above 90%. But the utilities driving this transition (Evergy, AEP Southwest, Xcel SPS) are doing so through regulated resource plans, not merchant investment. IPP presence is almost exclusively wind farms selling under long-term PPAs. Nebraska is unique as the only state with 100% public power—no private utilities at all.

New York’s grid is defined by a north–south divide. Upstate New York has abundant hydro (Niagara Falls, St. Lawrence) and Constellation’s nuclear fleet (Nine Mile Point, Ginna, and FitzPatrick), delivering low-carbon power at wholesale prices. Downstate (NYC, Long Island) is constrained by transmission bottlenecks and relies heavily on gas-fired generation—some of the most expensive and carbon-intensive power in the Northeast. The closure of Indian Point nuclear plant in 2021 removed 2 GW of zero-carbon baseload from the NYC zone, increasing gas dependency. New York’s CLCPA (100% clean by 2040) is the most aggressive state mandate in the country, driving massive offshore wind and storage procurement.

New England is the most gas-dependent grid in the country—and the most exposed to winter supply crunches. Gas pipeline constraints mean that during cold snaps, gas-fired generators compete with home heating for limited supply, driving wholesale prices to extreme levels ($100+/MWh intraday). This “fuel security” risk led ISO-NE to retain Mystic Station and develop the Inventoried Energy Program. The region’s IOUs (Eversource, National Grid, Unitil) own very little generation directly—they are primarily distribution utilities that procure power through competitive solicitations. True vertically integrated utilities are rare; Vermont (Green Mountain Power) is the notable exception. Nuclear (Millstone, Seabrook) provides ~30% of the region’s carbon-free generation, supplemented by Hydro-Québec imports.

Cross-Cutting Themes

The regulatory model determines the decarbonization pathway. Vertically integrated utilities transition through state-approved integrated resource plans (IRPs)—a deliberate, sometimes slow process constrained by rate-of-return regulation and stranded asset risk. IPPs transition by responding to market signals and PPA demand, which can be faster but is more volatile and dependent on policy certainty.

The biggest generators by volume are regulated. Southern Company, Duke, AEP, Entergy, Dominion, and Berkshire Hathaway Energy collectively generate more than 600 TWh annually—almost entirely through regulated, vertically integrated subsidiaries. Constellation is the dominant exception: ~300+ TWh of merchant generation across nuclear (legacy Constellation fleet) and gas/geothermal (via its Calpine subsidiary), making it the nation’s largest IPP by output.

Some companies span both models. NextEra operates Florida Power & Light (a regulated utility) alongside NextEra Energy Resources (the world’s largest wind/solar IPP). AES has regulated subsidiaries in Indiana and Ohio alongside merchant assets in other markets. These hybrid companies can arbitrage between regulatory frameworks.

Municipal and cooperative utilities are a significant “third category.” ERCOT’s CPS Energy, NYISO’s NYPA, Nebraska’s 100% public power system, and California’s LADWP/SMUD operate outside both the merchant and investor-owned utility models. They’re accountable to city councils or member-owners, not shareholders or PUCs—creating yet another set of incentive structures for the clean energy transition.
