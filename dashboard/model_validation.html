Model Validation & Scientific Integrity

How we tested the model, why results are sound, and what the limitations are

1. Executive Summary

The 8,760 Problem Optimizer is a 2025-snapshot energy system model that co-optimizes resource mix and incremental cost for hourly clean energy procurement across 7 U.S. Independent System Operators (ISOs). It evaluates every hour of the year (8,760 hours) to determine which combinations of clean resources — solar, wind, nuclear, CCS, hydro, battery storage, and long-duration storage — can match electricity demand at each target threshold, from 50% to ≥99.99% hourly clean energy.

What the model claims: Given a target hourly clean energy percentage, what is the least-cost resource portfolio to achieve it, and how does that cost change under different technology cost and policy assumptions?

What the model does NOT claim: This is not a forecast. It does not predict future electricity prices, technology adoption timelines, or policy outcomes. It is a parametric snapshot showing the 2025 cost landscape under 5,832 scenario combinations.

2. Hourly Match Score — The Physics Engine

How the model determines what percentage of electricity demand is met by clean energy in each hour

2.1 Supply Construction

For each resource r (solar, wind, nuclear, CCS, hydro, offshore wind) with allocation percentage pctr and normalized hourly generation profile Pr(h):

Total Clean Supply at Hour h
\[ S(h) = \text{procurement} \times \sum_{r} \frac{pct_r}{100} \times P_r(h) \]

where profiles \(P_r(h)\) are normalized so that \(\sum_{h=1}^{8760} P_r(h) = 1.0\), and procurement is typically 1.0 (100%).

dispatch_utils.py:720–745 — Supply is built from a pre-computed supply matrix (6 resources × 8,760 hours) multiplied by resource weight vector.

2.2 Hourly Matching

At each hour, the amount of demand met by clean energy is the minimum of supply and demand:

Hourly Match
\[ \text{matched}(h) = \min\big(D(h),\; S(h)\big) \]
Hourly Match Score (%)
\[ \text{score} = \frac{\sum_{h=1}^{8760} \text{matched}(h)}{\sum_{h=1}^{8760} D(h)} \times 100\% \]

dispatch_utils.py:778–780 — fossil_displaced = min(demand, total_clean), residual_demand = max(0, demand - total_clean).

2.3 Merit-Order Dispatch

When multiple resources generate simultaneously, they are dispatched in merit order (baseload first, variable last):

  1. Clean Firm (nuclear) — dispatched first, flat baseload with seasonal derate
  2. CCS-CCGT — flat baseload, deployed as carbon-capture natural gas
  3. Hydro — existing fleet, follows historical hourly shape
  4. Offshore Wind — variable but higher capacity factor than onshore
  5. Wind — variable, follows regional wind patterns
  6. Solar — dispatched last, zero at night, peak midday

dispatch_utils.py:248 — DISPATCH_ORDER = ['clean_firm', 'ccs_ccgt', 'hydro', 'offshore_wind', 'wind', 'solar']

Justification: The min(demand, supply) hourly matching metric is the standard used in 24/7 clean energy procurement literature, including Google's 24/7 CFE program, Microsoft's hourly matching framework, and Xu et al. (2024, Joule). Merit-order dispatch prioritizes firm/baseload resources that can't be curtailed, matching how grid operators actually dispatch generation.

3.2 Algorithm

For each day (24-hour window), the battery:

  1. Computes available charge energy: \(\text{avail} = \eta_{\text{RT}} \times \sum_{h} \max(0, S(h) - D(h))\)
  2. Computes demand gap: \(\text{gap} = \sum_{h} \max(0, D(h) - S(h))\)
  3. Sets dispatch target: \(E_{\text{dispatch}} = \min(\text{capacity}, \text{avail}, \text{gap})\)
  4. Charges from hours with largest surplus first (greedy), respecting power limit \(\frac{\text{capacity}}{\text{duration}}\)
  5. Discharges to hours with largest gap first (greedy), same power limit

dispatch_utils.py:260–319 — _battery_loop() Numba-compiled inner loop.

Energy Accounting
\[ E_{\text{charged}} = \frac{E_{\text{dispatch}}}{\eta_{\text{RT}}} \]

Charge always exceeds discharge by factor \(1/\eta\). At 85% RTE, 1.176 MWh charged per 1.0 MWh discharged.

Justification: Greedy dispatch (charge from largest surpluses, discharge to largest gaps) is a heuristic approximation of optimal dispatch. For daily-cycle batteries with well-defined solar surplus patterns, greedy produces near-optimal results. A perfect-foresight linear program would yield ~2–5% higher utilization, but at 100× computational cost for 7M+ resource mixes. The heuristic introduces a conservative bias — actual battery value is at least as high as modeled.
Known limitation: No inter-day energy transfer, no price-responsive dispatch, no battery degradation modeling. The daily window prevents batteries from carrying charge across midnight — a simplification that slightly undervalues 8-hour batteries during multi-day weather events.

4.3 Algorithm

State-of-charge (SOC) persists across rolling windows:

  1. For each window: identify surplus hours (clean supply > demand)
  2. Charge from largest surplus hours first, up to energy capacity, respecting power limit \(\frac{\text{capacity}}{\text{duration}}\)
  3. Discharge to largest gap hours: \(E_{\text{out}} = \text{SOC} \times \eta_{\text{LDES}}\), SOC debited by \(\frac{E_{\text{out}}}{\eta}\)
  4. SOC carries to next window (enables multi-week storage)

dispatch_utils.py:322–376 — _ldes_loop() Numba-compiled with state-of-charge persistence.

Justification: The 50% RTE for iron-air is Form Energy's published specification for their 100-hour system (announced 2023). The 7-day rolling window captures synoptic weather patterns — cold snaps and heat domes typically last 4–7 days, which is the primary use case for LDES. SOC persistence across windows enables the system to accumulate charge over sunny/windy periods and discharge over extended cloudy/calm periods.
Known limitation: Greedy dispatch within each window is sub-optimal for LDES — a full-year linear program would yield ~10–15% higher utilization by optimizing charge/discharge timing globally. This introduces a conservative bias: actual LDES value is higher than modeled. The 50% RTE is a pre-commercial estimate for iron-air; deployed systems may achieve 45–55%.

5. Cost Function — How Resources Translate to $/MWh

Incremental cost of clean energy procurement, accounting for existing fleet, new-build, and system costs

5.1 Total Cost Structure

Total Incremental Cost ($/MWh of demand)
\[ C_{\text{total}} = C_{\text{solar}} + C_{\text{wind}} + C_{\text{osw}} + C_{\text{CCS}} + C_{\text{CF}} + C_{\text{storage}} + C_{\text{gas\_backup}} \]

5.2 New vs. Existing Pricing

Existing fleet resources are sunk cost — $0 incremental. Only new-build above the existing share incurs cost:

Per-Resource Cost
\[ C_r = \frac{\max(0,\; pct_r - pct_{\text{existing},r})}{100} \times (\text{LCOE}_r + \text{TX}_r) \]

where \(\text{LCOE}_r\) is the levelized cost from NREL ATB 2024 / Lazard v17-18, and \(\text{TX}_r\) is the transmission interconnection adder ($/MWh).

step3_cost_optimization.py:638–658

Justification: Existing fleet at $0 is standard in capacity expansion models (GenX, ReEDS, NREL Standard Scenarios). Nuclear, hydro, and existing renewables have near-zero marginal operating cost — their capital is sunk. The model evaluates incremental cost of achieving a higher clean energy target, not total system cost.

5.3 Clean Firm Merit-Order Tranche Pricing

New clean firm capacity is priced via a merit-order stack, filling the cheapest options first:

  1. Tranche 1: Nuclear Uprates — Existing plant power uprates (capped per ISO at 8% of installed capacity × 90% CF). LCOE: $15–40/MWh depending on cost sensitivity. No new transmission needed.
  2. Tranche 2: Geothermal (CAISO only) — Enhanced geothermal systems, capped at 39 TWh (~5 GW × 90% CF). LCOE varies by Low/Medium/High toggle.
  3. Tranche 3: Nuclear New-Build vs. CCS — Remaining demand filled by the cheaper of new nuclear or CCS-CCGT. CCS is capped at regional geologic storage limits; overflow goes to nuclear.

5.5 Storage Pricing

Storage Cost (annualized)
\[ C_{\text{storage}} = \sum_{s \in \{bat4, bat8, LDES, H_2\}} \frac{pct_s}{100} \times \text{LCOE}_s \]

No transmission adder — storage is assumed co-located with generation or load.

5.6 Gas Backup (Resource Adequacy)

The model computes incremental gas capacity needed to meet peak demand with a 15% reserve margin:

Gas Backup Cost
\[ \text{gas\_raw} = \frac{\max(0,\; \text{RA\_peak} - \text{clean\_peak\_MW})}{\text{GAF}} \] \[ C_{\text{gas}} = \frac{\text{existing\_gas\_FOM} + \text{new\_gas\_CAPEX}}{\text{demand\_MWh}} \]

RA_peak = peak demand × 1.15. Clean peak MW includes capacity credits per resource (nuclear 100%, solar 30%, wind 10%, battery 95%). GAF = gas availability factor (82–88% by ISO).

6. CO&sub2; Emission Displacement Model

Merit-order fossil fuel retirement: coal first, then oil, then gas

6.1 Dispatch Stack Retirement

Clean energy displaces fossil fuels in merit order, starting with the highest-emitting fuel:

Displacement Calculation
\[ \text{additional\_clean\_TWh} = \frac{(\text{clean\%} - \text{baseline\_clean\%})}{100} \times D_{\text{TWh}} \] \[ \text{coal\_displaced} = \min(\text{coal\_cap}, \text{additional\_clean\_TWh}) \] \[ \text{oil\_displaced} = \min(\text{oil\_cap}, \text{remaining}) \] \[ \text{gas\_displaced} = \text{remaining} - \text{oil\_displaced} \]

Retirement threshold: Above 70% clean energy, all coal and oil are considered fully retired from the fossil fleet. Only gas remains as the marginal fuel.

Weighted Displaced Emission Rate
\[ r_{\text{displaced}} = \frac{\sum_f (\text{displaced}_f \times r_f)}{\sum_f \text{displaced}_f} \]

where \(f \in \{\text{coal, oil, gas}\}\) and \(r_f\) is the fuel-specific emission rate in tCO₂/MWh.

Known limitation: Actual retirement timing depends on policy (state clean energy standards, EPA regulations), plant age, stranded asset economics, and labor considerations — not purely economic merit order. The model captures the directional effect correctly but does not predict the specific year each plant retires.

9. Known Limitations & Scope Boundaries

Honest assessment of what the model does not capture

  1. 2025 Snapshot Model — No forward projections or demand growth forecasts. Demand growth is modeled as a parametric multiplier (1.0×–2.5×) in sensitivity analysis, not as a time series. The model shows the 2025 cost landscape, not a 2030 or 2040 prediction.
  2. Greedy Storage Dispatch — Battery and LDES use a greedy heuristic (charge from largest surplus, discharge to largest gap) instead of a perfect-foresight linear program. This conservative bias understates storage value by an estimated 5–15% for LDES, 2–5% for daily-cycle batteries. Actual storage economics are better than modeled.
  3. No Transmission Congestion — Transmission is modeled as a flat $/MWh adder per resource type per ISO, not as a nodal network with congestion pricing. This simplification means the model doesn't capture locational marginal costs within an ISO (e.g., West Texas wind vs. Dallas load).
  4. No Weather Year Sensitivity — Generation profiles use a single representative year (2025) or 4-year average. No Monte Carlo weather uncertainty analysis across historical weather years. Extreme events (polar vortex, drought) are not stress-tested.
  5. Merit-Order Retirement is Directional — The CO₂ model assumes clean energy displaces the dirtiest fossil fuel first (coal → oil → gas). Actual retirement timing depends on policy, plant age, fuel contracts, and stranded asset economics. The directional effect is correct but timing is approximate.
  6. CCS Deployment Risk — Regional CCS caps (0–200 TWh/yr) are based on the NETL Carbon Storage Atlas's geological potential, not permitted or financed capacity. Actual CCS deployment may be significantly lower due to permitting, public opposition, and technology risk.
  7. Geothermal Cap (39 TWh CAISO) — Assumes enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) at commercial scale. Fervo Energy's pilot is still pre-commercial. The 5 GW cap represents USGS's identified hydrothermal + EGS potential, not deployed capacity.
  8. No Electricity Market Feedback — The model doesn't capture price suppression from renewable buildout (the "merit-order effect"). Large-scale solar/wind deployment reduces wholesale electricity prices, which could change the economics of incumbent generators.
  9. Battery Degradation Not Modeled — Assumes constant 85% round-trip efficiency over the battery lifetime. Real Li-ion batteries degrade 2–3% per year in capacity and efficiency. This slightly overstates battery value in later years of a 20-year project life.
  10. Single Demand Shape — Load profiles are a representative year, not worst-case. Extreme heat waves or polar vortexes that stress the grid are not separately modeled. The demand profile captures average seasonal patterns but not tail-risk events.
  11. CCS Capture Rate Fixed at 90% — The model assumes 90% CO₂ capture (residual 0.037 tCO₂/MWh). Real-world performance varies: Boundary Dam achieved ~92% (design), Gorgon averaged ~60% (operational issues). The assumed 90% is aspirational but within commercial targets.

10. Design Decision Justifications

Why the model is built this way, and what alternatives were considered

10.1 Why Co-Optimize Cost + Resource Mix?

Different cost assumptions produce different optimal resource mixes. A model that optimizes the mix at "medium" costs and then recalculates cost under "low" or "high" assumptions would miss the interaction: cheap CCS makes higher nuclear allocations suboptimal, while expensive transmission makes remote wind less competitive. Co-optimization is the core scientific contribution — it shows that the answer to "what's the cheapest clean portfolio?" depends on which cost assumptions you believe.

Alternative considered: Optimize mix once at medium costs, then re-price under all scenarios. Rejected because it decouples the key interaction between cost assumptions and optimal resource allocation.

10.2 Why 5,832 Cost Scenarios?

The model sweeps 8 sensitivity toggles: Renewable Gen (L/M/H), Firm Gen (L/M/H), Storage (L/M/H), Fossil Fuel (L/M/H), Transmission (N/L/M/H), CCS (L/M/H), 45Q (On/Off), and Geothermal (CAISO only, L/M/H). This produces 36 × 4 × 2 = 5,832 combinations per ISO (17,496 for CAISO with geothermal). Paired toggles (e.g., solar+wind move together under "Renewable Gen") reduce dimensionality from 10+ individual parameters while reflecting real-world cost correlations — when solar gets cheaper, wind usually does too.

10.3 Why 21 Thresholds?

The threshold grid is designed to capture the key features of the cost-vs-clean-energy curve:

  • 10–40%: Coarse 10% steps — existing fleet handles most of this range, costs are near-zero
  • 50–80%: 5% steps — the "buildout zone" where new resources start being needed
  • 85–97.5%: 2.5% steps — the "inflection zone" where costs accelerate nonlinearly
  • 99–99.99%: Fine steps — the "last mile" where costs explode due to extreme reliability requirements

10.4 Why ≥99.99% Instead of 100%?

True 100% hourly matching is physically unreachable with variable renewables — achieving it would require infinite storage or curtailment. The top threshold is ≥99.99% (8,759.1 of 8,760 hours matched), which represents the practical ceiling. Displaying "100%" would be scientifically misleading.

10.5 Why Existing Fleet at $0?

Existing nuclear, hydro, solar, and wind capacity is sunk cost. The marginal cost of continued operation is near-zero (no fuel cost for renewables/hydro, minimal fuel cost for nuclear). This is the standard approach in capacity expansion models including NREL's Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS), MIT's GenX, and Princeton's ZERO Lab REPEAT model. The model evaluates incremental cost of higher clean energy, not total system cost.

10.6 Why Greedy Dispatch Instead of LP?

The model evaluates 7M+ resource mixes × 8,760 hours × 4+ storage technologies. A linear program per mix would take ~1 second; total = 80+ compute-days. The greedy heuristic takes <1ms per mix, completing 7M mixes in hours. The greedy approach is conservative — it understates storage value, meaning the true optimal cost is lower than what the model reports. This is a deliberate design choice: we'd rather understate the benefit of storage than overstate it.

Alternative considered: LP dispatch via CVXPY/Gurobi. Rejected due to 100× computational cost. Could be implemented as a post-hoc refinement on the Pareto frontier (~1,000 mixes) rather than the full grid (~7M mixes).

10.7 Why Hourly Matching (Not Annual)?

Annual matching (buying RECs equal to annual consumption) ignores temporal mismatch: solar generates during the day but demand peaks in the evening; wind is seasonal. Hourly matching forces the model to solve the hardest hours — midnight in winter, cloudy weeks, heat domes — which drives the need for storage, firm power, and diverse resource mixes. This is the methodological foundation of the 24/7 CFE movement and the reason the project is called "The 8,760 Problem."
