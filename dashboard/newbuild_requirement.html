Would You Build the Wrong Grid?

Procurement that ignores existing clean resources produces a fundamentally different — and more expensive — portfolio.
Here's how the resource mix changes when you don't account for what's already on the grid.

The Greenfield Distortion

Same Clean Energy Target. Radically Different Investment Decisions.

Every ISO already has nuclear plants, hydroelectric dams, and legacy renewables generating clean energy around the clock. When a procurement strategy ignores these resources — optimizing as if starting from zero — it produces a portfolio that over-invests in firm generation and under-invests in the renewables that would actually fill the remaining gap.

Without Existing Nuclear, the Optimizer Over-Buys Firm

Remove existing nuclear and hydro from the optimization, and the model's cheapest path to hourly matching is new firm generation. Clean firm provides 24/7 coverage without storage, so starting from zero, the optimizer allocates 70–96% of the portfolio to firm resources (nuclear uprates, geothermal, new nuclear).

But no buyer actually starts from zero. Grids with existing nuclear already have nighttime and baseload coverage. The marginal need is for daytime solar, variable wind, and modest firm top-ups — not a full firm rebuild. Ignoring this produces a portfolio that duplicates existing capacity.

The Gap Widens at Higher Targets

At low thresholds (50–70%), the greenfield distortion is already significant: the optimizer is building massive firm capacity that the baseline approach doesn't need because existing resources already cover it. As the clean energy target rises to 85–95%, the greenfield approach doubles down on firm — while the baseline diversifies into solar and wind to fill the daytime gap.

The mismatch matters because these are different capital allocation signals. One says "build nuclear." The other says "build solar + wind with firm at the margin." Both hit the same target, but with radically different investment implications and cost profiles.

Regional Grids, Regional Answers

The distortion is most severe in regions with large existing clean fleets. PJM (32% nuclear) and NYISO (18% nuclear + 16% hydro) see massive over-procurement of firm in the greenfield case because their existing fleets already provide substantial clean coverage that the greenfield optimizer doesn't see.

ERCOT is the counterexample: with only 8.6% nuclear, its greenfield portfolio (dominated by wind) looks closer to the baseline. The lesson: the more clean generation you already have, the more wrong a greenfield approach gets.

Your Starting Point Determines Your Investment Path

Procurement strategies that ignore what's already on the grid don't just cost more — they direct capital into the wrong resources entirely.

Two Portfolios, One Target, Opposite Signals

A buyer accounting for existing nuclear and hydro builds a balanced portfolio: solar and wind for daytime generation, modest firm additions for nighttime gaps. A buyer ignoring those assets builds a firm-heavy portfolio that duplicates what the grid already has — paying new-build prices for capacity that's already generating at wholesale cost.

The difference isn't marginal. At 90% clean in CAISO, the greenfield approach allocates 76% to firm vs. 62% in the baseline. That 14-point gap represents billions in misallocated capital — money that would build nuclear capacity the grid doesn't need instead of the renewables it does.

Same target, opposite capital allocation signals

The Policy Implication

Clean energy mandates and corporate procurement programs that don't account for existing grid resources will systematically over-invest in firm generation and under-invest in renewables. This isn't an academic distinction — it determines whether tens of billions in annual clean energy spending goes to the technologies that actually close the gap or duplicates what's already built.

The fix is straightforward: any procurement optimization must use the existing clean generation profile as its starting point. Zeroing out existing resources doesn't create a "conservative" or "worst-case" scenario — it creates a wrong one.

Sources & Methodology

New-build track methodology: The optimizer was run with all existing clean generation set to zero — no nuclear, no hydro, no legacy renewables — to isolate the greenfield case. This simulates what a buyer would procure if they treated the grid as a blank slate. Nuclear uprate tranches remain active as new-build options. Results (Track 2) are compared against the baseline track (Track 1, which builds on top of existing generation) to quantify how the resource mix and cost change when you ignore what's already on the grid.
