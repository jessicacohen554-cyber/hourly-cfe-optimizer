About This Model

This model is a full-year, hourly-resolution clean energy procurement optimizer that co-optimizes resource mix and cost across every hour of the year. It evaluates millions of resource combinations under thousands of cost scenarios to find the most cost-effective paths to any hourly clean energy matching target—from 10% to ≥99.99%.

1. The Problem: Why Hourly Matching Is Hard

Most clean energy procurement today operates on an annual matching basis: buy enough clean energy over the year to offset total consumption. This approach is simple but masks a fundamental temporal mismatch—solar doesn't generate at night, wind is intermittent, and demand is constant.

Hourly matching requires that clean energy supply meets or exceeds demand in every single hour of the year—all 8,760 of them. This is an exponentially harder problem. Each hour has different solar irradiance, wind speeds, hydro availability, and demand levels. The resource mix that works at 75% hourly matching looks fundamentally different from the mix needed at 95% or 99%.

This optimizer was built to answer a deceptively simple question: what does it actually cost to match clean energy to demand on an hourly basis, and what's the most cost-effective way to get there?

The answer depends on where you are, what clean resources already exist on your grid, what you're willing to pay for new capacity, and how close to 100% you need to get. This model captures all of those dimensions simultaneously.

2. Model Scope & Scale

The optimizer operates at a scale designed to capture the full complexity of the hourly matching problem without taking analytical shortcuts.

8,760 Hours per year
7 US ISO/RTO regions
21 CFE thresholds
5,832+ Cost scenarios per region

Geographic Coverage

The model covers all seven major US Independent System Operators and Regional Transmission Organizations: CAISO, ERCOT, PJM, NYISO, NEISO, MISO, and SPP. Each region has distinct generation profiles, existing clean energy mixes, wholesale market conditions, and transmission characteristics that produce meaningfully different optimal resource portfolios.

Threshold Coverage

The model evaluates 21 hourly matching targets spanning the full range from 10% to ≥99.99%. Threshold spacing is tighter in the critical inflection zones (85–97.5%) where cost curves steepen, and finest in the "last mile" (99–≥99.99%) where each additional fraction of a percent demands disproportionate resources. The top target is ≥99.99%—true 100% hourly matching is physically unreachable due to irreducible weather variability.

Sensitivity Analysis

Cost is not a single number—it depends on technology costs, fuel prices, transmission availability, and policy assumptions. The model evaluates each resource mix under 5,832 distinct cost scenarios per region (17,496 for CAISO, which includes geothermal as an additional dimension). These scenarios are generated from 8 sensitivity toggle groups spanning renewable generation costs, firm generation costs, storage costs, fossil fuel prices, transmission costs, CCS assumptions, the 45Q tax credit, and region-specific factors.

All toggles use a Low / Medium / High framework grounded in published cost ranges from NREL, Lazard, and LBNL.

3. Resources Modeled

The optimizer evaluates portfolios drawn from the full spectrum of clean energy technologies, each with technology-appropriate temporal profiles and dispatch characteristics.

Solar — Variable, daytime generation
Wind — Variable, often strongest at night
Hydro — Seasonal, existing-only
Clean Firm — Nuclear & geothermal baseload
CCS-CCGT — Dispatchable with carbon capture
Battery — Short-duration daily-cycle storage
LDES — Long-duration multi-day storage
Green H₂ — Ultra-long-duration seasonal storage
Geothermal — CAISO-only, flat year-round

Each resource type is modeled with region-specific hourly generation profiles derived from actual grid data. Storage technologies use technology-appropriate dispatch constraints reflecting real-world operational characteristics—daily cycling for batteries, multi-day arbitrage for LDES, and seasonal balancing for Green H₂.

The model distinguishes between existing clean resources already on the grid (priced at wholesale market rates) and new-build capacity (priced at regionalized levelized costs plus applicable transmission). This two-tier approach reflects the reality that procurement up to the existing clean share of the grid is fundamentally cheaper than building new dedicated capacity.

4. Analytical Pipeline

The model is implemented as a modular, multi-phase computational pipeline. Each phase builds on the outputs of prior phases, progressing from raw data through physics-based feasibility analysis to economic optimization and derived analytics.

  1. Data Acquisition & Preparation Automated ingestion, validation, and harmonization of hourly generation profiles, demand curves, emission factors, and market data from federal and state sources. Quality controls ensure temporal consistency and flag anomalies before they propagate.
  2. Physics-Based Feasibility Analysis Exhaustive evaluation of resource combinations to identify every portfolio capable of meeting each hourly matching threshold. This phase operates at full 8,760-hour resolution—no monthly or seasonal aggregation shortcuts—and evaluates millions of candidate mixes per region. Storage dispatch is simulated with technology-appropriate operational constraints at each candidate point.
  3. Economic Optimization & Sensitivity Feasible portfolios are evaluated under thousands of cost scenarios reflecting different assumptions about technology costs, fuel prices, policy environment, and transmission availability. The optimizer identifies the cost-minimizing resource mix for each scenario, producing a complete map of how optimal strategy shifts with economic conditions. Marginal abatement cost analysis, CO₂ displacement modeling, and market pricing impacts are computed from the optimized results.
  4. Derived Analytics & Scenario Comparison Cross-regional deployment analysis, procurement strategy comparison, storage utilization patterns, and EAC market scarcity assessment. This phase synthesizes upstream results into actionable insights about procurement pathways—comparing consequential, hourly, and annual matching strategies under different market conditions.
The physics-based feasibility phase is computationally intensive, running for hours on cloud infrastructure. All subsequent phases operate on cached physics results and complete in seconds to minutes—enabling rapid iteration on economic assumptions without re-running the physics.

5. Data Foundation

Every input to the model is grounded in published, peer-reviewed, or government-collected data. No proprietary datasets are used for generation profiles, demand curves, or cost benchmarks.

Hourly Generation Profiles

Solar, wind, hydro, nuclear, and fossil generation at hourly resolution for all 7 ISOs, sourced from EIA Hourly Electric Grid Monitor and cross-validated with Form 923 plant-level data.

Demand Curves

8,760-hour demand profiles by ISO from EIA, normalized and quality-controlled for chronological consistency and outlier detection.

Cost Benchmarks

Levelized costs from NREL Annual Technology Baseline, Lazard LCOE analysis, and LBNL interconnection studies. Regionalized with ISO-specific adjustments for resource quality, transmission distance, and market conditions.

Emission Factors

EPA eGRID subregion emission rates, fossil fuel mix data by ISO, and regional fuel-switching elasticities for dynamic CO₂ displacement modeling.

Market Data

Wholesale electricity prices, locational marginal pricing data, and state-level renewable portfolio standard requirements from DSIRE and ISO market reports.

Storage Parameters

Technology-specific round-trip efficiencies, duration ratings, and cost trajectories from NREL ATB and published technology assessments for Li-ion, iron-air, and hydrogen storage systems.

6. Analytical Outputs

The model produces a comprehensive set of results that together answer the key questions facing clean energy procurement decision-makers.

Cost Optimization Results

For every combination of ISO, CFE threshold, and cost scenario: the optimal resource mix, total procurement cost, cost per MWh of matched demand, curtailment rates, and the contribution of each resource to hourly matching. These results power the interactive Cost Optimizer dashboard, where users can adjust sensitivity toggles and see how optimal strategy shifts in real time.

CO₂ Abatement Analysis

Marginal and average CO₂ abatement costs at each threshold, benchmarked against alternative decarbonization pathways (direct air capture, sustainable aviation fuel, bioenergy with CCS). Includes dispatch-based emission displacement modeling that accounts for which fossil fuels are actually retired as clean energy penetration increases.

Storage Dispatch Analysis

Hourly dispatch profiles for battery and long-duration storage, showing charge/discharge patterns, capacity utilization, and the role of storage in bridging temporal gaps between supply and demand at different CFE thresholds.

EAC Market Scarcity Assessment

Analysis of energy attribute certificate supply and demand balance across ISOs, incorporating state RPS requirements, corporate procurement activity, and the temporal structure of clean energy surplus and deficit hours. Identifies regions where hourly matching at scale may face supply constraints.

Procurement Strategy Comparison

Side-by-side evaluation of consequential, hourly, and annual matching procurement strategies under different market conditions, emission accounting frameworks, and corporate ambition levels. Includes deployment pathway analysis showing how procurement decisions aggregate across regions.

Synthetic LMP Analysis

Merit-order-based wholesale price modeling showing how clean energy procurement at scale affects locational marginal prices, and the feedback between procurement cost and market impact.

7. Methodological Rigor

This model was built to withstand academic scrutiny. Key design choices that distinguish it from simpler clean energy planning tools:

Full 8,760-Hour Resolution

No aggregation to monthly or seasonal averages. Every hour of the year is evaluated independently, preserving the temporal variability that drives the cost of hourly matching.

Cost-Mix Co-Optimization

Cost assumptions and resource mix are optimized simultaneously—not sequentially. Different cost assumptions produce different optimal mixes. This co-optimization is the model's core contribution.

Exhaustive Feasibility Search

The physics layer evaluates millions of candidate resource portfolios per region, ensuring the optimizer doesn't miss non-obvious solutions. Sub-optimality bounds are validated to confirm results are within a narrow tolerance of the true optimum.

Region-Specific Cost Structures

Every cost parameter—generation, transmission, storage, fuel—is regionalized to reflect actual market conditions, resource quality, and infrastructure constraints in each ISO.

Multi-Technology Storage Dispatch

Three storage technologies (short-duration, long-duration, and ultra-long-duration) with technology-appropriate dispatch algorithms that respect operational constraints including round-trip efficiency losses and duration limits.

Peer-Reviewed Data Inputs

All cost benchmarks, generation profiles, and emission factors sourced from published government and research institution datasets (NREL ATB, EIA, EPA eGRID, Lazard, LBNL).

8. Assumptions & Limitations

Transparency about model boundaries is essential for proper interpretation of results.

Demand Growth Projections (Tracks 2 & 3)

The new-build (Track 2) and cost-to-replace (Track 3) analyses model demand growth over a 25-year horizon (2026–2050), with each CFE threshold mapped to a target year via SBTi milestones. Demand compounds annually as (1 + rate)years, where years = target_year − 2025. Existing clean generation remains constant in absolute TWh; its share of total generation declines as demand grows.

Growth rates are ISO-specific and set at three levels (Low / Medium / High), calibrated from EIA AEO 2025 reference and side cases, NERC 2024 LTRA, and ISO/RTO planning forecasts:

ISOLow (%/yr)Medium (%/yr)High (%/yr)
CAISO1.41.92.5
ERCOT2.03.55.5
PJM1.52.43.6
NYISO1.32.04.4
NEISO0.91.82.9
MISO1.22.23.8
SPP1.01.83.0

Low reflects baseline economic/population growth (EIA Low Economic Growth case, ~1.2% GDP CAGR). Medium incorporates confirmed large-load requests (data centers, industrial reshoring) anchored to the EIA AEO 2025 Reference Case (~1.6% national electricity CAGR), regionalized using ISO planning data. High assumes full realization of data center/AI load growth plus accelerated electrification (economy-wide net-zero pathway); ERCOT's 5.5% aligns with recent ERCOT board projections of near-term demand doubling.

Key Assumptions

  • 2025 snapshot model — generation profiles and grid mix reflect current conditions. No forward projections of capacity additions or retirements, except where learning curves are explicitly modeled for new-build cost trajectories.
  • ISO-level geographic resolution — resources are sourced within the buyer's ISO/RTO region. No intra-ISO transmission constraints or nodal pricing.
  • Hydro is existing-only — no new hydroelectric capacity is modeled. Existing hydro is available at wholesale market rates with no incremental transmission cost.
  • No incrementality requirement — buyers can claim existing clean generation via EAC procurement. If additionality were required, costs would increase significantly at lower thresholds.
  • Flat corporate load profile — demand is modeled as approximately flat baseload, representative of data center and large commercial loads.

Known Limitations

  • Static supply model — does not account for price-induced supply responses. In reality, high EAC prices would stimulate new clean energy investment.
  • No cross-ISO interactions — each ISO is modeled independently. Scarcity in one region could drive inter-regional EAC trade or load migration.
  • Weather year sensitivity — results are based on a single representative year of generation profiles. Multi-year weather variability could affect optimal mix composition.
  • Policy evolution — state RPS targets, federal tax credits, and GHG Protocol requirements continue to evolve. Results reflect current policy as of early 2025.
  • Interconnection queue constraints — the model assumes new capacity can be built as needed, without modeling interconnection queue delays or developer competition effects.
Interpreting results: This model identifies the theoretical cost-optimal resource mix under specified assumptions. Real-world procurement involves additional factors—developer availability, interconnection timelines, contract structure, and counterparty risk—that may shift actual portfolios from the modeled optimum.
