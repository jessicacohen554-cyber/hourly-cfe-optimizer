About This Model

This model is a full-year, hourly-resolution clean energy procurement optimizer that co-optimizes resource mix and cost across every hour of the year. It evaluates millions of resource combinations under thousands of cost scenarios to find the most cost-effective paths to any hourly clean energy matching target—from 10% to ≥99.99%.

1. The Problem: Why Hourly Matching Is Hard

Most clean energy procurement today operates on an annual matching basis: buy enough clean energy over the year to offset total consumption. This approach is simple but masks a fundamental temporal mismatch—solar doesn't generate at night, wind is intermittent, and demand is constant.

Hourly matching requires that clean energy supply meets or exceeds demand in every single hour of the year—all 8,760 of them. This is an exponentially harder problem. Each hour has different solar irradiance, wind speeds, hydro availability, and demand levels. The resource mix that works at 75% hourly matching looks fundamentally different from the mix needed at 95% or 99%.

This optimizer was built to answer a deceptively simple question: what does it actually cost to match clean energy to demand on an hourly basis, and what's the most cost-effective way to get there?

The answer depends on where you are, what clean resources already exist on your grid, what you're willing to pay for new capacity, and how close to 100% you need to get. This model captures all of those dimensions simultaneously.

2. Scope & Intent

This model is a high-resolution procurement screening and scenario exploration tool designed for corporate buyers evaluating hourly clean energy matching strategies. It answers the question: given a target hourly clean energy percentage, what is the least-cost resource portfolio to achieve it, and how does that cost change under different technology cost and policy assumptions?

What This Model Is

  • A procurement decision support tool — optimized for the question corporate buyers actually face: what portfolio of clean energy resources minimizes my hourly matching cost?
  • A scenario exploration framework — evaluating ~5.87 million unique resource mixes under 5,832–17,496 cost scenarios per region, providing uncertainty coverage orders of magnitude beyond standard capacity expansion models
  • A transparency engine — every feasible mix is visible and explorable, avoiding the “black box” problem of mathematical optimization where stakeholders must trust a solver’s single answer

What This Model Is Not

  • Not a capacity expansion model — it does not endogenously determine what to build or when. Models like GenX, PyPSA, ReEDS, and US-REGEN solve that problem with endogenous investment decisions, multi-period dynamics, and transmission network modeling.
  • Not a transmission planning tool — each ISO is modeled as a single zone. Intra-ISO congestion, inter-regional power flows, and nodal pricing are not captured.
  • Not a grid reliability simulator — operating reserves, unit commitment constraints, and real-time dispatch are modeled partially (nuclear must-run, coal minimum generation) but not at full fidelity.
  • Not a forecast — it does not predict future electricity prices, technology adoption timelines, or policy outcomes. It is a parametric snapshot showing the 2025 cost landscape under thousands of scenario combinations.

Complementary Role

This model and capacity expansion models answer different questions. CE models generate the technology cost projections and system-level deployment trajectories that inform this optimizer’s input assumptions (LCOE ranges, transmission costs, capacity factors). This optimizer then applies those assumptions to the specific problem of hourly load matching—a procurement-scale question that system models are not designed to answer at the temporal granularity required. The two approaches are complementary, not competing.

The optimizer operates at a scale designed to capture the full complexity of the hourly matching problem without taking analytical shortcuts.

Geographic Coverage

The model covers all seven major US Independent System Operators and Regional Transmission Organizations: CAISO, ERCOT, PJM, NYISO, NEISO, MISO, and SPP. Each region has distinct generation profiles, existing clean energy mixes, wholesale market conditions, and transmission characteristics that produce meaningfully different optimal resource portfolios.

Threshold Coverage

The model evaluates 21 hourly matching targets spanning the full range from 10% to ≥99.99%. Threshold spacing is tighter in the critical inflection zones (85–97.5%) where cost curves steepen, and finest in the "last mile" (99–≥99.99%) where each additional fraction of a percent demands disproportionate resources. The top target is ≥99.99%—true 100% hourly matching is physically unreachable due to irreducible weather variability.

Sensitivity Analysis

Cost is not a single number—it depends on technology costs, fuel prices, transmission availability, and policy assumptions. The model evaluates each resource mix under 5,832 distinct cost scenarios per region (17,496 for CAISO, which includes geothermal as an additional dimension). These scenarios are generated from 8 sensitivity toggle groups spanning renewable generation costs, firm generation costs, storage costs, fossil fuel prices, transmission costs, CCS assumptions, the 45Q tax credit, and region-specific factors.

All toggles use a Low / Medium / High framework grounded in published cost ranges from NREL, Lazard, and LBNL.

4. Resources Modeled

The optimizer evaluates portfolios drawn from the full spectrum of clean energy technologies, each with technology-appropriate temporal profiles and dispatch characteristics.

Battery — Short-duration daily-cycle storage
LDES — Long-duration multi-day storage
Green H₂ — Ultra-long-duration seasonal storage
Geothermal — CAISO-only, flat year-round

Each resource type is modeled with region-specific hourly generation profiles derived from actual grid data. Storage technologies use technology-appropriate dispatch constraints reflecting real-world operational characteristics—daily cycling for batteries, multi-day arbitrage for LDES, and seasonal balancing for Green H₂.

The model distinguishes between existing clean resources already on the grid (priced at wholesale market rates) and new-build capacity (priced at regionalized levelized costs plus applicable transmission). This two-tier approach reflects the reality that procurement up to the existing clean share of the grid is fundamentally cheaper than building new dedicated capacity.

5. Analytical Pipeline

The model is implemented as a modular, multi-phase computational pipeline. Each phase builds on the outputs of prior phases, progressing from raw data through physics-based feasibility analysis to economic optimization and derived analytics.

Phase 1: Multi-Stage Adaptive Physics Search

The physics layer identifies every resource portfolio capable of achieving each hourly matching threshold. Rather than a single uniform grid search, it uses a six-stage adaptive pipeline that progressively refines resolution where it matters most:

  1. Coarse grid generation — Resource fractions are varied independently in 5-percentage-point steps across 4–6 dimensions depending on the ISO: clean firm (0–120%), solar (0–100%), wind (0–250%), hydro (0–existing cap), plus offshore wind for coastal ISOs and geothermal for CAISO. Combinations filtered to sum > 0 and total procurement ≤ 350% of demand.
  2. 8,760-hour scoring — Every coarse combination is dispatched against full hourly resolution generation and demand profiles. No monthly or seasonal aggregation. Each mix’s score is sum(min(demandh, supplyh)) across all 8,760 hours.
  3. Zone search — 1-percentage-point refinement around promising regions identified from the coarse grid, capturing near-optimal solutions between grid points.
  4. Floor-aware augmentation — Ensures adequate coverage at 50–80% thresholds, where the coarse grid may miss transition-zone portfolios.
  5. Fine-grid PFS — Dense coverage at 40–75% thresholds with finer increments to map the inflection zone where cost curves begin steepening.
  6. Storage refinement — Battery (4hr & 8hr Li-ion, 85% RTE), LDES (100hr iron-air, 50% RTE), and Green H₂ (1000hr, 35% RTE) dispatch is simulated with technology-appropriate operational constraints: daily cycling for batteries, 7-day rolling windows for LDES, 30-day windows for H₂. Storage grids are swept independently per base mix to find optimal dispatch allocation.

Phase 2: Efficient Frontier Extraction

The raw physics output (tens of millions of scored mixes) is reduced to a non-dominated efficient frontier via three-phase filtering:

  1. Threshold gate — Retain only mixes meeting each target threshold.
  2. Resource cap filter — Enforce realistic bounds (hydro ≤ existing capacity, solar ≤ 100%, total procurement ≤ 350%).
  3. Global deduplication — For each unique resource + storage dispatch combination, keep only the highest-scoring instance.

The resulting efficient frontier contains ~5.87 million unique mixes across all 7 ISOs:

Phase 3: Vectorized Cost Optimization

Every efficient frontier mix is evaluated under 5,832 cost scenarios per region (17,496 for CAISO). The 8 sensitivity toggle groups—renewable generation costs, firm generation costs, storage costs, fossil fuel prices, transmission costs, CCS technology, 45Q tax credit, and geothermal (CAISO only)—generate the full combinatorial scenario space. A Numba JIT-compiled vectorized engine evaluates the coefficient matrix × price vector product across all mixes and scenarios simultaneously, with per-threshold bucketed argmin to find the cheapest mix per scenario per threshold. Typically ~100–2,000 unique winning “archetypes” per ISO emerge from billions of cost evaluations.

Phase 4: Derived Analytics & Scenario Comparison

Cross-regional deployment analysis, procurement strategy comparison, storage utilization patterns, and EAC market scarcity assessment. This phase synthesizes upstream results into actionable insights about procurement pathways—comparing consequential, hourly, and annual matching strategies under different market conditions. Marginal abatement cost analysis, CO₂ displacement modeling, and market pricing impacts are computed from the optimized results.

Phase 5: Market Dynamics & Retirement Risk

Built on the SMARTargets methodology, this phase extends the model beyond static optimization into probabilistic market simulation. An enhanced parametric design evaluates 540+ market scenarios—spanning demand growth rates, fuel price trajectories, PPA structures, gas friction assumptions, and IRA policy toggle (on/off)—alongside 10 structured net-zero transition pathways through 2050. The simulation incorporates announced coal retirements (EIA 860 schedule through 2035), transmission constraint modeling, RGGI carbon pricing for northeast ISOs, state RPS deployment floors, and a regulatory feedback mechanism that dampens unrealistic sustained high LMP. This probabilistic approach produces confidence distributions rather than point estimates.

The physics-based feasibility phase (Phase 1) is computationally intensive, running for hours on cloud infrastructure. All subsequent phases operate on cached physics results and complete in seconds to minutes—enabling rapid iteration on economic assumptions without re-running the physics.

6. Data Foundation

Every input to the model is grounded in published, peer-reviewed, or government-collected data. No proprietary datasets are used for generation profiles, demand curves, or cost benchmarks.

Hourly Generation Profiles

Solar, wind, hydro, nuclear, and fossil generation at hourly resolution for all 7 ISOs, sourced from EIA Hourly Electric Grid Monitor (Form 930) and cross-validated with Form 923 plant-level data. Profile shapes are 5-year element-wise averages (2021–2025)—each hour’s normalized generation is averaged across all five years independently, then re-normalized. This smooths single-year weather anomalies while preserving diurnal and seasonal patterns. Absolute generation quantities are from 2025 actuals. Individual year profiles are preserved for weather-year sensitivity analysis.

Demand Curves

8,760-hour demand profiles by ISO from EIA, using the same 5-year element-wise averaging methodology as generation profiles. Demand shapes averaged across 2021–2025; absolute quantities (peak MW, annual MWh) from 2025 actuals. Normalized and quality-controlled for chronological consistency and outlier detection.

Cost Benchmarks

Levelized costs from NREL Annual Technology Baseline, Lazard LCOE analysis, and LBNL interconnection studies. Regionalized with ISO-specific adjustments for resource quality, transmission distance, and market conditions.

Emission Factors

EPA eGRID subregion emission rates, fossil fuel mix data by ISO, and regional fuel-switching elasticities for dynamic CO₂ displacement modeling.

Market Data

Wholesale electricity prices, locational marginal pricing data, and state-level renewable portfolio standard requirements from DSIRE and ISO market reports.

Storage Parameters

Technology-specific round-trip efficiencies, duration ratings, and cost trajectories from NREL ATB and published technology assessments for Li-ion, iron-air, and hydrogen storage systems.

7. Analytical Outputs

The model produces a comprehensive set of results that together answer the key questions facing clean energy procurement decision-makers.

Cost Optimization Results

For every combination of ISO, CFE threshold, and cost scenario: the optimal resource mix, total procurement cost, cost per MWh of matched demand, curtailment rates, and the contribution of each resource to hourly matching. These results power the interactive Cost Optimizer dashboard, where users can adjust sensitivity toggles and see how optimal strategy shifts in real time.

CO₂ Abatement Analysis

Marginal and average CO₂ abatement costs at each threshold, benchmarked against alternative decarbonization pathways (direct air capture, sustainable aviation fuel, bioenergy with CCS). Includes dispatch-based emission displacement modeling that accounts for which fossil fuels are actually retired as clean energy penetration increases.

Storage Dispatch Analysis

Hourly dispatch profiles for battery and long-duration storage, showing charge/discharge patterns, capacity utilization, and the role of storage in bridging temporal gaps between supply and demand at different CFE thresholds.

EAC Market Scarcity Assessment

Analysis of energy attribute certificate supply and demand balance across ISOs, incorporating state RPS requirements, corporate procurement activity, and the temporal structure of clean energy surplus and deficit hours. Identifies regions where hourly matching at scale may face supply constraints.

Procurement Strategy Comparison

Side-by-side evaluation of consequential, hourly, and annual matching procurement strategies under different market conditions, emission accounting frameworks, and corporate ambition levels. Includes deployment pathway analysis showing how procurement decisions aggregate across regions.

Synthetic LMP Analysis

Merit-order-based wholesale price modeling showing how clean energy procurement at scale affects locational marginal prices, and the feedback between procurement cost and market impact. The LMP engine includes three pricing layers:

  • Merit-order base — marginal cost from dispatch stack, reflecting fuel costs, heat rates, and variable O&M for each unit in the regional fossil fleet.
  • Unit commitment / must-run floor — nuclear operates at 100% must-run; coal steam generators have a 40% minimum stable generation constraint. When residual demand falls below the total must-run floor, prices are depressed by surplus generation—capturing the off-peak price dynamics observed in real markets (e.g., PJM off-peak ~$28/MWh).
  • Scarcity pricing — ORDC-based pricing with ISO-specific value-of-lost-load caps ($5,000/MWh ERCOT ORDC, $2,000/MWh capacity market ISOs). Triggers when reserve margins fall below reliability thresholds.

A regulatory feedback mechanism dampens sustained high LMP at high clean penetration. When rolling 3-year average LMP exceeds 2× the ISO's 2023 baseline, a capacity mechanism reform is triggered—reducing scarcity hours for energy-only ISOs (ERCOT, SPP) and increasing capacity procurement for capacity-market ISOs (PJM, NYISO, NEISO, MISO). This prevents unrealistic sustained prices (e.g., $172/MWh ERCOT 2050 averages) by modeling the regulatory responses that real markets exhibit under price stress.

LMP component decomposition tracks merit-order base, scarcity, and demand-quantile adders separately, enabling apples-to-apples comparison against NREL Cambium's short-run marginal cost (SRMC) metric.

8. Methodological Rigor

This model was built to withstand academic scrutiny. Key design choices that distinguish it from simpler clean energy planning tools:

Full 8,760-Hour Resolution

No aggregation to monthly or seasonal averages. Every hour of the year is evaluated independently, preserving the temporal variability that drives the cost of hourly matching.

Cost-Mix Co-Optimization

Cost assumptions and resource mix are optimized simultaneously—not sequentially. Different cost assumptions produce different optimal mixes. This co-optimization is the model's core contribution.

Adaptive Multi-Stage Feasibility Search

A six-stage adaptive pipeline evaluates millions of candidate portfolios per region at progressively finer resolution (5% coarse → 1% zone search → storage sweep). The resulting ~5.87 million unique efficient frontier mixes across all ISOs ensure the optimizer doesn’t miss non-obvious solutions. The fine-grid refinement (1% steps in zone search) bounds the discretization gap between enumerated grid points and the continuous optimum.

Region-Specific Cost Structures

Every cost parameter—generation, transmission, storage, fuel—is regionalized to reflect actual market conditions, resource quality, and infrastructure constraints in each ISO.

Multi-Technology Storage Dispatch

Three storage technologies (short-duration, long-duration, and ultra-long-duration) with technology-appropriate dispatch algorithms that respect operational constraints including round-trip efficiency losses and duration limits.

Peer-Reviewed Data Inputs

All cost benchmarks, generation profiles, and emission factors sourced from published government and research institution datasets (NREL ATB, EIA, EPA eGRID, Lazard, LBNL).

10. Validation & Uncertainty

Weather-Year Sensitivity

Generation and demand profiles use 5-year element-wise averages (2021–2025), not a single weather year. This smooths year-to-year weather variability while preserving diurnal and seasonal patterns. To quantify the sensitivity of results to weather variation, the ~5.87 million efficient frontier mixes were re-scored against each individual year’s profiles independently. Results are reported as the change in hourly match score (in percentage points) relative to the 5-year average baseline.

Result: Weather-driven variation is small relative to scenario uncertainty. Across all 7 ISOs and all active thresholds (50–99.99%), the year-to-year spread in hourly match scores averages ±0.9 percentage points (median 0.9pp, max 1.6pp). This confirms that 5-year averaging produces robust baseline profiles.

These years exhibit anomalous demand patterns that are not representative of typical grid conditions and would distort the weather-sensitivity assessment. The remaining 4–5 years per ISO provide a robust basis for quantifying typical weather-driven variation. All scores reflect generation-only matching without storage re-dispatch; storage smoothing would further reduce year-to-year variance, making these reported spreads an upper bound on weather-driven uncertainty.

LMP Model Calibration

The synthetic LMP engine is calibrated against published 2024 market statistics from ISO market monitors (Monitoring Analytics for PJM, Potomac Economics for ERCOT/MISO/SPP, ISO-NE and CAISO Department of Market Monitoring). Calibration targets include load-weighted average LMP, P10/P50/P90 distribution, volatility, and frequency of negative-price and scarcity-price hours. These published statistics implicitly reflect weather variation across the actual 2024 operating year.

Storage Dispatch Conservatism

Battery and LDES use a greedy heuristic (charge from largest surplus, discharge to largest gap) rather than a perfect-foresight linear program. This conservative approach deliberately understates storage value by an estimated 2–5% for daily-cycle batteries and 5–15% for LDES. Actual storage economics are better than modeled—a deliberate bias toward understatement.

Enumeration Coverage

The adaptive multi-stage grid search evaluates mixes at 1–5% resource fraction steps. The true continuous optimum may lie between grid points. The fine-grid refinement (1% steps in zone search) bounds this discretization gap. For a buyer making a procurement commitment, the practical difference between the enumerated optimum and the true continuous optimum is small relative to the uncertainty captured by the 5,832-scenario sensitivity analysis.

Bottom line: Our model's clean energy penetration trajectories are directionally aligned with consensus forecasts in 60% of year-over-year comparisons. Near-term (2030) projections are close to EIA for most ISOs, but our market-only reference sweep consistently projects slower decarbonization than policy-inclusive benchmarks. The three most important findings from this validation:

  1. Our model captures scarcity pricing that capacity-expansion models miss. At high clean penetration (>70%), our hourly dispatch resolution shows wholesale prices rising — not falling — as fossil generation becomes scarce and expensive to maintain. EIA and NREL models, which optimize capacity over annual blocks, project steadily declining prices from VRE surplus. The truth likely falls between these views.
  2. Our reference sweep is a market-only scenario — no emission caps, no RPS mandates, no carbon price escalation. This is intentional: it shows what the market alone would deliver. Benchmarks that include IRA incentives, state policies, and EPA regulations naturally project faster decarbonization. The gap between our reference case and these policy-inclusive benchmarks is itself informative — it quantifies the policy premium.
  3. Cross-border clean imports matter for NYISO. Our model treats ISOs as independent grids, ignoring ~25 TWh/yr of Hydro-Québec clean energy imports into NYISO. This partly explains the NYISO clean penetration lag (our ~40% vs. benchmarks' 58–62% at 2030). We've implemented a correction for this in the model code (see Model Improvements below).

Where We Diverge — And Why

Three structural differences explain 90% of the deviation

1. The LMP Divergence at High Clean Penetration

This is the most striking finding. By 2040–2050, our model projects wholesale prices rising in most ISOs, while EIA and NREL project them falling or staying flat. In ERCOT, our unconstrained reference case projects P50 LMP reaching $172/MWh by 2050, versus EIA's $35 and Cambium's $10. However, our updated model now includes a regulatory feedback mechanism that caps effective average LMP at ~1.75× the 2023 baseline when rolling 3-year averages exceed 2× — modeling the capacity mechanism reforms that real ISOs implement under sustained price stress (e.g., ERCOT's ORDC reforms post-2021 freeze).

Why this happens: Our model includes hourly scarcity pricing. When clean penetration exceeds ~70%, the remaining fossil fleet operates fewer hours but at higher marginal cost (peakers, not baseload). Our ERCOT model uses ORDC-based pricing with a $5,000/MWh VOLL cap. A small number of scarcity hours can pull the annual average up dramatically. Capacity expansion models like ReEDS optimize away this scarcity by building "just enough" firm capacity — they assume perfect foresight and optimal buildout, which real markets don't achieve. The regulatory feedback mechanism now dampens the most extreme projections, but a gap remains between our hourly dispatch view and annual optimization models.

The truth falls between these views. Real markets will experience some scarcity pricing (our model captures this), and policy interventions will dampen sustained extremes (our regulatory feedback models this). The remaining gap with EIA/NREL quantifies the value of market design and the difference between hourly dispatch realism and annual capacity optimization.

2. The Emissions Gap: Market-Only vs. Policy-Inclusive

Our reference sweep emissions consistently exceed both EIA and NREL projections. In PJM, we project ~380 Mt CO₂ at 2050 versus EIA's 140 Mt and Cambium's 70 Mt. This is by design: our reference scenario answers "what would the market deliver without policy intervention?"

The benchmarks include IRA tax credits (driving economics), state RPS mandates (driving deployment minimums), RGGI/cap-and-trade (driving coal retirement), and EPA regulations (setting emission limits). Our reference sweep includes none of these policy drivers — it's pure market economics.

The policy premium: The gap between our market-only trajectory and policy-inclusive benchmarks quantifies the emissions reduction attributable to policy. For PJM, ~240 Mt CO₂ of the 2050 difference is policy-driven, not market-driven. This is a feature of our model design, not a flaw.

3. NYISO Early-Period Deployment Lag

Our model projects NYISO at ~40% clean by 2030 (P50), while both EIA (58%) and NREL (62%) project much higher levels. This is the widest near-term gap and has two root causes:

  1. Missing Hydro-Québec imports: NYISO imports ~25 TWh/yr of clean hydropower from Québec via HVDC ties — roughly a 15 percentage-point boost to clean penetration. Our model treated ISOs as independent grids and missed this entirely. We've now added an EXTERNAL_CLEAN_IMPORTS_TWH correction to the model (see Model Improvements below), which will close about half of the gap in the next sweep run.
  2. Missing state mandates: The benchmarks include NY CLCPA mandates (70% clean by 2030 state law), which drive rapid offshore wind and solar deployment. Our reference sweep, being market-only, doesn't enforce these mandates — and in pure market terms, NYISO's tight geography and high interconnection costs slow voluntary deployment.

The remaining deviation after the HQ import correction reflects the difference between a legal mandate (CLCPA) and market incentive alone. Our policy scenarios (IRA overlay with RPS floors) will close most of the residual gap.

Our Model's Edge

What hourly dispatch resolution reveals that annual models miss

Most published forecasts use capacity expansion models — they find the cost-optimal portfolio of generators to build, assuming perfect foresight and annual energy balance. Our model operates at 8,760-hour resolution, dispatching resources hour by hour against 5-year averaged demand and generation profiles (2021–2025 element-wise averages). This captures three dynamics that capacity expansion models smooth over:

VRE Price Suppression

When solar floods the grid midday, our model drives prices to zero or negative — matching observed CAISO duck curve dynamics. Annual models only capture the average effect, missing the volatility that makes storage valuable and shapes investment signals.

Scarcity Pricing

When wind dies down for days in ERCOT, our model dispatches expensive peakers and triggers ORDC scarcity pricing. This is how real markets work — and it reveals the true cost of the last 5% of decarbonization that capacity models understate.

Storage Dispatch Realism

Our model dispatches battery (4hr/8hr), LDES (100hr iron-air), and green H₂ (1000hr) with rolling-window algorithms against actual hourly profiles. This reveals when storage saturates, when it can't cover extended low-wind events, and the diminishing returns at high deployment.

ISO-Specific Market Design

ERCOT's energy-only ORDC produces fundamentally different price signals than PJM's RPM capacity market. Our model captures this — ERCOT shows extreme price spikes that PJM's capacity market prevents. Most national models use a single pricing framework.

What This Means

This validation exercise reveals that our model occupies a distinct and valuable position in the landscape of electricity market forecasts:

  1. Near-term reliability: Through 2035, our clean energy deployment projections align with consensus. Corporate procurement decisions based on our model are well-grounded.
  2. Long-term insight, not prediction: Our 2040-2050 projections shouldn't be read as "prices will reach $172/MWh in ERCOT." They should be read as "without policy intervention and capacity market reform, scarcity pricing dynamics could become severe." The benchmarks' lower prices assume policy and market design evolve to prevent this — our model shows what happens if they don't.
  3. The policy gap is quantifiable: The difference between our market-only reference and policy-inclusive benchmarks measures the emissions reduction attributable to policy. This is independently useful for policy analysis and corporate strategy.
Confidence level: High for near-term (2030-2035) resource deployment and cost analysis. Moderate for mid-term (2035-2040) market dynamics. Our 2040-2050 projections are scenario-dependent and should be interpreted as conditional forecasts, not predictions — which is exactly what they're designed to be.

12. Assumptions & Limitations

Transparency about model boundaries is essential for proper interpretation of results.

Demand Growth Projections (Tracks 2 & 3)

The new-build (Track 2) and cost-to-replace (Track 3) analyses model demand growth over a 25-year horizon (2026–2050), with each CFE threshold mapped to a target year via SBTi milestones. Demand compounds annually as (1 + rate)years, where years = target_year − 2025. Existing clean generation remains constant in absolute TWh; its share of total generation declines as demand grows.

Growth rates are ISO-specific and set at three levels (Low / Medium / High), calibrated from EIA AEO 2025 reference and side cases, NERC 2024 LTRA, and ISO/RTO planning forecasts:

Low reflects baseline economic/population growth (EIA Low Economic Growth case, ~1.2% GDP CAGR). High assumes full realization of data center/AI load growth plus accelerated electrification (economy-wide net-zero pathway); ERCOT's 5.5% aligns with recent ERCOT board projections of near-term demand doubling.

Key Assumptions

  • 2025 snapshot model with 5-year averaged profiles — generation and demand profile shapes are element-wise averages across 2021–2025 (smoothing single-year weather anomalies); absolute quantities (TWh, peak MW) are from 2025 actuals. No forward projections of capacity additions or retirements, except where learning curves are explicitly modeled for new-build cost trajectories. Per-year profiles are preserved for weather-year sensitivity analysis (see Section 10).
  • ISO-level geographic resolution — resources are sourced within the buyer's ISO/RTO region. No intra-ISO transmission constraints or nodal pricing.
  • Hydro is existing-only — no new hydroelectric capacity is modeled. Existing hydro is available at wholesale market rates with no incremental transmission cost.
  • No incrementality requirement — buyers can claim existing clean generation via EAC procurement. If additionality were required, costs would increase significantly at lower thresholds.
  • Flat corporate load profile — demand is modeled as approximately flat baseload, representative of data center and large commercial loads.

Known Limitations

  • Static supply model — does not account for price-induced supply responses. In reality, high EAC prices would stimulate new clean energy investment.
  • No cross-ISO interactions — each ISO is modeled independently. Brown & Botterud (2021) found that inter-state coordination and transmission expansion can reduce system costs by up to 46% in high-renewable scenarios. This limitation is appropriate for the procurement question (a buyer operates within one ISO) but means system-level cost estimates may overstate the true cost of hourly matching relative to a nationally coordinated approach. Scarcity in one region could also drive inter-regional EAC trade or load migration.
  • No intra-ISO transmission constraints — each ISO is modeled as a single zone (copper plate). Transmission costs are flat $/MWh adders rather than flow-constrained network optimization. Production models like GenX (DC optimal power flow) and EPRI's US-REGEN (zonal pipe-and-bubble) capture congestion-driven price separation within ISOs that can materially affect resource siting economics.
  • Partial unit commitment — the LMP engine enforces must-run / minimum-generation constraints (nuclear 100% must-run, coal steam 40% min stable output) that capture off-peak price depression dynamics. Full unit commitment with minimum up/down times, ramp rate limits, and start-up costs is not yet modeled—this may slightly understate cycling costs at high variable renewable penetration.
  • Reserve margin without hourly reserves — the model enforces a resource adequacy requirement (reserve margin) in cost optimization, but does not model spinning, non-spinning, or regulation reserves as hourly dispatch constraints. This captures planning-level reliability but not real-time ancillary service costs.
  • No demand-side flexibility — load is treated as perfectly inelastic. No demand response, load shifting, or flexible consumption (EV charging, data center workload scheduling) is modeled. The Brattle Group (2019) estimates US demand response capability could reach ~120 GW—equivalent to 10–20% of peak demand—with national benefits exceeding $15 billion/year. NREL analysis shows DR provides flexibility equivalent to ~1 GW of 6-hour battery storage per region. Ignoring this flexibility likely overstates supply-side resource requirements, particularly for storage and peaking capacity, by a comparable order of magnitude.
  • Single-sector scope — electricity-only analysis. Cross-sector interactions (transport electrification, building heating, industrial hydrogen demand) that affect electricity prices and resource availability are not captured.
  • Policy evolution — the model includes an IRA policy overlay (PTC/ITC incentives, RGGI carbon pricing, state RPS floors) as a toggleable sweep dimension, enabling comparison between market-only and policy-inclusive scenarios. However, policy parameters are static as of early 2025. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, signed July 2025) enacted an accelerated phaseout of IRA clean energy credits with differential treatment by technology: wind and solar PTC (§45Y) expires for projects not under construction by July 2026, while nuclear (§45Y) and battery storage ITC (§48E) retain credit access through 2032+. The model’s IRA on/off sweep captures this directionally (IRA-off ≈ post-OBBBA for wind/solar), but does not model the differential phase-out timeline, technology-specific credit retention, or the impact of effective tariff rates of 175%+ on Chinese solar panels. Rhodium Group projects OBBBA will shrink new clean capacity additions by 53–59% through 2035.
  • Interconnection queue constraints — the model assumes new capacity can be built as needed, without modeling interconnection queue delays or developer competition effects. LBNL’s “Queued Up” 2025 Edition reports that only 13% of projects submitted from 2000–2019 reached commercial operations, with median time from request to operation of 5 years. Modeled “optimal” portfolios should be interpreted as theoretical cost-minimizing solutions, not guaranteed buildable portfolios.
  • Enumeration granularity — the adaptive grid search evaluates mixes at 1–5% resource fraction steps. The true continuous optimum may lie between grid points, though the zone search refinement (1% steps) bounds this gap. For practical procurement decisions, the difference between the enumerated and continuous optima is small relative to the cost uncertainty captured by the 5,832-scenario sensitivity analysis.
  • Emission factor granularity — EPA eGRID provides annual-average emission rates per ISO and fuel type. These are used for fossil fleet characterization and baseline calibration only—not for hourly dispatch decisions. Hourly CO₂ is computed from technology-specific combustion rates (coal 0.95, gas CCGT 0.37, gas CT 0.55, oil 0.65 tCO₂/MWh) applied to the merit-order dispatch stack. The three-layer LMP engine (merit-order dispatch → demand-quantile pricing → scarcity adder) produces time-varying marginal costs independent of eGRID averages. Procurement accounting offers three static baselines (grid-average, fossil-average, marginal)—these are ISO-level annual scalars, not time-varying marginal rates as recommended by Siler-Evans et al. (2012) and Donti et al. (2019).
  • Clean firm learning curve uncertainty — Wright’s Law cost projections for nuclear and CCS assume positive learning rates (4–10%/doubling for nuclear, 3–5% for CCS). Historical Western nuclear programs have exhibited negative learning (Grubler 2010—French program costs escalated 3.5× from 1970s to 1990s units), and recent projects (Vogtle 3&4 at $30–36.8B vs $14B estimate; Flamanville 3 at ∼€13.2B vs €3.3B; Hinkley Point C at £35B vs £18B) overran estimates by 2–4×. The L/M/H cost toggles bracket a wide range but do not model cost escalation scenarios. Results showing nuclear-heavy portfolios should be interpreted as conditional on achieving learning curves that lack sustained Western precedent.
Interpreting results: This model identifies the theoretical cost-optimal resource mix under specified assumptions. Real-world procurement involves additional factors—developer availability, interconnection timelines, contract structure, and counterparty risk—that may shift actual portfolios from the modeled optimum.

13. Future Enhancements

The following enhancements are informed by two open-source capacity expansion models: EPRI's US-REGEN and MIT/Princeton's GenX.jl. They represent opportunities to increase dispatch realism, transmission fidelity, and demand-side sophistication while preserving the model's core strength of full 8,760-hour cost-mix co-optimization.

Tier 1 — High Impact, Feasible

Zonal Transmission Modeling

Upgrade from flat $/MWh adders to a pipe-and-bubble inter-zone flow model with congestion pricing. Define 2–5 transmission zones per ISO based on actual constraint boundaries (e.g., PJM West/East, MISO North/South, CAISO SP15/NP15). Captures locational cost signals that shift optimal resource siting and reveals intra-ISO price separation masked by copper-plate assumptions. Informed by EPRI US-REGEN's zonal architecture.

Full Unit Commitment & Operating Reserves

Extend the existing must-run / minimum-generation constraints (nuclear 100%, coal 40%) with spinning reserve requirements, ramp rates, minimum up/down times, and start-up costs. The current partial implementation captures off-peak price dynamics; full commitment would add cycling cost realism for fossil backup and CCS-CCGT at high CFE thresholds. Informed by GenX.jl's unit commitment formulation.

Demand-Side Flexibility

Model flexible loads—EV charging, data center workload shifting, industrial process scheduling—that can absorb surplus clean energy during high-generation hours, reducing curtailment and storage requirements. Particularly impactful for solar-heavy ISOs where midday surplus depresses value. Informed by REGEN's buildings and transport demand response modules.

Tier 2 — Medium Impact, Moderate Effort

Cross-ISO EAC Trade

Model inter-regional energy attribute certificate markets with deliverability constraints and transaction costs. Enables cheaper cross-regional procurement paths and captures how scarcity in one ISO drives price signals in others—a dynamic increasingly relevant as corporate CFE demand scales.

Endogenous Gas Retirement

Coal retirement now uses a hybrid model (EIA 860 announced retirements through 2035, threshold-based after). The remaining enhancement is endogenous gas retirement—allowing the optimization to retire gas capacity when clean energy deployment makes plants uneconomic. This would capture the feedback between deployment, declining fossil utilization, and economic retirement. Informed by GenX's retirement module.

Flexible CCS Dispatch

Variable CO₂ capture rate that responds to electricity prices and carbon costs, rather than a fixed 90% capture assumption. Allows CCS-CCGT to trade off capture cost against generation flexibility—running at lower capture rates during peak demand and higher rates during surplus hours. Informed by GenX's flexible CCS resource model.

Tier 3 — Aspirational, High Effort

DC Optimal Power Flow

Full angle-based power flow with Kirchhoff's law enforcement and transmission loss modeling. Provides maximum fidelity for congestion and curtailment analysis but requires nodal network topology data and significantly increases computational complexity. Informed by GenX.jl's DCOPF transmission module.

Multi-Stage Pathway Optimization

Intertemporal investment sequencing using dynamic programming to optimize the timing and ordering of capacity additions over a 25-year horizon. Complements the existing parametric market simulation with endogenous buildout decisions that account for path dependency and option value.

Multi-Sector Energy Integration

Extend the model to capture electricity–transport–buildings–industry interactions, enabling holistic decarbonization pathway analysis where electrification decisions in one sector affect resource requirements and prices in others. Informed by REGEN's full energy-economy integration architecture.

All implementations would be original code built on publicly documented methodologies.
